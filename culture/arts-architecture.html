معماری ایرانیIranian architecture

me'māri-ye irāni

Iwans and domes that lift the spirit, windcatchers, qanats and ice houses that beat the desert heat, and bridges built for strolling: a short guide to reading an Iranian building.

Facts

  • Taq Kasra, the Sasanian palace arch at Ctesiphon near Baghdad, is still one of the largest single-span brick vaults in the world.
  • The Jameh Mosque of Isfahan, with building work from more than a thousand years, was added to UNESCO's World Heritage list in 2012.
  • The dome of Soltaniyeh (1302–12), built as the tomb of the Mongol ruler Oljeitu, is one of the largest brick domes in the world and a UNESCO site since 2005.
  • In 2016 UNESCO added 'The Persian Qanat', a group of eleven qanats, to its World Heritage list.
  • The word کاشی kāshi tile comes from Kashan, a great centre of tile-making in the Middle Ages.
  • Si-o-se Pol is named after its 33 arches; it is also called the Allahverdi Khan Bridge, after the general of Shah Abbas who built it.

Brick, light and shade

Iranian architecture has two great talents: making buildings that lift the spirit, and making buildings that keep people alive in a hot, dry land. Its materials are humble – sun-dried خشت khesht mud brick, fired آجر ājor brick, plaster and glazed کاشی kāshi tile – yet with them builders created some of the most beautiful spaces in the world. Learn a handful of words and you can read almost any Iranian building.

The iwan and the four-iwan mosque

The signature form is the ایوان eyvān iwan: a tall vaulted hall, closed on three sides and open on the fourth, like a giant porch. Iwans go back to the Parthians, and the Sasanian palace arch at Ctesiphon, Taq Kasra, is still one of the largest brick vaults ever built.

In the 12th century, under the Seljuks, builders placed an iwan in the middle of each side of a courtyard. This چهارایوانی chahār-eyvāni four-iwan plan, made classic by the Jameh Mosque of Isfahan, became the standard layout for Iranian mosques, schools and caravanserais. The Shah Mosque on Isfahan's Naqsh-e Jahan Square follows it too: four iwans around a courtyard pool, and behind the iwan that faces Mecca, a great dome.

Domes and muqarnas

How do you put a round dome on a square room? The Sasanians solved it with the squinch, a small arch across each corner that turns the square into an octagon. Their fire temples – four arches under a dome, the چهارطاق chahār-tāgh four arches – are the ancestors of every Iranian گنبد gombad dome. Later builders made domes taller and lighter with two shells, an inner ceiling and an outer skin.

Corners and portals are filled with مقرنس mogharnas muqarnas: tiers of tiny niches that look like a honeycomb or hanging icicles. Covered in mirror, plaster or tile, they catch the light and make heavy brick seem to dissolve.

Wind and water

In the desert cities the skyline is made of towers. A بادگیر bādgir windcatcher rises above the roof with vents that catch the slightest breeze and send it down into the house, while hot air escapes upwards. Yazd has so many that it is called the city of windcatchers.

Water came from underground. A قنات ghanāt qanat is a gently sloping tunnel that taps groundwater at the foot of the mountains and carries it for kilometres to villages and fields, with a line of shafts above it for air and digging – from the air they look like rows of craters. Iranians have dug qanats for about three thousand years, and many still flow.

Ice in summer

How did a desert town have ice in July? It built a یخچال yakhchāl ice house. In winter, water was let into long shallow pools shaded by a high wall, where it froze overnight. The ice was cut and stored in a deep pit under a tall, cone-shaped mud dome. Thick walls of ساروج sāruj sarooj, a waterproof mortar made with lime, clay, sand and ash, kept it cold until summer. Today yakhchāl is simply the Persian word for a fridge.

The bridges of Isfahan

Isfahan's river, the Zayandeh Rud, is crossed by bridges that are also places to stroll and meet. سی‌وسه‌پل Si-o-se-pol the Bridge of Thirty-three Arches, built around 1600 for Shah Abbas, carries its walkway between two storeys of arches. The Khaju Bridge, about fifty years younger, is a bridge and a dam in one: sluices under its arches control the water, and a pavilion in the middle was kept for the shah. On summer evenings people still gather under the arches to sing for the echo – even in the years when the river runs dry.

Say it in Persian

Show

ایوان سالنی است که یک طرفش باز است.

eyvān sālon-i ast ke yek taraf-ash bāz ast.

An iwan is a hall that is open on one side.

مسجد جامع اصفهان چهار ایوان دارد.

masjed-e jāme'-e Esfahān chahār eyvān dārad.

The Jameh Mosque of Isfahan has four iwans.

گنبدهای ایرانی را با کاشی آبی می‌پوشاندند.

gombad-hā-ye irāni rā bā kāshi-ye ābi mi-pushāndand.

Iranian domes were covered with blue tiles.

Literally: they used to cover Iranian domes with blue tile

مقرنس‌ها شبیه کندوی عسل هستند.

mogharnas-hā shabih-e kandu-ye 'asal hastand.

Muqarnas look like a honeycomb.

بادگیر هوای خنک را به داخل خانه می‌فرستد.

bādgir havā-ye khonak rā be dākhel-e khāne mi-ferestad.

A windcatcher sends cool air into the house.

یزد را شهر بادگیرها می‌نامند.

Yazd rā shahr-e bādgir-hā mi-nāmand.

Yazd is called the city of windcatchers.

قنات آب را از زیر زمین به روستا می‌آورد.

ghanāt āb rā az zir-e zamin be rustā mi-āvarad.

A qanat brings water from under the ground to the village.

ایرانی‌ها حدود سه هزار سال است که قنات می‌سازند.

irāni-hā hodud-e se hezār sāl ast ke ghanāt mi-sāzand.

Iranians have been building qanats for about three thousand years.

در یخچال‌های قدیمی، یخ را تا تابستان نگه می‌داشتند.

dar yakhchāl-hā-ye ghadimi, yakh rā tā tābestān negah mi-dāshtand.

In the old ice houses, ice was kept until summer.

امروز به یخچال برقی هم یخچال می‌گویند.

emruz be yakhchāl-e barghi ham yakhchāl mi-guyand.

Today an electric fridge is also called a yakhchal.

سی‌وسه‌پل سی و سه دهانه دارد.

Si-o-se-pol si o se dahāne dārad.

Si-o-se Pol has thirty-three arches.

مردم شب‌ها زیر پل خواجو آواز می‌خوانند.

mardom shab-hā zir-e pol-e Khāju āvāz mi-khānand.

In the evenings people sing under the Khaju Bridge.
