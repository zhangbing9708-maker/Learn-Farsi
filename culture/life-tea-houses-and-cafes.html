Culture / Everyday life

قهوه‌خانه و کافهTea houses and cafés

ghahve-khāne va kāfe

Carpeted day-beds and samovars in the old tea house, the bubbling qalyan, the storyteller who stopped at the most exciting moment – and the espresso-and-laptop cafés of young Iranians today.

Facts

  • Despite the name, a traditional ghahve-khāne ('coffee house') serves tea; coffee was the drink of the Safavid coffee houses of the 17th century.
  • Naqqali was inscribed on UNESCO's list of intangible heritage in need of urgent safeguarding in 2011.
  • Coffee-house painting is a folk style of large canvases showing Shahnameh heroes and religious scenes, made to hang in tea houses and to illustrate the storyteller's tales.
  • Café Naderi in central Tehran was a favourite of writers such as Sadegh Hedayat, Jalal Al-e Ahmad and Forugh Farrokhzad.
  • Persian tradition credits the invention of backgammon, takhte-nard, to Bozorgmehr, the wise vizier of the Sasanian king Khosrow I.
  • Tehranis often say chāyi rather than chāy for tea.
  • Qalyān is the Persian word for the water pipe; its tobacco is tanbāku.

The qahve-khaneh

Despite its name, the traditional قهوه‌خانه ghahve-khāne coffee house serves tea. Coffee was the fashion in the coffee houses of Safavid Isfahan, but tea took over in the nineteenth century and the old name stayed. A classic tea house has tiled walls, old photographs, a row of gleaming samovars and wooden day-beds, each a تخت takht day-bed, spread with carpets and bolster cushions, the پشتی poshti bolster. Customers sit cross-legged, order دیزی dizi lamb and chickpea stew at lunchtime and play تخته‌نرد takhte-nard backgammon in the afternoon. The old tea house was a men's world of drivers, porters and bazaar workers; the restored ones in old mansions and in mountain spots such as Darband, above Tehran, welcome families and tourists too.

Tea rituals

Tea comes fast and often. The keeper of the tea house, the قهوه‌چی ghahve-chi tea-house keeper, brings it تازه‌دم tāze-dam freshly brewed in small glasses on saucers, with a bowl of قند ghand sugar lumps or sticks of saffron rock candy. Many older men pour a little of the hot tea into the saucer, the نعلبکی na'lbeki saucer, to cool it and sip it from there, with a sugar lump held between the teeth. Ordering is easy: hold up two fingers and say دو تا چای do tā chāy two teas – or, as Tehranis say, دو تا چایی do tā chāyi two teas – and a refill is never far away.

The qalyan

Many tea houses also serve the قلیان ghalyān water pipe: a glass base full of water, a long hose and a clay bowl of moist تنباکو tanbāku tobacco, topped with glowing زغال zoghāl charcoal. The smoke bubbles through the water and the pipe is passed from hand to hand. Today fruit-flavoured tobacco is popular with the young, though doctors warn that the qalyan is no healthier than cigarettes.

The storyteller

The tea house was also a theatre. In the evening a نقال nagghāl storyteller would stand before the audience and perform the tales of the Shahnameh – Rostam's هفت‌خان haft-khān seven labours, the tragedy of Rostam and Sohrab – with his voice, his whole body and a stick, often pointing to a huge painted canvas behind him. These نقاشی قهوه‌خانه‌ای nagghāshi-ye ghahve-khāne'i coffee-house paintings, crowded with heroes, battles and religious scenes, became a folk art of their own. A good storyteller stopped at the most exciting moment, so that everyone would come back the next night. In 2011 UNESCO listed نقالی nagghāli naqqali as heritage in need of urgent safeguarding.

The café generation

In recent decades a new kind of place has spread through Iranian cities: the کافه kāfe café. Here the drink is قهوه ghahve coffee – espresso, cappuccino or Turkish coffee – with cakes and sandwiches, and the customers are mostly young: students with laptops, friends talking for hours, groups playing board games. Cafés have become favourite meeting places, and many double as bookshops or galleries. Yet café culture has older roots. The کافه نادری Kāfe Nāderi Café Naderi in central Tehran, founded by an Armenian immigrant, was once the haunt of writers such as Sadegh Hedayat and Forugh Farrokhzad.

pedar-bozorg-am chāy rā dar na'lbeki mi-rizad va mi-nushad.

My grandfather pours his tea into the saucer and drinks it.

Spoken بابابزرگم چایی رو تو نعلبکی می‌ریزه و می‌خوره. bābā-bozorg-am chāyi ro tu na'lbeki mi-rize o mi-khore.

In the tea house we sit on a day-bed and eat dizi.

Spoken تو قهوه‌خونه رو تخت می‌شینیم و دیزی می‌خوریم. tu ghahve-khune ru takht mi-shinim o dizi mi-khorim.

نقال داستان رستم و سهراب را تعریف می‌کرد.

nagghāl dāstān-e Rostam va Sohrāb rā ta'rif mi-kard.

The storyteller was telling the story of Rostam and Sohrab.

او همیشه در هیجان‌انگیزترین جای داستان دست نگه می‌داشت.

u hamishe dar hayajān-angiz-tarin jā-ye dāstān dast negah mi-dāsht.

He always stopped at the most exciting point in the story.

این کافه قهوه‌ی خیلی خوبی دارد.

in kāfe ghahve-ye kheyli khub-i dārad.

This café has really good coffee.

Spoken این کافه قهوه‌ی خیلی خوبی داره. in kāfe ghahve-ye kheyli khub-i dāre.

یک کاپوچینو و یک تکه کیک شکلاتی، لطفاً.

A cappuccino and a piece of chocolate cake, please.

Spoken یه کاپوچینو و یه تیکه کیک شکلاتی، لطفاً. ye kāpuchino o ye tike keyk-e shokolāti, lotfan.
