Culture / Everyday life

پول و خریدMoney and shopping

pul va kharid

Why Iranians count in tomans when the money says rials, how prices are said, cards and card readers, corner shops and hypermarkets, when to bargain – and what to do with the receipt.

Facts

  • Iran's official currency is the rial, but people almost always count in tomans: one toman is ten rials.
  • Banknotes are printed in rials, so a note marked 100,000 rials is worth 10,000 tomans.
  • Toman comes from tümen, a Mongol word for 'ten thousand'.
  • Price tags and receipts use Persian digits (۰۱۲۳۴۵۶۷۸۹), so they are worth learning early.
  • In Iran a supermārket is usually a small neighbourhood grocery; big stores are called forushgāh.
  • Most Iranians pay even small amounts by bank card at a kārt-khān, a 'card reader'; foreign bank cards do not work in Iran.
  • Fāktor, an itemised bill, comes from French facture.

Rials and tomans

Iran's official currency is the ریال riyāl rial, but almost nobody counts in rials. In everyday life prices are given in تومان tumān tomans: one toman is ten rials, so you knock a zero off the rial figure. Banknotes are printed in rials, which means that a note marked 100,000 is worth 10,000 tomans. When you see a price tag, ask تومان یا ریال؟ tumān yā riyāl? tomans or rials? – the difference is a factor of ten. The word toman itself goes back to a Mongol word for 'ten thousand'.

Saying prices

Prices change quickly, and people save breath by leaving out zeros. A shopkeeper who says پنجاه panjāh fifty for a kilo of fruit almost certainly means fifty thousand tomans, and bigger sums are shortened in the same way; the context, or a quick question, will tell you which. The numbers themselves are simple: هزار hezār thousand, میلیون milyun million, صد و پنجاه هزار تومان sad o panjāh hezār tumān 150,000 tomans. Price tags, receipts and banknotes use Persian digits – ۰ ۱ ۲ ۳ ۴ ۵ ۶ ۷ ۸ ۹ – so they are worth learning early.

Cards and cash

Iranians pay for almost everything by bank card, from a taxi ride to a loaf of bread. Every shop has a کارت‌خوان kārt-khān card reader: you hand over or tap your card, type your رمز ramz PIN, and a moment later a text message from your bank confirms the payment. Phone apps and QR codes are spreading too, while پول نقد pul-e naghd cash is still handy for tips and small stalls. Foreign bank cards do not work in Iran, so visitors bring cash and change it at an exchange office, a صرافی sarrāfi exchange office, or buy a prepaid card for tourists.

Shops and supermarkets

Daily shopping is local. The neighbourhood سوپرمارکت supermārket corner shop – in Iran a 'supermarket' is usually a small grocery – sells milk, bread, eggs and cheese; fruit and vegetables come from the میوه‌فروشی mive-forushi greengrocer, meat from the قصابی ghassābi butcher's, and medicine from the داروخانه dārukhāne pharmacy. Big chain stores and hypermarkets, each called a فروشگاه forushgāh store, are popular in the cities. Many shops deliver if you phone them, and fruit and vegetables are sold by the کیلو kilu kilo: نیم کیلو nim kilu half a kilo, یک کیلو yek kilu one kilo.

To bargain or not

Bargaining belongs to the bazaar: to carpets and handicrafts, to second-hand goods and to taxis without a meter. In supermarkets, pharmacies and shops with price tags, prices are fixed, and a sign may say so: قیمت مقطوع gheymat-e maghtu' fixed price. Even then, تعارف ta'ārof ritual politeness has its place. A shopkeeper may wave away your money with قابل ندارد ghābel na-dārad it's nothing; smile, insist, and ask چقدر تقدیم کنم؟ cheghadr taghdim konam? how much do I owe you? – literally 'how much shall I offer?'.

The receipt

After you pay by card you will hear رسید می‌خواهید؟ resid mi-khāhid? do you want the receipt? – the little slip from the card reader. For bigger purchases ask for a فاکتور fāktor itemised bill, from French facture, stamped by the shop. Keep it: many small shops will exchange goods but not refund them, and some display the old warning جنس فروخته‌شده پس گرفته نمی‌شود jens-e forukhte-shode pas gerefte nemi-shavad goods sold cannot be returned. If in doubt, ask before you buy whether you can عوض کردن avaz kardan exchange the item.

resid mi-khāhid?

Do you want the receipt?

یک کیلو سیب و نیم کیلو انگور می‌خواهم.

yek kilu sib va nim kilu angur mi-khāham.

I'd like a kilo of apples and half a kilo of grapes.

Spoken یه کیلو سیب و نیم کیلو انگور می‌خوام. ye kilu sib o nim kilu angur mi-khām.

قیمت‌ها این روزها خیلی زود عوض می‌شوند.

gheymat-hā in ruz-hā kheyli zud avaz mi-shavand.

Prices change very quickly these days.

Spoken قیمتا این روزا خیلی زود عوض می‌شن. gheymat-ā in ruz-ā kheyli zud avaz mi-shan.

ببخشید، این را می‌شود عوض کرد؟

be-bakhshid, in rā mi-shavad avaz kard?

Excuse me, can I exchange this?

Spoken ببخشید، اینو می‌شه عوض کرد؟ be-bakhshid, in-o mi-she avaz kard?

فاکتور را هم بدهید، لطفاً.

fāktor rā ham be-dahid, lotfan.

Could I have the itemised bill too, please?

Spoken فاکتور رو هم بدین، لطفاً. fāktor ro ham be-din, lotfan.

چقدر تقدیم کنم؟

cheghadr taghdim konam?

How much do I owe you?

Literally: how much shall I offer?

A very polite way to ask the price, especially after the shopkeeper's ta'arof.

این‌جا قیمت‌ها مقطوع است.

in-jā gheymat-hā maghtu' ast.

Prices here are fixed.
