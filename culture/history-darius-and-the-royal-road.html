داریوش و راه شاهیDarius and the Royal Road

Dāriush o rāh-e shāhi

Cyrus founded the Persian Empire; Darius I made it work. He divided it into satrapies, carved his story on the cliff of Behistun, linked Sardis to Susa with the Royal Road and its relay riders, and built a palace from the materials of a whole world.

Facts

  • Darius I reigned for 36 years, from 522 to 486 BC.
  • The Behistun inscription is written in Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian; it was copied by Henry Rawlinson in the 1830s–40s and became a key to deciphering cuneiform.
  • Behistun was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 2006.
  • Herodotus counted 111 post stations on the Royal Road between Sardis and Susa.
  • Herodotus' praise of the Persian royal couriers is carved on the James Farley Post Office in New York.
  • The gold daric, introduced under Darius, was one of the first gold coins of the ancient world.
  • Glazed brick friezes of archers from Darius's palace at Susa are displayed in the Louvre.

A king who had to fight for the throne

In 522 BC Cambyses, the son of Cyrus, died on his way home from Egypt. A man calling himself Bardiya, Cambyses' brother, already held the throne. A young noble named داریوش Dāriush Darius, from a side branch of the هخامنشیان Hakhāmaneshiyān Achaemenids, killed him with six fellow Persians and declared that he had been an impostor, a priest called Gaumata. Historians still argue about whether that was true.

The empire exploded into شورش shuresh revolt: Babylon, Media, Elam and even Persia itself rose against the new king. Darius says that in a single year he fought nineteen battles and captured nine rebel kings. By the end of it he was master of an empire stretching from the Aegean to the Indus, and he ruled it until his death in 486 BC.

Behistun: the story on the cliff

Darius told his version of events in a giant سنگ‌نوشته sang-neveshte rock inscription at بیستون Bisotun Behistun, near Kermanshah, on the old road from Babylon to Ecbatana. High above the road, the relief shows Darius with his foot on the fallen Gaumata, nine rebel kings roped together by the neck, and above them the winged symbol of اهورامزدا Ahurāmazdā Ahura Mazda.

The text is written in خط میخی khatt-e mikhi cuneiform in three languages: Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian. That made it the Rosetta Stone of the Near East. In the 1830s and 1840s the British officer Henry Rawlinson climbed the cliff to copy it, and the Old Persian version became a key to reading cuneiform. Behistun has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2006.

Satrapies: ruling a world

To hold so many peoples together, Darius organised the empire into provinces called satrapies – the Greek historian Herodotus lists twenty. Each was governed by a ساتراپ sātrāp satrap, from Old Persian xshaçapāvan, 'protector of the kingdom'. Each province paid a fixed خراج kharāj tribute in silver or goods, and the satrap was watched by military commanders and royal secretaries who reported to the king. Greek writers called the king's inspectors 'the eyes and ears of the king'.

Darius also introduced the gold دریک darik daric coin, standard weights and measures, and used Aramaic as the everyday language of government from Egypt to Bactria.

The Royal Road and its riders

The arteries of the empire were its roads. The most famous, the راه شاهی rāh-e shāhi Royal Road, ran about 2,500 kilometres from Sardis in western Anatolia to شوش Shush Susa. Herodotus counted 111 stations along it, each with fresh horses. An ordinary traveller needed about three months to walk the whole road; a message carried by relays of royal riders could arrive in about a week.

Herodotus admired these riders: nothing, he wrote, neither snow nor rain nor heat nor night, stopped them from finishing their stage. His words are carved today on the main post office in New York. In Persian such a mounted messenger is a چاپار chāpār courier, and a post station is a چاپارخانه chāpār-khāne post house. Clay tablets from Persepolis show the system at work: travellers with a sealed document from the king received rations of flour, wine and beer at each stop.

A palace from the whole empire

Darius made Susa his main capital and built there a great کاخ kākh palace and an audience hall with tall stone columns. His foundation text lists where everything came from: cedar from Lebanon, gold from Sardis and Bactria, lapis lazuli and carnelian from Sogdiana, turquoise from Chorasmia, ivory from Ethiopia, India and Arachosia. Ionians and Sardians cut the stone, Medes and Egyptians worked the gold, and Babylonians made the bricks.

The walls were decorated with glazed brick friezes of archers and winged bulls, now in the Louvre in Paris. The message was clear: every people of the empire had a share in the king's house.

Darius's legacy

Darius also began the terraces of Persepolis around 518 BC, dug a canal linking the Nile to the Red Sea, and sent the expedition that the Greeks defeated at Marathon in 490 BC. He was buried in a rock tomb at نقش رستم Naghsh-e Rostam Naqsh-e Rostam, and his son Xerxes succeeded him.

Today داریوش Dāriush Dariush is still a common Iranian boy's name.

داریوش بزرگ از سال ۵۲۲ تا ۴۸۶ پیش از میلاد پادشاه بود.

Dāriush-e bozorg az sāl-e pānsad-o-bist-o-do tā chahārsad-o-hashtād-o-shesh pish az milād pādshāh bud.

Darius the Great was king from 522 to 486 BC.

داریوش می‌گوید که در یک سال نوزده نبرد را برده است.

Dāriush mi-guyad ke dar yek sāl nuzdah nabard rā borde ast.

Darius says that he won nineteen battles in one year.

او کشور بزرگش را به چند استان تقسیم کرد.

u keshvar-e bozorg-ash rā be chand ostān taghsim kard.

He divided his large country into several provinces.

هر استان یک ساتراپ داشت که از طرف شاه حکومت می‌کرد.

har ostān yek sātrāp dāsht ke az taraf-e shāh hokumat mi-kard.

Each province had a satrap who ruled on behalf of the king.

سنگ‌نوشته‌ی بیستون به سه زبان نوشته شده است.

sang-neveshte-ye Bisotun be se zabān neveshte shode ast.

The Behistun inscription is written in three languages.

این کتیبه نزدیک کرمانشاه، بالای یک کوه بلند است.

in katibe nazdik-e Kermānshāh, bālā-ye yek kuh-e boland ast.

This inscription is near Kermanshah, high up on a tall mountain.

راه شاهی از سارد تا شوش ادامه داشت.

rāh-e shāhi az Sārd tā Shush edāme dāsht.

The Royal Road ran from Sardis to Susa.

چاپارها در هر ایستگاه اسب تازه می‌گرفتند.

chāpār-hā dar har istgāh asb-e tāze mi-gereftand.

The couriers took a fresh horse at every station.

یک مسافر معمولی این راه را در سه ماه می‌رفت.

yek mosāfer-e ma'muli in rāh rā dar se māh mi-raft.

An ordinary traveller covered this road in three months.

داریوش در شوش کاخ بزرگی ساخت.

Dāriush dar Shush kākh-e bozorg-i sākht.

Darius built a great palace in Susa.

صنعتگران از همه‌ی سرزمین‌های شاهنشاهی برای ساختن کاخ آمدند.

san'atgarān az hame-ye sarzamin-hā-ye shāhanshāhi barā-ye sākhtan-e kākh āmadand.

Craftsmen came from all the lands of the empire to build the palace.

آرامگاه داریوش در نقش رستم است.

ārāmgāh-e Dāriush dar Naghsh-e Rostam ast.

The tomb of Darius is at Naqsh-e Rostam.
