Culture / Food

بیرون غذا خوردنEating out in Iran

birun ghazā khordan

Kebab houses and carpeted teahouses, busy cafés and street carts selling beetroot and broad beans – plus the art of ta'arof at the table and the polite battle over who pays.

Facts

  • In many Iranian restaurants you pay at the cash desk (sandugh), not at the table.
  • Iranians usually speak of prices in tomans; one toman is ten rials.
  • Ghābel nadārad, 'it is not worthy (of you)', is ta'arof: the customer is expected to insist on paying.
  • Abadan and Ahvaz in Khuzestan are famous for spicy falafel.
  • Boiled beetroot (labu) and boiled broad beans (baghali) are classic winter street foods.
  • Traditional sofrekhanehs seat diners on carpeted wooden day-beds called takht.

Where to eat

For Iranians home cooking is the gold standard, and many of the best stews and polos are hard to find on menus. But there is plenty to eat out:

  • the کبابی kabābi kebab house or چلوکبابی chelow-kabābi chelow kabab restaurant: kebab, rice and doogh, served fast;
  • the سفره‌خانه sofre-khāne traditional restaurant: carpets, cushions and wooden day-beds (تخت takht day-bed) where you sit cross-legged to eat dizi, drink tea and smoke a water pipe (قلیان ghalyān water pipe);
  • the رستوران restorān restaurant, from simple to grand, with Iranian classics and, in the big cities, pizza, burgers and foreign cuisines;
  • the کافه kāfe café, hugely popular with young people in Tehran and other cities.

On summer evenings Tehranis head up to Darband and Darakeh at the foot of the mountains, where restaurants set their day-beds beside the stream.

Street food

Street food follows the seasons. In winter, carts sell steaming لبو labu boiled beetroot and broad beans (باقالی bāghāli broad beans) boiled with golpar and vinegar; in summer, corn on the cob (بلال balāl corn on the cob) is grilled over charcoal and dipped in salt water. جگرکی jegaraki liver-kebab stalls grill lamb's liver, heart and kidneys late into the night. Khuzestan is famous for spicy فلافل falāfel falafel, and every city has ساندویچی sāndevichi sandwich shops selling sausage and الویه olviye chicken and potato salad sandwiches. And before dawn the کله‌پزی kalle-pazi sheep's-head restaurant serves کله‌پاچه kalle-pāche sheep's head and trotters – a winter breakfast for the brave.

Ordering and paying

In simple places you often order and pay at the counter (صندوق sandugh cash desk) first; in others you ask for the bill (صورت‌حساب surat-hesāb bill) at the end and pay the cashier on the way out. Menus often give prices in تومان tumān toman – one toman is ten rials – and people almost always talk in tomans. Some restaurants add a service charge; otherwise a small tip (انعام an'ām tip) is welcome but not expected.

Ta'arof at the table

تعارف ta'ārof ritual politeness shapes every meal with guests. The host says بفرمایید befarmāyid please, go ahead and keeps filling your plate; a guest is expected to protest a little, and a clean plate is quickly refilled – so if you are truly full, leave a little and say سیر شدم sir shodam I'm full. After the meal, thank the cook with دستتان درد نکند dast-etān dard na-konad thank you; lit. 'may your hand not hurt'; the answer is نوش جان nush-e jān enjoy it; lit. 'sweet to your soul'.

Who pays?

When friends eat out, the bill starts a polite battle. Everyone reaches for a wallet, insisting مهمان من باشید mehmān-e man bāshid be my guest, and an experienced payer slips away to the cash desk before dessert. Splitting the bill – دنگی dongi going Dutch – is now common among students and young colleagues, but with older relatives or a visitor from abroad, someone will insist on paying. And if a taxi driver or shopkeeper says قابل ندارد ghābel na-dārad it's not worthy of you when you ask the price, that too is ta'arof: thank them and insist on paying.
