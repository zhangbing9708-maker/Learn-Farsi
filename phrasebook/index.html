کتاب عباراتPhrasebook

ketāb-e ebārāt

Where the two differ, every phrase is given twice: first in standard written Persian – correct, polite and understood everywhere – and then, marked 🗣, in the everyday spoken Persian of Tehran, which is what you will actually hear in shops, taxis and homes. Learn to say the spoken form and to recognise both. Persian has two words for 'you': shomā, for anyone you don't know well, anyone older than you and any official (it is also the plural), and to, for friends, family and children. Phrases marked formal use shomā – verbs end in -id, spoken -in – or the polite register; the others use to – verbs end in -i – and are for people you are close to. When in doubt, use shomā: nobody minds too much respect, and when an Iranian friend starts calling you to, you can answer in kind. Tap a phrase to hear it.

Greetings and goodbyes سلام و خداحافظی

Salām works at any hour and with anyone, and the person who arrives says it first – a younger person greets an older one. Questions about your health and family follow straight away; they are part of the greeting, so answer briefly and ask back. Goodbyes take time: expect several rounds of good wishes at the door.

سلام!

Hello!

Works at any time of day and with anyone – shopkeepers, drivers, neighbours, officials.

formal

سلام علیکم.

Peace be upon you (a respectful hello).

More traditional, especially with older people. The reply is aleykom assalām.

From late afternoon until dark.

Said when you leave in the evening or go to bed – not as a greeting.

Introductions معرفی و آشنایی

Iranians ask personal questions early – your job, your age, whether you are married, what you think of Iran. It is friendliness, not nosiness; answer as much as you like. Use shomā with everyone you meet for the first time.

Courtesy and ta'ārof ادب و تعارف

Ta'ārof is the ritual politeness that runs through Iranian life: offering what you don't expect to be accepted, refusing what you would like, insisting that the other person go first. The rule of thumb is to refuse an offer once or twice and accept the third time – and when a shopkeeper or driver says your money is 'not worthy', thank them and pay anyway.

formal

ممنون.

mamnun.

Thanks.

The everyday thank-you, fine with anyone.

مرسی.

Thanks.

Borrowed from French; heard everywhere in the cities, a touch casual.

Thank you (for all your trouble).

Spoken دستتون درد نکنه. dast-etun dard na-kone.

Thanks for something someone has made or done for you – a meal, a favour, a gift.

Said when giving a present – and by shopkeepers and drivers when you ask the price. It is courtesy: insist, and pay.

Understanding each other حرف زدن و فهمیدن

Iranians are delighted when a foreigner tries Persian, and most will happily slow down, repeat and teach you a new word. Don't be shy about asking – and expect plenty of people to try out their English on you in return.

Numbers and money عدد و پول

Prices are written in rials but almost always spoken in tomans: one toman is ten rials, so a tag that says 500,000 rials means 50,000 tomans. In speech the 'thousand' is usually dropped as well, so if a price sounds impossibly low, ask 'tomans or rials?'. Foreign bank cards do not work in Iran: visitors bring cash to change, or buy a prepaid local card.

Time and dates وقت و تاریخ

Iran keeps its own solar calendar: the year begins at Nowruz, on the spring equinox around 21 March, and the twelve months have Persian names – Farvardin, Ordibehesht, Khordād and so on. Ask for the milādi (Western) date when you need it. The week starts on Saturday, and Friday is the day off.

Asking the way راه و نشانی

Iranians give directions generously – sometimes even when they are not sure – so it is worth asking twice. Addresses run from big to small: the main street, then the side street (kuche), then the alley, then the number; squares (meydān) and well-known landmarks matter more than house numbers.

Taxis and the metro تاکسی و مترو

Most people in Tehran get around by ride-hailing app – Snapp or Tapsi – or by metro. Street taxis work in two ways: shared taxis run along fixed routes and pick up passengers on the way, while darbast means you hire the whole car. With a street taxi, agree the fare before you get in.

formal

دربست!

darbast!

Private hire! (I'll take the whole car.)

Call it to a passing taxi; otherwise the driver may treat you as a shared-taxi passenger.

At the hotel در هتل
