# Yazd

### A city of mud brick

یزد (*Yazd*, ‘Yazd’) lies in the middle of Iran, between two great deserts: the دشت کویر (*Dasht-e Kavir*, ‘Dasht-e Kavir’) to the north and the دشت لوت (*Dasht-e Lut*, ‘Dasht-e Lut’) to the south-east. Its old town, built almost entirely of خشت (*khesht*, ‘sun-dried mud brick’), became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2017.

Walk its narrow lanes, the کوچه (*kuche*, ‘alley’), and you pass under ساباط (*sābāt*, ‘covered passages’) that keep off the sun, past wooden doors with two knockers: a heavy one for men and a light ring for women, so that the people inside knew who was calling.

### Windcatchers, qanats and ice houses

![The Dowlat Abad garden and its windcatcher, Yazd](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6a/Jard%C3%ADn_de_Dolat-Abad,_Yazd,_Ir%C3%A1n,_2016-09-21,_DD_41.jpg/960px-Jard%C3%ADn_de_Dolat-Abad,_Yazd,_Ir%C3%A1n,_2016-09-21,_DD_41.jpg)
<sub>The Dowlat Abad garden and its windcatcher, Yazd. Photo: [Diego Delso](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Jard%C3%ADn_de_Dolat-Abad,_Yazd,_Ir%C3%A1n,_2016-09-21,_DD_41.jpg), CC BY-SA 4.0.</sub>

Yazd survives the desert with clever engineering. Above the roofs rise hundreds of بادگیر (*bādgir*, ‘windcatchers’), towers that catch the slightest breeze and send it down into the rooms below. The tallest, in the باغ دولت‌آباد (*Bāgh-e Dowlat-ābād*, ‘Dowlat Abad Garden’), is 33.8 metres high.

Water comes from قنات (*ghanāt*, ‘qanats’), underground channels that carry groundwater for kilometres from the foot of the mountains; the city's Water Museum shows how they were dug and how the water was shared. In desert towns nearby, such as Meybod, you can still see a یخچال (*yakhchāl*, ‘ice house’), a domed pit where winter ice was stored for the summer – which is why the Persian word for 'fridge' is *yakhchāl* today.

### Mosques and Amir Chakhmaq

![The Amir Chakhmaq complex, Yazd](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/da/Iran_-_Yazd_-_Amir_Chakmaq_Complex.jpg/960px-Iran_-_Yazd_-_Amir_Chakmaq_Complex.jpg)
<sub>The Amir Chakhmaq complex, Yazd. Photo: [Alireza Javaheri](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Iran_-_Yazd_-_Amir_Chakmaq_Complex.jpg), CC BY 3.0.</sub>

The مسجد جامع یزد (*Masjed-e Jāme'-e Yazd*, ‘Jameh Mosque of Yazd’), mostly 14th century, has a tiled entrance crowned by twin minarets 52 metres high, the tallest in Iran. A short walk away, the امیرچخماق (*Amir Chakhmāgh*, ‘Amir Chakhmaq’) complex shows a three-storey façade of arched niches that glows after dark. In front of it stands a نخل (*nakhl*, ‘nakhl’), a huge wooden frame that men carry through the streets in the mourning processions of محرم (*Moharram*, ‘Muharram’).

### The Zoroastrians of Yazd

![The Zoroastrian fire temple of Yazd](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5f/Zoroastrian_Fire_Temple_in_Yazd.JPG/960px-Zoroastrian_Fire_Temple_in_Yazd.JPG)
<sub>The Zoroastrian fire temple of Yazd. Photo: [Zenith210](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Zoroastrian_Fire_Temple_in_Yazd.JPG), CC BY-SA 3.0.</sub>

Yazd is the centre of Iran's زرتشتی (*zartoshti*, ‘Zoroastrian’) community. In the آتشکده (*ātashkade*, ‘fire temple’), built in 1934, a sacred fire burns behind glass; Zoroastrians believe it has been kept alight since about AD 470. On the hills at the edge of town stand two دخمه (*dakhme*, ‘towers of silence’), where the dead were once laid out for the vultures so that a body would not pollute the earth; they were last used in the 1970s.

In the desert mountains near Ardakan, the cave shrine of چک‌چک (*Chak-chak*, ‘Chak Chak’), named after the sound of dripping water, draws Zoroastrian pilgrims every June.

### Sweets, termeh and local words

![Termeh, the woven silk of Yazd](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/74/%D8%B7%D8%B1%D8%AD_%D8%AA%D8%A7%D8%AC.jpg/960px-%D8%B7%D8%B1%D8%AD_%D8%AA%D8%A7%D8%AC.jpg)
<sub>Termeh, the woven silk of Yazd. Photo: [Sahar.aghakasiri](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%D8%B7%D8%B1%D8%AD_%D8%AA%D8%A7%D8%AC.jpg), CC BY-SA 4.0.</sub>

Yazd is Iran's sweet shop. Try باقلوا (*bāghlavā*, ‘baklava’), little cardamom کیک یزدی (*keyk-e yazdi*, ‘Yazdi cupcakes’), almond-filled قطاب (*ghotāb*, ‘ghotab’) and پشمک (*pashmak*, ‘Persian candy floss’), spun from sugar and sesame. The city's traditional cloth is ترمه (*terme*, ‘termeh’), a fine woven fabric patterned with the بته‌جقه (*bote-jeghe*, ‘paisley’) motif.

Other Iranians recognise the Yazdi accent at once, and Yazdis are proud of it – and of their reputation for hard work and thrift. Listen also for the words of the old town itself: *bādgir*, *sābāt*, *ghanāt* and آب‌انبار (*āb-anbār*, ‘water cistern’).

### Getting there and when to go

Yazd has an airport and lies on the railway between Tehran and Kerman, and buses arrive from Isfahan, Shiraz and every other big city. Many visitors stay in a restored courtyard house in the old town. Come between October and April: summer afternoons often pass 40°C, while desert nights in winter are cold. Good day trips go to the villages of خرانق (*Kharānagh*, ‘Kharanaq’) and میبد (*Meybod*, ‘Meybod’), or out into the desert for a night under the stars.

### Facts

- The historic city of Yazd was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list in 2017.
- The windcatcher of the Dowlat Abad Garden, laid out in the mid-18th century, is 33.8 metres tall.
- The twin minarets of the Jameh Mosque of Yazd, 52 metres high, are the tallest in Iran.
- Zoroastrians say the fire in Yazd's fire temple has burned since about AD 470; the present temple building dates from 1934.
- Yazd's towers of silence were last used in the 1970s.
- Yakhchāl, literally 'ice pit', once meant an ice house; today it is the everyday word for a fridge.
- Zoroastrian pilgrims gather at the shrine of Chak Chak each year in mid-June.

### Say it in Persian

- این خانه را از خشت ساخته‌اند.  
  *in khāne rā az khesht sākhte-and.* — This house is built of mud brick.
- بادگیر هوای خنک را به خانه می‌آورد.  
  *bādgir havā-ye khonak rā be khāne mi-āvarad.* — The windcatcher brings cool air into the house.
- آب این قنات از کوه می‌آید.  
  *āb-e in ghanāt az kuh mi-āyad.* — The water of this qanat comes from the mountains.
- در قدیم، یخ را در یخچال نگه می‌داشتند.  
  *dar ghadim, yakh rā dar yakhchāl negah mi-dāshtand.* — In the old days they kept ice in an ice house.
- در خانه‌های قدیمی دو کوبه روی در است.  
  *dar khāne-hā-ye ghadimi do kube ru-ye dar ast.* — In old houses there are two knockers on the door.
- ببخشید، این کوچه به مسجد جامع می‌رسد؟  
  *bebakhshid, in kuche be Masjed-e Jāme' mi-resad?* — Excuse me, does this alley lead to the Jameh Mosque?  
  Spoken: ببخشید، این کوچه می‌رسه به مسجد جامع؟ *bebakhshid, in kuche mi-rese be Masjed-e Jāme'?*
- می‌گویند آتش این آتشکده از ۱۵۰۰ سال پیش روشن است.  
  *mi-guyand ātash-e in ātashkade az hezār-o-pānsad sāl pish rowshan ast.* — They say the fire in this temple has been burning for 1,500 years.
- زرتشتیان هر سال در خرداد به چک‌چک می‌روند.  
  *zartoshtiyān har sāl dar Khordād be Chak-chak mi-ravand.* — Every year in Khordad (June) Zoroastrians go to Chak Chak.
- یک کیک یزدی و یک چای، لطفاً.  
  *yek keyk-e yazdi va yek chāy, lotfan.* — A Yazdi cupcake and a tea, please.
- این پارچه‌ی ترمه دست‌باف است؟  
  *in pārche-ye terme dast-bāf ast?* — Is this termeh cloth hand-woven?
- تابستان‌ها هوای یزد خیلی گرم و خشک است.  
  *tābestān-hā havā-ye Yazd kheyli garm o khoshk ast.* — In summer the weather in Yazd is very hot and dry.
- شب در کویر آسمان پر از ستاره است.  
  *shab dar kavir āsmān por az setāre ast.* — At night in the desert the sky is full of stars.
