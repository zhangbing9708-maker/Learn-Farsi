# The Armenians, Jews and Zoroastrians of Iran
## ارمنیان، یهودیان و زرتشتیان ایران
*armaniyān, yahudiyān va zartoshtiyān-e Irān* — [read on the website with audio](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/culture/history-the-armenians-jews-and-zoroastrians-of-iran.html)

_Iran is mostly Shia Muslim, but some of its oldest communities are not. Armenian Christians, Jews and Zoroastrians have lived on the Iranian plateau for many centuries – the Jews and Zoroastrians for well over two thousand years – and each has left its mark on Iranian cities, languages and memory._

### Old communities, recognised minorities

Iran's constitution recognises three non-Muslim اقلیت‌های دینی (*aghalliyat-hā-ye dini*, ‘religious minorities’): Zoroastrians, Jews and Christians. Five of the 290 seats in مجلس (*majles*, ‘parliament’) are reserved for them: two for Armenians, one for Assyrian and Chaldean Christians, one for Jews and one for Zoroastrians. Their members have their own schools, places of worship and family law.

The communities are small today, and many members have emigrated since the 1979 revolution. Their situation has known good and hard periods; the followers of religions not on this list, above all the Baha'is, are not recognised at all. But Armenians, Jews and Zoroastrians are an old and familiar part of Iranian life, and they share with their Muslim neighbours the festivals of نوروز (*nowruz*, ‘Nowruz’) and یلدا (*yaldā*, ‘Yalda’).

### The Armenians and New Julfa

Armenians have lived in north-western Iran since ancient times; the Armenian monasteries of Azerbaijan province, such as St Thaddeus near Maku, have been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2008. But the best-known Armenian community is in Isfahan, and its story begins with a forced move.

In 1604–05, during his wars with the Ottomans, Shah Abbas I deported the Armenians of Julfa, a rich trading town on the Aras river, and settled them across the river from his new capital. Their quarter was called جلفای نو (*Jolfā-ye now*, ‘New Julfa’), or simply جلفا (*Jolfā*, ‘Jolfa’). Armenian merchants soon ran much of Iran's silk trade, from India to Amsterdam. Their کلیسای وانک (*Kelisā-ye Vānk*, ‘Vank Cathedral’), finished in the 1660s, looks like a mosque from outside, with a Persian dome, and is covered inside with paintings of Bible stories. The first printing press in Iran was set up in New Julfa in the 1630s. Today most Iranian Armenians live in Tehran, and they celebrate Christmas on 6 January.

### Jews in Iran: from Cyrus to today

![The Cyrus Cylinder, 6th century BC](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e5/Cyrus_Cylinder_front.jpg/960px-Cyrus_Cylinder_front.jpg)
<sub>The Cyrus Cylinder, 6th century BC. Photo: [Prioryman](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cyrus_Cylinder_front.jpg), CC BY-SA 3.0.</sub>

Jewish history in Iran begins with کوروش (*Kurosh*, ‘Cyrus’). When he took Babylon in 539 BC, he allowed the Jews exiled there to return to Jerusalem and rebuild their Temple; the Bible calls him the Lord's 'anointed'. Many Jews stayed in Babylonia and in Iran. The Book of Esther is set at the Persian court in Susa, and the shrine of استر و مردخای (*Ester va Mordekhāy*, ‘Esther and Mordecai’) in Hamadan, like the tomb of the prophet Daniel at Shush, is still a place of pilgrimage.

Persian Jews wrote their language in Hebrew letters, and some of these Judeo-Persian texts are among the oldest written examples of New Persian. Over the centuries the community knew both tolerance and persecution. Today it numbers several thousand, the largest Jewish community in the Middle East outside Israel, with synagogues in Tehran, Isfahan and Shiraz. In Iran Jews are often politely called کلیمی (*kalimi*, ‘'followers of Moses'’).

### Zoroastrians of Yazd and Kerman

![The Zoroastrian fire temple of Yazd](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5f/Zoroastrian_Fire_Temple_in_Yazd.JPG/960px-Zoroastrian_Fire_Temple_in_Yazd.JPG)
<sub>The Zoroastrian fire temple of Yazd. Photo: [Zenith210](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Zoroastrian_Fire_Temple_in_Yazd.JPG), CC BY-SA 3.0.</sub>

Zoroastrianism was the main religion of Iran before Islam. After the Arab conquest the number of زرتشتیان (*zartoshtiyān*, ‘Zoroastrians’) slowly fell; some left for India, where their descendants are the Parsis, and others held on in the desert cities of یزد (*Yazd*, ‘Yazd’) and کرمان (*Kermān*, ‘Kerman’). Zoroastrians call themselves بهدین (*behdin*, ‘'of the good religion'’); the old word *gabr* is an insult and should be avoided.

In the آتشکده (*ātashkade*, ‘fire temple’) of Yazd burns a sacred fire that is said to have been kept alight for some fifteen centuries. On the hills outside the city stand the دخمه (*dakhme*, ‘towers of silence’), where the dead were once laid out, a custom abandoned in the twentieth century. Every June pilgrims climb to the mountain shrine of چک‌چک (*Chak-chak*, ‘Chak Chak’) near Ardakan, where water drips from the rock.

Iranians often point out how much of their national culture these communities helped to shape: Zoroastrian festivals like Sadeh and Mehregan, Armenian craftsmen and photographers in Qajar Tehran, Jewish musicians and doctors. Learning a few words – کلیسا (*kelisā*, ‘church’), کنیسه (*kanise*, ‘synagogue’), آتشکده (*ātashkade*, ‘fire temple’) – is a small way of seeing that Iran's history has always had more than one voice.

### Facts

- Article 13 of Iran's constitution recognises Zoroastrians, Jews and Christians as religious minorities; five seats in parliament are reserved for them.
- Shah Abbas I moved the Armenians of Julfa on the Aras river to Isfahan in 1604–05.
- The Armenian monastic ensembles of Iran, including St Thaddeus near Maku, became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2008.
- Cyrus the Great took Babylon in 539 BC and allowed the exiled Jews to return to Jerusalem.
- Judeo-Persian – Persian written in Hebrew letters – includes some of the oldest surviving texts in New Persian.
- The Parsis of India are descendants of Zoroastrians who left Iran after the Arab conquest.
- The pilgrimage to Chak Chak near Ardakan takes place every year in June.

### Say it in Persian

- در ایران پیروان چند دین قدیمی زندگی می‌کنند.  
  *dar Irān peyrovān-e chand din-e ghadimi zendegi mi-konand.* — Followers of several ancient religions live in Iran.
- ارمنی‌ها، یهودیان و زرتشتیان نمایندگان خودشان را در مجلس دارند.  
  *armani-hā, yahudiyān va zartoshtiyān namāyandegān-e khod-eshān rā dar majles dārand.* — Armenians, Jews and Zoroastrians have their own representatives in parliament.
- شاه عباس ارمنی‌ها را از جلفا به اصفهان آورد.  
  *Shāh 'Abbās armani-hā rā az Jolfā be Esfahān āvard.* — Shah Abbas brought the Armenians from Julfa to Isfahan.
- کلیسای وانک در محله‌ی جلفای اصفهان است.  
  *Kelisā-ye Vānk dar mahalle-ye Jolfā-ye Esfahān ast.* — Vank Cathedral is in the Jolfa quarter of Isfahan.
- دیوارهای این کلیسا پر از نقاشی است.  
  *divār-hā-ye in kelisā por az naghghāshi ast.* — The walls of this church are covered with paintings.
- کوروش به یهودیان اجازه داد به اورشلیم برگردند.  
  *Kurosh be yahudiyān ejāze dād be Urshalim bargardand.* — Cyrus allowed the Jews to return to Jerusalem.
- آرامگاه استر و مردخای در همدان است.  
  *ārāmgāh-e Ester va Mordekhāy dar Hamedān ast.* — The tomb of Esther and Mordecai is in Hamadan.
- در ایران به یهودیان «کلیمی» هم می‌گویند.  
  *dar Irān be yahudiyān «kalimi» ham mi-guyand.* — In Iran Jews are also called 'kalimi'.
- یزد و کرمان شهرهای مهم زرتشتیان هستند.  
  *Yazd va Kermān shahr-hā-ye mohemm-e zartoshtiyān hastand.* — Yazd and Kerman are important cities for Zoroastrians.
- در آتشکده‌ی یزد آتشی است که قرن‌ها روشن مانده است.  
  *dar ātashkade-ye Yazd ātash-i ast ke gharn-hā rowshan mānde ast.* — In the fire temple of Yazd there is a fire that has stayed alight for centuries.
- زرتشتیان خودشان را «بهدین» هم می‌نامند.  
  *zartoshtiyān khod-eshān rā «behdin» ham mi-nāmand.* — Zoroastrians also call themselves 'behdin'.
- هر سال در تابستان زرتشتیان به زیارتگاه چک‌چک می‌روند.  
  *har sāl dar tābestān zartoshtiyān be ziyāratgāh-e Chak-chak mi-ravand.* — Every summer Zoroastrians go to the shrine of Chak Chak.
