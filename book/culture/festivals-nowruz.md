# Nowruz, the Persian New Year
## نوروز، جشن سال نو
*nowruz, jashn-e sāl-e now* — [read on the website with audio](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/culture/festivals-nowruz.html)

_Nowruz, the 'new day', begins the Iranian year at the spring equinox. Weeks of cleaning and shopping lead up to one exact moment around the haft-sin table – followed by two weeks of visits, eydi and sweets._

### A new day

نوروز (*nowruz*, ‘Nowruz’) means 'new day'. It is the first day of فروردین (*Farvardin*, ‘the first month’) and of the Iranian solar year, and it falls on the spring equinox, around 20–21 March. The festival is far older than Islam in Iran. In the Shahnameh, Ferdowsi tells how the legendary king جمشید (*Jamshid*) rose into the sky on a jewelled throne and the people named that day the new day; by Sasanian times it was the greatest feast of the year. Today Nowruz is shared far beyond Iran – by Kurds, Afghans, Tajiks, Azerbaijanis and many others – and since 2010 the United Nations has marked 21 March as the International Day of Nowruz.

### Shaking the house

![Sabzeh, sprouted wheat for Nowruz](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ed/Haft-Seen_(6).jpg/960px-Haft-Seen_(6).jpg)
<sub>Sabzeh, sprouted wheat for Nowruz. Photo: [Elekes Andor](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Haft-Seen_(6).jpg), CC BY-SA 4.0.</sub>

Preparations begin weeks ahead with خانه‌تکانی (*khāne-tekāni*, ‘'shaking the house'’), a top-to-bottom spring clean: carpets are washed, curtains taken down, windows polished and broken things thrown out. Everyone gets لباس نو (*lebās-e now*, ‘new clothes’), and the bazaars stay open late. About two weeks before the New Year, someone soaks wheat or lentils and spreads them on a plate to grow the سبزه (*sabze*, ‘sprouted greens’). On the last Tuesday evening of the year comes the fire festival of چهارشنبه‌سوری (*chahārshanbe-suri*, ‘Chaharshanbe Suri’).

### The haft-sin, item by item

![A goldfish, part of the Nowruz table](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/65/Gold_fish1.jpg/960px-Gold_fish1.jpg)
<sub>A goldfish, part of the Nowruz table. Photo: [לינה אבוגוש](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gold_fish1.jpg), CC BY-SA 3.0.</sub>

The heart of Nowruz is the سفره‌ی هفت‌سین (*sofre-ye haft-sin*, ‘haft-sin spread’): a cloth laid with seven things whose names begin with the letter *sin* (س). Each carries a wish – these are the meanings families usually give them:

- سبزه (*sabze*, ‘sprouts’) – rebirth and growth
- سمنو (*samanu*, ‘sweet wheat pudding’) – plenty
- سنجد (*senjed*, ‘dried oleaster fruit’) – love
- سیب (*sib*, ‘apple’) – health and beauty
- سیر (*sir*, ‘garlic’) – medicine
- سماق (*somāgh*, ‘sumac’) – the colour of sunrise
- سرکه (*serke*, ‘vinegar’) – age and patience

A coin, سکه (*sekke*), or a hyacinth, سنبل (*sonbol*), may take the place of any of them. Around the seven go a آینه (*āyene*, ‘mirror’), candles, تخم‌مرغ رنگی (*tokhm-e morgh-e rangi*, ‘painted eggs’), a bowl with a ماهی قرمز (*māhi-ye ghermez*, ‘goldfish’), and a book: the Quran in many homes, the Divan of Hafez or the Shahnameh in others.

### The moment the year turns

Nowruz does not begin at midnight. It begins at the exact moment of the equinox, تحویل سال (*tahvil-e sāl*, ‘the turning of the year’), which can fall at any hour – in the middle of the afternoon or at four in the morning. Families gather in their new clothes around the haft-sin while the television counts down the last seconds, and many say a short prayer asking God to turn their state 'to the best of states'. Then the year turns: everyone hugs and kisses, says عید شما مبارک (*eyd-e shomā mobārak*, ‘happy New Year to you’), and the eldest hands out عیدی (*eydi*, ‘New Year money’) – crisp new banknotes, often kept between the pages of the book on the spread.

### Visits and eydi

The days that follow are for دید و بازدید (*did o bāzdid*, ‘visits and return visits’). The order matters: the young call first on the oldest of the family – grandparents, then elder uncles and aunts – who return the visits later. Every home keeps tea, fruit, آجیل (*ājil*, ‘nuts and dried fruit’) and شیرینی (*shirini*, ‘pastries’) ready, and children collect eydi in house after house. Offices close for the first four days and schools for about two weeks, so Tehran empties as families travel. If you are invited, take pastries or flowers and expect to be fed generously. The holiday ends on the thirteenth day with the picnic of سیزده‌به‌در (*sizdah-be-dar*, ‘Sizdah Bedar’).

### Hajji Firuz and Amu Nowruz

![Hajji Firuz, the Nowruz herald](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/Haji_Firuz_on_the_road.jpg/960px-Haji_Firuz_on_the_road.jpg)
<sub>Hajji Firuz, the Nowruz herald. Photo: [ninara](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Haji_Firuz_on_the_road.jpg), CC BY-SA 2.0.</sub>

In the weeks before Nowruz you may meet حاجی فیروز (*Hāji Firuz*, ‘Hajji Firuz’) in the street: a merry herald in red satin and a pointed hat who beats a دایره (*dāyere*, ‘frame drum’) and sings حاجی فیروزه، سالی یه روزه (*Hāji Firuz-e, sāli ye ruz-e*, ‘it's Hajji Firuz, it comes one day a year’). His face is traditionally blackened with soot, a custom many now criticise, and some performers have dropped it. His gentle counterpart is عمو نوروز (*Amu Nowruz*, ‘Uncle Nowruz’), a white-bearded old man in a felt hat who brings presents for children. In the folk tale his sweetheart, ننه سرما (*Nane Sarmā*, ‘Granny Cold’), waits for him every year but always falls asleep just before he arrives.

### Facts

- Nowruz falls on the March equinox, 1 Farvardin, the first day of the Iranian solar year.
- The year turns at the exact astronomical moment of the equinox, so families may gather round the haft-sin at any hour of the day or night.
- In the Shahnameh, Ferdowsi credits the legendary king Jamshid with the first Nowruz.
- UNESCO first inscribed Nowruz on its list of the intangible heritage of humanity in 2009, as a festival shared by several countries.
- In 2010 the UN General Assembly proclaimed 21 March the International Day of Nowruz.
- Iran's official Nowruz holiday is the first four days of Farvardin, but schools stay closed until after Sizdah Bedar.
- Before Nowruz people queue at banks for crisp new banknotes to give as eydi.

### Say it in Persian

- نوروز مبارک!  
  *nowruz mobārak!* — Happy Nowruz!
- سال نو مبارک!  
  *sāl-e now mobārak!* — Happy New Year!
- عید شما هم مبارک!  
  *eyd-e shomā ham mobārak!* — Happy New Year to you too!  
  _The usual reply to a New Year greeting._
- صد سال به این سال‌ها!  
  *sad sāl be in sāl-hā!* — May you see a hundred more years like this one!  
  Literally: a hundred years to these years
- هر روزتان نوروز، نوروزتان پیروز!  
  *har ruz-etān nowruz, nowruz-etān piruz!* — May every day be Nowruz for you, and every Nowruz a happy one!  
  Literally: your every day Nowruz, your Nowruz victorious
- سال خوبی داشته باشید.  
  *sāl-e khub-i dāshte bāshid.* — Have a good year.
- قبل از عید خانه‌تکانی می‌کنیم.  
  *ghabl az eyd khāne-tekāni mi-konim.* — Before the New Year we give the whole house a spring clean.
- سفره‌ی هفت‌سین را کنار پنجره چیده‌ایم.  
  *sofre-ye haft-sin rā kenār-e panjere chide-im.* — We've laid out the haft-sin by the window.
- سال ساعت چند تحویل می‌شود؟  
  *sāl sā'at-e chand tahvil mi-shavad?* — What time does the new year begin?  
  Spoken: سال ساعت چند تحویل می‌شه؟ *sāl sā'at-e chand tahvil mi-she?*  
  Literally: at what hour is the year handed over?
- بچه‌ها از پدربزرگ عیدی گرفتند.  
  *bacche-hā az pedar-bozorg eydi gereftand.* — The children got their New Year money from Grandpa.
- فردا برای عیددیدنی به خانه‌ی مادربزرگ می‌رویم.  
  *fardā barā-ye eyd-didani be khāne-ye mādar-bozorg mi-ravim.* — Tomorrow we're going to Grandma's for a New Year visit.  
  Spoken: فردا برای عیددیدنی می‌ریم خونه‌ی مادربزرگ. *fardā barā-ye eyd-didani mi-rim khune-ye mādar-bozorg.*
- حاجی فیروز با دایره در خیابان می‌خواند.  
  *Hāji Firuz bā dāyere dar khiyābān mi-khānad.* — Hajji Firuz sings in the street, beating his drum.
