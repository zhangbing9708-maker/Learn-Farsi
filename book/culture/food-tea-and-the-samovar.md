# Tea and the samovar
## چای و سماور
*chāy o samāvar* — [read on the website with audio](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/culture/food-tea-and-the-samovar.html)

_Strong, clear and amber-red, served in small glasses with a lump of sugar: tea is Iran's everyday drink, poured from a teapot that sits on top of a bubbling samovar._

### From coffee to tea

Iranians drink چای (*chāy*, ‘tea’) from morning to night: at breakfast, after every meal, in shops and offices, and the moment a guest walks in. It was not always so. In Safavid times the fashionable drink was coffee, served in the new قهوه‌خانه (*ghahve-khāne*, ‘coffeehouse’)s of Isfahan. In the nineteenth century tea, coming in from Russia and India, gradually took its place – but the coffeehouse kept its name, and a traditional Iranian *ghahvekhaneh* serves tea, not coffee.

### The father of Iranian tea

Iran grows its own tea thanks to a Qajar prince and diplomat, کاشف‌السلطنه (*Kāshef-os-saltane*, ‘Kashef al-Saltaneh’). Around 1900, after serving in British India, he brought tea plants and seeds back to لاهیجان (*Lāhijān*, ‘Lahijan’) in Gilan and showed that they would grow there. The green, rainy hills of Gilan and Mazandaran are still Iran's tea country, and his tomb in Lahijan stands beside a museum of tea. Iranians call him the father of Iranian tea.

### The samovar

![A glass of Persian tea](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/2b/Persian_tea_glass.JPG/960px-Persian_tea_glass.JPG)
<sub>A glass of Persian tea. Photo: [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Persian_tea_glass.JPG), Public domain.</sub>

The سماور (*samāvar*, ‘samovar’) arrived from Russia in the nineteenth century – its Russian name means 'self-boiler' – and Iranian workshops soon made their own. Water boils in the urn, heated by charcoal in the old days and by gas or electricity today, and on top sits a قوری (*ghuri*, ‘teapot’) of strong tea, brewing slowly in the rising heat (دم کشیدن (*dam keshidan*, ‘to brew’)).

To serve, you pour a little of the strong brew into a glass and top it up with hot water from the samovar's tap: more water for weak tea (کم‌رنگ (*kam-rang*, ‘weak, 'little colour'’)), less for strong (پررنگ (*porrang*, ‘strong, 'full of colour'’)). Iranians judge tea by its colour, which should be clear and a deep reddish amber.

### Sugar on the side

![Nabat, saffron rock sugar](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cb/Iranian_nabat.jpg/960px-Iranian_nabat.jpg)
<sub>Nabat, saffron rock sugar. Photo: [Teardrops777](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Iranian_nabat.jpg), CC BY 4.0.</sub>

Tea is served in small clear glasses (استکان (*estekān*, ‘tea glass’)) on saucers (نعلبکی (*na'lbeki*, ‘saucer’)), so that you can admire the colour, and never with milk. Many Iranians take a sugar lump (قند (*ghand*, ‘sugar lump’)), dip it, hold it between their teeth and sip the tea through it – چای قندپهلو (*chāy-e ghand-pahlu*, ‘tea with sugar 'on the side'’). Others prefer نبات (*nabāt*, ‘rock candy’): yellow saffron rock candy on a stick, stirred into the glass. چای‌نبات (*chāy-nabāt*, ‘tea with rock candy’) is the classic remedy when someone feels unwell, and cardamom (هل (*hel*, ‘cardamom’)) is a favourite addition. An old saying describes the perfect glass: لب‌سوز، لب‌دوز، لب‌ریز (*lab-suz, lab-duz, lab-riz*, ‘lip-burning, lip-sticking, brim-full’) – hot, sweet and filled to the top.

### Tea and hospitality

![A coffee-house painting of the battle of Karbala](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4a/Iran_Battle_of_Karbala_19th_century.jpg/960px-Iran_Battle_of_Karbala_19th_century.jpg)
<sub>A coffee-house painting of the battle of Karbala. Photo: [Photo: Andreas Praefcke](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Iran_Battle_of_Karbala_19th_century.jpg), Public domain.</sub>

Offering tea is the first act of hospitality. A guest has hardly sat down before a tray (سینی (*sini*, ‘tray’)) of tea glasses appears, and saying no the first time is just polite تعارف (*ta'ārof*, ‘ritual politeness’) – the host will ask again. Shopkeepers in the bazaar offer tea while you bargain, and offices run on it.

The old coffeehouse was also a theatre. There a storyteller (نقال (*nagghāl*, ‘storyteller’)) performed tales from the Shahnameh beneath large paintings of epic and religious scenes, in the style now called coffeehouse painting. This storytelling, *naqqali*, was inscribed on UNESCO's list of heritage in need of urgent safeguarding in 2011.

### Facts

- Iran's tea industry began around 1900, when Prince Mohammad Mirza Kashef al-Saltaneh brought tea plants from India to Lahijan.
- Kashef al-Saltaneh's tomb in Lahijan stands beside a museum of tea.
- Samovar is Russian for 'self-boiler'; estekan, the tea glass, is usually traced to Russian stakan, 'glass'.
- Iranian tea is drunk without milk, often with a sugar lump held between the teeth.
- Iran's coffeehouses served coffee in Safavid times; tea replaced it in the 19th century, but the name ghahvekhaneh stayed.
- Naqqali, Iranian dramatic storytelling, was inscribed on UNESCO's Urgent Safeguarding List in 2011.

### Say it in Persian

- چای می‌خورید؟  
  *chāy mi-khorid?* — Would you like some tea?  
  Spoken: چای می‌خورین؟ *chāy mi-khorin?*
- بله، لطفاً؛ کم‌رنگ باشد.  
  *bale, lotfan; kam-rang bāshad.* — Yes, please – weak.  
  Spoken: آره، لطفاً؛ کم‌رنگ باشه. *āre, lotfan; kam-rang bāshe.*
- سماور را روشن کن تا آب جوش بیاید.  
  *samāvar rā rowshan kon tā āb jush bi-yāyad.* — Switch on the samovar so that the water comes to the boil.
- چای را در قوری بریز و بگذار دم بکشد.  
  *chāy rā dar ghuri be-riz va bo-gzār dam be-keshad.* — Put the tea in the teapot and let it brew.
- ما چای را با قند می‌خوریم، نه با شیر.  
  *mā chāy rā bā ghand mi-khorim, na bā shir.* — We drink tea with sugar lumps, not with milk.
- یک استکان چای داغ خستگی را از تن آدم بیرون می‌کند.  
  *yek estekān chāy-e dāgh khastegi rā az tan-e ādam birun mi-konad.* — A glass of hot tea drives the tiredness out of your body.
- چای باید لب‌سوز، لب‌دوز و لب‌ریز باشد.  
  *chāy bāyad lab-suz, lab-duz va lab-riz bāshad.* — Tea should be lip-burning, lip-sticking and brim-full.  
  _An old saying: hot, sweet and filled to the top._
- دلم درد می‌کند؛ یک چای‌نبات برایم درست می‌کنی؟  
  *del-am dard mi-konad; yek chāy-nabāt barā-yam dorost mi-koni?* — I've got a stomach ache – will you make me a tea with nabat?  
  Spoken: دلم درد می‌کنه؛ یه چای‌نبات برام درست می‌کنی؟ *del-am dard mi-kone; ye chāy-nabāt barā-m dorost mi-koni?*
- تعارف نکنید، یک چای دیگر بخورید.  
  *ta'ārof na-konid, yek chāy-e digar bo-khorid.* — Don't be shy – have another tea.  
  Spoken: تعارف نکنین، یه چای دیگه بخورین. *ta'ārof na-konin, ye chāy-e dige bo-khorin.*
- چای ایرانی بیشتر در گیلان و مازندران کاشته می‌شود.  
  *chāy-e irāni bishtar dar Gilān va Māzandarān kāshte mi-shavad.* — Iranian tea is grown mostly in Gilan and Mazandaran.
- قدیم‌ها نقال‌ها در قهوه‌خانه‌ها داستان‌های شاهنامه را می‌گفتند.  
  *ghadim-hā nagghāl-hā dar ghahve-khāne-hā dāstān-hā-ye Shāhnāme rā mi-goftand.* — In the old days storytellers told the tales of the Shahnameh in the coffeehouses.
