# The palace of Jamshid
## آن قصر که جمشید
Khayyam (خیام), Rubaiyat, 11th century. [Listen on the website](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/poetry/khayyam-an-ghasr.html)

These six quatrains are about ruins, dust and the people who went before us – the classic theme of the poems that go under the name of Omar Khayyam (1048–1131), the mathematician and astronomer of Nishapur. But did he write them? No writer of his own time mentions any Persian verse by him: the first quatrains quoted as his appear some seventy years or more after his death, the oldest anthologies have a few dozen, and later manuscripts several hundred, even over a thousand. Scholars call many of them 'wandering quatrains', because the same poem turns up under several names. Their fame in the West began with Edward FitzGerald's very free English versions of 1859, which made 'Omar' a household name in Britain and America. Whoever wrote them, Iranians read them as Khayyam's.

### The poem

> آن قصر که جمشید در او جام گرفت &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; آهو بچه کرد و روبه آرام گرفت  
> بهرام که گور می‌گرفتی همه عمر &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; دیدی که چگونه گور بهرام گرفت  
> آن قصر که بر چرخ همی‌زد پهلو &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; بر درگه او شهان نهادندی رو  
> دیدیم که بر کنگره‌اش فاخته‌ای &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; بنشسته همی‌گفت که کوکو کوکو  
> جامی است که عقل آفرین می‌زندش &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; صد بوسه ز مهر بر جبین می‌زندش  
> این کوزه‌گر دهر چنین جام لطیف &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; می‌سازد و باز بر زمین می‌زندش  
> این کوزه چو من عاشق زاری بوده است &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; در بند سر زلف نگاری بوده است  
> این دسته که بر گردن او می‌بینی &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; دستی است که بر گردن یاری بوده است  
> هر سبزه که بر کنار جویی رسته است &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; گویی ز لب فرشته‌خویی رسته است  
> پا بر سر سبزه تا به خواری ننهی &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; کان سبزه ز خاک لاله‌رویی رسته است  
> ای بس که نباشیم و جهان خواهد بود &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; نی نام ز ما و نی نشان خواهد بود  
> زین پیش نبودیم و نبد هیچ خلل &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; زین پس چو نباشیم همان خواهد بود  

### Line by line

- آن قصر که جمشید در او جام گرفت / آهو بچه کرد و روبه آرام گرفت  
  *ān ghasr ke jamshid dar u jām gereft / āhu bacche kard o rubah ārām gereft* — In the palace where Jamshid once raised his cup, / the deer has borne its young and the fox has made its home.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  قصر *ghasr* palace · جمشید *jamshid* Jamshid, a legendary king of Iran, owner of a magic cup · جام گرفتن *jām gereftan* to take up the cup – to feast · آهو *āhu* gazelle, deer · بچه کردن *bacche kardan* to give birth, to have young · روبه *rubah* fox – today روباه · آرام گرفتن *ārām gereftan* to settle down, to rest

  </details>
- بهرام که گور می‌گرفتی همه عمر / دیدی که چگونه گور بهرام گرفت  
  *bahrām ke gur mi-gerefti hame 'omr / didi ke chegune gur bahrām gereft* — Bahram, who spent his whole life catching wild asses – / you saw how the grave caught Bahram.  
  _گور is both 'wild ass, onager' and 'grave'. Bahram Gur, a Sasanian king of the fifth century, was famous as a hunter of onagers; in the end the grave hunted him._
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  بهرام *bahrām* Bahram Gur, a Sasanian king and great hunter · گور *gur* wild ass, onager; also 'grave' · می‌گرفتی *mi-gerefti* used to catch – the old habitual ـی (today می‌گرفت) · همه عمر *hame 'omr* all his life · دیدی *didi* you saw, did you see · چگونه *chegune* how

  </details>
- آن قصر که بر چرخ همی‌زد پهلو / بر درگه او شهان نهادندی رو  
  *ān ghasr ke bar charkh hami-zad pahlu / bar dargah-e u shahān nahādandi ru* — That palace which rubbed shoulders with the sky, / where kings once bowed their faces at the door –
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  چرخ *charkh* the wheel – the sky, the heavens · پهلو زدن *pahlu zadan* to rival, to rub shoulders with (lit. 'to strike side') · همی‌زد *hami-zad* used to strike – همی = می · درگه *dargah* doorway, court – short for درگاه · شهان *shahān* kings – شاهان · رو نهادن *ru nahādan* to lay one's face (on the ground), to bow · نهادندی *nahādandi* used to lay – the old habitual ـی

  </details>
- دیدیم که بر کنگره‌اش فاخته‌ای / بنشسته همی‌گفت که کوکو کوکو  
  *didim ke bar kongere-ash fākhte-i / benshaste hami-goft ke kuku kuku* — we saw a ring dove perched on its battlements, / calling: 'Where, where? Where, where?'  
  _کو *ku* means 'where?', so the dove's call کوکو asks where the kings have gone._
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  دیدیم *didim* we saw · کنگره *kongere* battlement · فاخته *fākhte* ring dove, collared dove · بنشسته *benshaste* sitting, perched – old ب- + نشسته · همی‌گفت *hami-goft* was saying – همی = می · کو *ku* where? – old form of کجا

  </details>
- جامی است که عقل آفرین می‌زندش / صد بوسه ز مهر بر جبین می‌زندش  
  *jāmi ast ke 'aghl āfarin mi-zanad-ash / sad buse ze mehr bar jabin mi-zanad-ash* — Here is a cup that reason cries 'bravo!' to, / and plants a hundred loving kisses on its brow;
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  جامی است *jāmi ast* there is a cup, it is a cup · عقل *'aghl* reason, intellect · آفرین زدن *āfarin zadan* to cry 'bravo!', to praise · می‌زندش *mi-zanad-ash* strikes it, gives it – ـش 'it' · مهر *mehr* love, affection · جبین *jabin* brow, forehead

  </details>
- این کوزه‌گر دهر چنین جام لطیف / می‌سازد و باز بر زمین می‌زندش  
  *in kuze-gar-e dahr chonin jām-e latif / mi-sāzad o bāz bar zamin mi-zanad-ash* — yet the potter of time makes so fine a cup / and then smashes it on the ground.  
  _The cup is the human body – or the head, with its brow – made by the potter of the world and broken again._
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  کوزه‌گر *kuze-gar* potter (کوزه 'jug' + ـگر 'maker') · دهر *dahr* time, fate, the world · چنین *chonin* such, so · لطیف *latif* delicate, fine · باز *bāz* then again · بر زمین زدن *bar zamin zadan* to throw to the ground, to smash

  </details>
- این کوزه چو من عاشق زاری بوده است / در بند سر زلف نگاری بوده است  
  *in kuze cho man 'āshegh-e zāri bude ast / dar band-e sar-e zolf-e negāri bude ast* — This jug was once a wretched lover like me, / caught in the tangle of a beauty's curls.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  کوزه *kuze* jug, water jar · چو من *cho man* like me – چو = چون · زار *zār* miserable, weeping · بوده است *bude ast* has been, was once · در بند *dar band-e* in the bonds of, captive to · زلف *zolf* hair, curls · نگار *negār* a beauty, a beloved (lit. 'painting')

  </details>
- این دسته که بر گردن او می‌بینی / دستی است که بر گردن یاری بوده است  
  *in daste ke bar gardan-e u mi-bini / dasti ast ke bar gardan-e yāri bude ast* — This handle you see on its neck / was an arm once wrapped round a lover's neck.  
  _دسته 'handle' comes from دست 'hand, arm': the potter's clay was once a living arm._
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  دسته *daste* handle · گردن *gardan* neck · می‌بینی *mi-bini* you see · دستی است *dasti ast* is a hand, an arm · یار *yār* lover, friend

  </details>
- هر سبزه که بر کنار جویی رسته است / گویی ز لب فرشته‌خویی رسته است  
  *har sabze ke bar kenār-e juyi roste ast / guyi ze lab-e fereshte-khuyi roste ast* — Every blade of grass that grows on the bank of a stream / seems to have sprung from the lips of someone angel-sweet.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  سبزه *sabze* grass, greenery · کنار *kenār* edge, bank · جوی *juy* stream, brook · رستن *rostan* to grow, to sprout; رسته است 'has grown' · گویی *guyi* you would say, as if · فرشته‌خو *fereshte-khu* angel-natured, angelic

  </details>
- پا بر سر سبزه تا به خواری ننهی / کان سبزه ز خاک لاله‌رویی رسته است  
  *pā bar sar-e sabze tā be khāri nanahi / kān sabze ze khāk-e lāle-ruyi roste ast* — Mind you do not tread on the grass with contempt, / for it grew from the dust of a tulip-cheeked beauty.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  پا نهادن *pā nahādan* to set foot, to tread · تا … ننهی *tā … nanahi* mind you do not set – تا here warns 'take care' · خواری *khāri* contempt, disrespect · کان *kān* for that – که + آن · خاک *khāk* dust, earth · لاله‌رو *lāle-ru* tulip-faced, rosy-cheeked

  </details>
- ای بس که نباشیم و جهان خواهد بود / نی نام ز ما و نی نشان خواهد بود  
  *ey bas ke nabāshim o jahān khāhad bud / ney nām ze mā vo ney neshān khāhad bud* — Long after we are gone the world will still be here, / with no name of ours and no trace of us.  
  Literally: O how long we shall not be and the world will be; there will be no name of us and no trace
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  ای بس *ey bas* how long, many a… – an exclamation · نباشیم *nabāshim* we shall not be · خواهد بود *khāhad bud* will be · نی *ney* nor, not – old form of نه · نشان *neshān* trace, sign

  </details>
- زین پیش نبودیم و نبد هیچ خلل / زین پس چو نباشیم همان خواهد بود  
  *zin pish nabudim o nabod hich khalal / zin pas cho nabāshim hamān khāhad bud* — Before this we did not exist, and nothing was missing; / when we are gone, it will be just the same.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  زین پیش *zin pish* before this – از این پیش · نبد *nabod* was not – short for نبود · خلل *khalal* flaw, gap, something wrong · زین پس *zin pas* after this, from now on · چو *cho* when – short for چون · همان *hamān* the same

  </details>

### Reading the poem

**The images.** These quatrains look at the world the way an archaeologist does. The palace where the legendary king Jamshid held his feasts now shelters deer and foxes – and Iranians still call the ruins of Persepolis تخت جمشید (*takht-e jamshid*), 'the throne of Jamshid'. A dove on the battlements of another palace calls *ku? ku?*, 'where? where?'. The potter's jug was once a lover, and its handle an arm round someone's neck; the grass by the stream grew from a lovely face. Everything around us is made of the dust of the people before us – and the world did not miss us before we were born and will not miss us when we are gone. The conclusion, in the other Khayyam quatrains, is always the same: enjoy today.

**The rhythm.** Each quatrain rhymes in lines 1, 2 and 4, usually with a repeated word or phrase after the rhyme, a ردیف (*radif*): گرفت (*gereft*), می‌زندش (*mi-zanad-ash*), بوده است (*bude ast*), رسته است (*roste ast*), خواهد بود (*khāhad bud*). The *robā'i* metre has a few variations but always the same heavy swing, close to the Arabic *lā howla va lā ghovvata ellā be-llāh*. Try *ān ghasr ke jam-shid da-ru jām ge-reft*, reading در او (*dar u*) as *daru*.

**The grammar.** Old habitual verbs end in ی (*-i*): می‌گرفتی (*mi-gerefti*) 'he used to catch' and نهادندی (*nahādandi*) 'they used to lay', where modern Persian says می‌گرفت (*mi-gereft*) and می‌نهادند (*mi-nahādand*). همی (*hami*) is the old می (*mi-*), نبد (*nabod*) is نبود (*nabud*), زین (*zin*) is از این (*az in*), and شهان (*shahān*) is شاهان (*shāhān*). The second quatrain in modern prose: آن قصری که به آسمان می‌رسید و شاهان بر درگاهش سجده می‌کردند، دیدیم که فاخته‌ای بر کنگره‌اش نشسته بود و می‌گفت: کو؟ کو؟ (*ān ghasri ke be āsemān mi-resid va shāhān bar dargāh-ash sajde mi-kardand, didim ke fākhte-i bar kongere-ash neshaste bud va mi-goft: ku? ku?*)

**Using it.** Iranians quote دیدی که چگونه گور بهرام گرفت (*didi ke chegune gur bahrām gereft*) when the mighty fall, and ای بس که نباشیم و جهان خواهد بود (*ey bas ke nabāshim o jahān khāhad bud*) to anyone who thinks they cannot be done without. The talking jugs are among the best-known images of Persian poetry, and Khayyam's garden tomb in Nishapur, with quatrains set into its tilework, is a favourite visit for Iranians and foreigners alike.
