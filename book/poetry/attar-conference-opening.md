# The birds gather
## مجمع مرغان
Attar (عطار), Mantiq al-Tayr, 12th century. [Listen on the website](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/poetry/attar-conference-opening.html)

Farid al-Din Attar (about 1145–1221) was a pharmacist of Nishapur – *'attār* means 'seller of perfumes and drugs' – and one of the great Sufi poets. In his *Mantiq al-Tayr* ('The Speech of the Birds', 1177) all the birds of the world meet to find a king. The hoopoe, once King Solomon's messenger, tells them they have one: the Simorgh, who lives behind Mount Qaf at the edge of the world. The birds set off, but one by one they make excuses – the nightingale cannot leave the rose, the peacock dreams of paradise, the duck will not leave its water – and the hoopoe answers each with a story. The road crosses seven valleys: quest, love, knowledge, detachment, unity, bewilderment, and poverty and annihilation. Of thousands, only thirty birds arrive. When at last they see the Simorgh, they see themselves: they are *si morgh*, 'thirty birds'.

### The poem

> مجمعی کردند مرغان جهان &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; آنچه بودند آشکارا و نهان  
> جمله گفتند این زمان در دور کار &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; نیست خالی هیچ شهر از شهریار  
> چون بود کاقلیم ما را شاه نیست &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; بیش ازین بی‌شاه بودن راه نیست  
> یکدگر را شاید ار یاری کنیم &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; پادشاهی را طلبکاری کنیم  
> زانکه چون کشور بود بی‌پادشاه &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; نظم و ترتیبی نماند در سپاه  
> پس همه با جایگاهی آمدند &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; سر به سر جویای شاهی آمدند  
> هدهد آشفته‌دل پرانتظار &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; در میان جمع آمد بی‌قرار  
> حله‌ای بود از طریقت در برش &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; افسری بود از حقیقت بر سرش  
> تیزوهمی بود در راه آمده &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; از بد و از نیک آگاه آمده  
> گفت ای مرغان منم بی‌هیچ ریب &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; هم برید حضرت و هم پیک غیب  
> هست ما را پادشاهی بی‌خلاف &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; در پس کوهی که هست آن کوه قاف  
> نام او سیمرغ سلطان طیور &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; او به ما نزدیک و ما زو دور دور

  یکدگر *yekdegar* one another – یکدیگر · شاید *shāyad* it is fitting (the old meaning; today 'perhaps') · ار *ar* if – short for اگر · یاری کردن *yāri kardan* to help · پادشاهی *pādeshāhi* a king (پادشاه + ـی) · طلبکاری *talabkāri* seeking, search (today طلبکار means 'creditor')

  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  *goft ey morghān manam bi-hich reyb / ham barid-e hazrat o ham peyk-e gheyb* — He said: 'Birds, without any doubt I am / the courier of the Presence and the messenger of the Unseen.'  
  _Here the hoopoe goes on for several couplets about his service as Solomon's messenger; they are not included on this page. The next two couplets are where he names the king._
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  منم *manam* I am – من + ـم · بی‌هیچ ریب *bi-hich reyb* without any doubt (ریب, Arabic 'doubt') · برید *barid* courier, royal messenger · حضرت *hazrat* the Presence – the court of God (today a title of respect) · پیک *peyk* messenger · غیب *gheyb* the Unseen

  </details>

  *hast mā rā pādeshāhi bi-khelāf / dar pas-e kuhi ke hast ān kuh-e ghāf* — 'We do have a king, beyond all dispute; / he lives behind a mountain, and that mountain is Qaf.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  ما را … هست *mā rā … hast* we have (lit. 'to us there is') · بی‌خلاف *bi-khelāf* beyond dispute, without doubt · در پس *dar pas-e* behind · کوه قاف *kuh-e ghāf* Mount Qaf, the mountain that rings the world

  </details>
- نام او سیمرغ سلطان طیور / او به ما نزدیک و ما زو دور دور  
  *nām-e u simorgh soltān-e toyur / u be mā nazdik o mā zu dur dur* — His name is Simorgh, the sultan of the birds; / he is close to us, and we are far, far from him.'  
  _«او به ما نزدیک و ما زو دور دور» is often quoted on its own: God is near to us, but we are far from him._
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  سیمرغ *simorgh* the Simorgh, the great mythical bird of Iranian legend · سلطان *soltān* sultan, king · طیور *toyur* birds (Arabic plural of طیر) · نزدیک *nazdik* near · زو *zu* from him – از او

  </details>

### Reading the poem

**The story.** The opening is a little political comedy – a parliament of birds complaining that every city has a king except theirs – and then the hoopoe steps forward. In the Quran the hoopoe is Solomon's messenger to the Queen of Sheba; here it becomes the spiritual guide, wearing the robe of the Path and the crown of the Truth, and it knows the way. The king it describes is the opposite of an earthly king: hidden behind the mountain at the end of the world, and so close that all the distance is on the birds' side. The birds think they are looking for a ruler. The end of the poem shows that they were looking for themselves.

**The rhythm.** Attar wrote in *ramal*, the metre Rumi later took for the *Masnavi*: *DUM-da-DUM-DUM DUM-da-DUM-DUM DUM-da-DUM*. Listen to *maj-ma-'i kar-dand mor-ghā-ne ja-hān*. Each couplet rhymes on its own – جهان (*jahān*) / نهان (*nahān*), کار (*kār*) / شهریار (*shahriyār*), برش (*bar-ash*) / سرش (*sar-ash*) – and several repeat a word after the rhyme, like نیست (*nist*), کنیم (*konim*) and آمدند (*āmadand*).

**The grammar.** Two old uses of را (*rā*) are worth learning: ما را شاه نیست (*mā rā shāh nist*) 'we have no king' and هست ما را پادشاهی (*hast mā rā pādeshāhi*) 'we have a king'. Here را (*rā*) means 'to, for': 'to us there is a king'. شاید (*shāyad*) means 'it is fitting', not 'perhaps'; ار (*ar*) is اگر (*agar*); زانکه (*zān-ke*) is 'because'; زو (*zu*) is از او (*az u*). Couplet 3 in modern prose: چطور ممکن است که سرزمین ما شاه نداشته باشد؟ بیش از این نمی‌شود بی‌شاه ماند (*chetowr momken ast ke sarzamin-e mā shāh nadāshte bāshad? bish az in nemi-shavad bi-shāh mānd*).

**Using it.** The Simorgh is an old Iranian bird – in the *Shahnameh* it raises the hero Zal in its nest on Mount Alborz – and Attar's pun سی مرغ (*si morgh*) / سیمرغ (*simorgh*) is one of the most famous in the language. Iranians quote او به ما نزدیک و ما زو دور دور (*u be mā nazdik o mā zu dur dur*) about God, or about anyone who is close to us while we keep our distance. And a couplet attributed to Rumi sums up Attar's fame: هفت شهر عشق را عطار گشت / ما هنوز اندر خم یک کوچه‌ایم (*haft shahr-e 'eshgh rā 'attār gasht / mā hanuz andar kham-e yek kuche-im*) – 'Attar has travelled the seven cities of love; we are still at the bend of the first lane.'
