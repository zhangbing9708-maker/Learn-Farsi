# The eagle
## عقاب
Naser Khosrow (ناصر خسرو), Divan, 11th century. [Listen on the website](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/poetry/naser-khosrow-oghab.html)

Naser Khosrow (1004–about 1088) was a government official in Merv until, at forty, a dream told him to give up wine and look for wisdom. He set out on a seven-year journey to Mecca and Cairo, described in his *Safarnameh* ('Travelogue'), and came home a missionary of the Ismaili Fatimid caliphs. Persecuted for his faith, he spent his last years in exile in the valley of Yamgan, in the mountains of Badakhshan, writing stern, intelligent poems about wisdom and the soul. This short poem is the best known of them: a proud eagle boasts that the whole world lies beneath its wings, until an arrow fletched with eagle feathers brings it down. Its last words have become a proverb.

### The poem

> روزی ز سر سنگ عقابی به هوا خاست &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; وندر طلب طعمه پر و بال بیاراست  
> بر راستی بال نظر کرد و چنین گفت &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; امروز همه روی جهان زیر پر ماست  
> بر اوج چو پرواز کنم از نظر تیز &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; می‌بینم اگر ذره‌ای اندر ته دریاست  
> گر بر سر خاشاک یکی پشه بجنبد &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; جنبیدن آن پشه عیان در نظر ماست  
> بسیار منی کرد و ز تقدیر نترسید &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; بنگر که از این چرخ جفاپیشه چه برخاست  
> ناگه ز کمینگاه یکی سخت کمانی &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; تیری ز قضا و قدر انداخت بر او راست  
> بر بال عقاب آمد آن تیر جگردوز &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; وز ابر مر او را به سوی خاک فروکاست  
> بر خاک بیفتاد و بغلتید چو ماهی &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; وانگاه پر خویش گشاد از چپ و از راست  
> گفتا عجب است این که ز چوبی و ز آهن &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; این تیزی و تندی و پریدن ز کجا خاست  
> چون نیک نظر کرد پر خویش در او دید &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; گفتا ز که نالیم که از ماست که بر ماست  

### Line by line

- روزی ز سر سنگ عقابی به هوا خاست / وندر طلب طعمه پر و بال بیاراست  
  *ruzi ze sar-e sang 'oghābi be havā khāst / vandar talab-e to'me par o bāl biyārāst* — One day an eagle rose into the air from the top of a rock, / and spread out its wings and feathers in search of prey.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  سنگ *sang* stone, rock · عقاب *'oghāb* eagle · به هوا خاستن *be havā khāstan* to rise into the air · وندر *vandar* and in – و + اندر · طلب *talab* search, seeking · طعمه *to'me* prey, bait · بیاراست *biyārāst* arranged, adorned (آراستن)

  </details>
- بر راستی بال نظر کرد و چنین گفت / امروز همه روی جهان زیر پر ماست  
  *bar rāsti-ye bāl nazar kard o chonin goft / emruz hame ru-ye jahān zir-e par-e māst* — It looked at how straight its wings were, and said: / 'Today the whole face of the world lies under my wings.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  راستی *rāsti* straightness; truth · نظر کردن *nazar kardan* to look · چنین *chonin* thus, like this · روی جهان *ru-ye jahān* the face of the world · زیر پر *zir-e par* under the wing · ماست *māst* is ours – ما + است (the eagle says 'we')

  </details>
- بر اوج چو پرواز کنم از نظر تیز / می‌بینم اگر ذره‌ای اندر ته دریاست  
  *bar owj cho parvāz konam az nazar-e tiz / mi-binam agar zarre-i andar tah-e daryāst* — When I fly up to the heights, my sharp eyes / can see a speck of dust at the bottom of the sea.  
  _Some editions read «تک دریاست» *tak-e daryāst*, with the same meaning._
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  اوج *owj* peak, the heights · چو *cho* when – چون · نظر تیز *nazar-e tiz* sharp sight · ذره *zarre* speck, particle · اندر *andar* in – the old form of در · ته *tah* bottom

  </details>
- گر بر سر خاشاک یکی پشه بجنبد / جنبیدن آن پشه عیان در نظر ماست  
  *gar bar sar-e khāshāk yeki pashe bejonbad / jonbidan-e ān pashe 'ayān dar nazar-e māst* — If a gnat stirs on a blade of straw, / I can see that gnat's stirring plainly.'
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  خاشاک *khāshāk* straw, twigs, rubbish · یکی *yeki* a, one · پشه *pashe* gnat, mosquito · بجنبد *bejonbad* moves, stirs (جنبیدن) · عیان *'ayān* clear, visible

  </details>
- بسیار منی کرد و ز تقدیر نترسید / بنگر که از این چرخ جفاپیشه چه برخاست  
  *besyār mani kard o ze taghdir natarsid / bengar ke az in charkh-e jafā-pishe che barkhāst* — It boasted a great deal and did not fear fate – / now see what came of this cruel turning sky.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  منی کردن *mani kardan* to boast, to be full of oneself (من 'I' + ـی) · تقدیر *taghdir* fate, destiny · بنگر *bengar* look!, see! · چرخ *charkh* the wheel – the sky, fate · جفاپیشه *jafā-pishe* cruel by trade, always unjust · برخاستن *barkhāstan* to rise; to come of, to result

  </details>
- ناگه ز کمینگاه یکی سخت کمانی / تیری ز قضا و قدر انداخت بر او راست  
  *nāgah ze kamingāh yeki sakht kamāni / tiri ze ghazā vo ghadar andākht bar u rāst* — Suddenly, from his hiding place, an archer with a strong bow / shot an arrow of fate straight at it.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  ناگه *nāgah* suddenly – ناگهان · کمینگاه *kamingāh* ambush, hiding place · سخت کمان *sakht kamān* with a strong bow – a strong archer · قضا و قدر *ghazā vo ghadar* fate and destiny · انداختن *andākhtan* to throw, to shoot · راست *rāst* straight

  </details>
- بر بال عقاب آمد آن تیر جگردوز / وز ابر مر او را به سوی خاک فروکاست  
  *bar bāl-e 'oghāb āmad ān tir-e jegar-duz / vaz abr mar u rā be su-ye khāk forukāst* — That piercing arrow struck the eagle's wing / and brought it down from the clouds to the earth.  
  _Some editions read «جگرسوز» *jegar-suz* 'heart-burning'._
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  جگردوز *jegar-duz* liver-piercing – deadly (جگر 'liver', the seat of feeling) · وز *vaz* and from – و + از · ابر *abr* cloud · مر او را *mar u rā* it (as object) – مر … را is an old way of marking the object · به سوی *be su-ye* towards · فروکاستن *forukāstan* to bring down, to lower

  </details>
- بر خاک بیفتاد و بغلتید چو ماهی / وانگاه پر خویش گشاد از چپ و از راست  
  *bar khāk biyoftād o beghaltid cho māhi / vāngāh par-e khish goshād az chap o az rāst* — It fell to the ground and flapped about like a fish, / and then spread its wings to the left and to the right.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  بیفتاد *biyoftād* fell – old ب- + افتاد · بغلتید *beghaltid* rolled about (غلتیدن) · چو ماهی *cho māhi* like a fish (out of water) · وانگاه *vāngāh* and then – و + آنگاه · گشاد *goshād* opened, spread (گشادن = گشودن) · چپ و راست *chap o rāst* left and right

  </details>
- گفتا عجب است این که ز چوبی و ز آهن / این تیزی و تندی و پریدن ز کجا خاست  
  *goftā 'ajab ast in ke ze chubi vo ze āhan / in tizi vo tondi vo paridan ze kojā khāst* — It said: 'How strange – a piece of wood and a bit of iron: / where did they get this sharpness, this speed, this flight?'
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  گفتا *goftā* he said – گفت, with ـا added for the metre · عجب *'ajab* strange, a wonder · چوب *chub* wood · آهن *āhan* iron · تیزی *tizi* sharpness · تندی *tondi* speed · پریدن *paridan* flying

  </details>
- چون نیک نظر کرد پر خویش در او دید / گفتا ز که نالیم که از ماست که بر ماست  
  *chon nik nazar kard par-e khish dar u did / goftā ze ke nālim ke az māst ke bar māst* — When it looked closely, it saw its own feathers on the arrow. / It said: 'Whom can we blame? What has struck us came from us.'  
  _Some editions read the first half «زی تیر نگه کرد و پر خویش بر او دید» 'it looked at the arrow and saw its own feathers on it'._
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  نیک *nik* well, closely · پر خویش *par-e khish* its own feathers · ز که *ze ke* of whom – از که · نالیدن از *nālidan az* to complain about · از ماست *az māst* is from us · بر ماست *bar māst* is upon us, falls on us

  </details>

### Reading the poem

**The story.** It is a fable, told in ten quick couplets. The eagle has everything: height, speed, eyes that can see a gnat move on a straw. Its fault is منی (*mani*) – 'I-ness', pride – and its pride makes it forget fate. The arrow comes from nowhere, from a hidden archer, and the fallen eagle is puzzled: how can a stick of wood and a bit of iron fly so fast? Then it sees the answer: the arrow is fletched with eagle feathers. Aesop tells the same fable ('The Eagle and the Arrow'); Naser Khosrow gives it a sharper moral. Our worst wounds are made with our own feathers: what we suffer, we have usually brought on ourselves.

**The rhythm.** The metre is a long, heavy *hazaj*, *DUM-DUM-da da-DUM-DUM-da da-DUM-DUM-da da-DUM-DUM*, which suits a story that swoops and falls: *ru-zi ze sa-re san-ge 'o-ghā-bi be ha-vā khāst*. All the couplets rhyme on ـاست (*-āst*) – خاست (*khāst*), بیاراست (*biyārāst*), ماست (*māst*), دریاست (*daryāst*), برخاست (*barkhāst*), راست (*rāst*), فروکاست (*forukāst*) – and the last line rhymes twice, از ماست که بر ماست (*az māst ke bar māst*), to drive the point home.

**The grammar.** The poem is full of the prefix ب (*be-*) on past verbs, which modern Persian has dropped: بیاراست (*biyārāst*) 'arranged', بیفتاد (*biyoftād*) 'fell', بغلتید (*beghaltid*) 'rolled'. گفتا (*goftā*) is simply گفت (*goft*) with an extra ا (*-ā*) for the metre, وندر (*vandar*) is و اندر (*va andar*) 'and in', and مر او را (*mar u rā*) is an old way of saying او را (*u rā*). The last couplet in modern prose: وقتی خوب نگاه کرد، پر خودش را روی تیر دید و گفت: از چه کسی شکایت کنیم؟ هر چه بر سر ما می‌آید از خود ماست (*vaghti khub negāh kard, par-e khod-ash rā ru-ye tir did va goft: az che kasi shekāyat konim? har che bar sar-e mā mi-āyad az khod-e māst*).

**Using it.** از ماست که بر ماست (*az māst ke bar māst*) is one of the commonest proverbs in Persian. Iranians say it about a country's troubles, a team's defeat, a family quarrel – any time the blame lies at home. Many people learned the whole poem at school and can still recite it from the first line to the last.
