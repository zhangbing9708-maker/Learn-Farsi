# The scent of the Muliyan
## بوی جوی مولیان
Rudaki (رودکی), Divan (fragments), 10th century. [Listen on the website](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/poetry/rudaki-bu-ye-juy-e-muliyan.html)

Rudaki (died 941) is called the father of Persian poetry: he served the Samanid court in Bukhara, where the new Persian literature was born. Only scattered pieces of his work survive, and this poem survives because of a story. Nezami Aruzi tells it in his *Chahar Maqale* ('Four Discourses', about 1156). The Samanid emir Nasr ibn Ahmad went to Herat one summer and liked it so much – the vineyards, the fruit, the mild seasons – that he stayed for four years. His officers, homesick for Bukhara and their families, promised Rudaki five thousand dinars if he could bring the emir home. One morning, as the emir drank his wine, Rudaki took up his harp and sang this poem. The emir leapt onto his horse without his riding boots and set off for Bukhara; the boots were sent after him.

### The poem

> بوی جوی مولیان آید همی &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; یاد یار مهربان آید همی  
> ریگ آموی و درشتی راه او &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; زیر پایم پرنیان آید همی  
> آب جیحون از نشاط روی دوست &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; خنگ ما را تا میان آید همی  
> ای بخارا شاد باش و دیر زی &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; میر زی تو شادمان آید همی  
> میر ماه است و بخارا آسمان &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; ماه سوی آسمان آید همی  
> میر سرو است و بخارا بوستان &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; سرو سوی بوستان آید همی  

### Line by line

- بوی جوی مولیان آید همی / یاد یار مهربان آید همی  
  *bu-ye ju-ye muliyān āyad hami / yād-e yār-e mehrabān āyad hami* — The scent of the Muliyan stream is coming to me, / the memory of a dear friend is coming to me.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  بو *bu* scent, smell · جوی *juy* stream, channel · مولیان *muliyān* the Muliyan, a canal in Bukhara lined with the royal gardens · آید همی *āyad hami* is coming – همی after the verb = می‌آید · یاد *yād* memory · مهربان *mehrabān* kind, loving

  </details>
- ریگ آموی و درشتی راه او / زیر پایم پرنیان آید همی  
  *rig-e āmuy o doroshti-ye rāh-e u / zir-e pā-yam parniyān āyad hami* — The sand of Amuy and the roughness of its road / feel like silk beneath my feet.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  ریگ *rig* sand · آموی *āmuy* Amuy (Amul), the crossing of the Oxus on the road to Bukhara · درشتی *doroshti* roughness, harshness · زیر پایم *zir-e pā-yam* under my feet · پرنیان *parniyān* fine silk

  </details>
- آب جیحون از نشاط روی دوست / خنگ ما را تا میان آید همی  
  *āb-e jeyhun az neshāt-e ru-ye dust / kheng-e mā rā tā miyān āyad hami* — For joy at a friend's face, the waters of the Oxus / come only up to our white horse's girth.  
  Literally: the water of the Jeyhun, from the joy of the friend's face, comes to our white horse up to the waist  
  _The great river seems shallow – or the horse eager – because the rider is going home._
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  جیحون *jeyhun* the Oxus, the Amu Darya · نشاط *neshāt* joy, high spirits · روی *ru* face · خنگ *kheng* a white or grey horse · تا میان *tā miyān* up to the waist

  </details>
- ای بخارا شاد باش و دیر زی / میر زی تو شادمان آید همی  
  *ey bokhārā shād bāsh o dir zi / mir zi to shādmān āyad hami* — O Bukhara, rejoice and live long! / Your prince is coming to you in joy.  
  _زی appears twice with two meanings: 'live!' in the first half and 'towards' in the second._
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  شاد باش *shād bāsh* be glad, rejoice · دیر زی *dir zi* live long – زی is the imperative of زیستن · میر *mir* prince, emir – short for امیر · زی *zi* towards, to (an old preposition) · شادمان *shādmān* joyful

  </details>
- میر ماه است و بخارا آسمان / ماه سوی آسمان آید همی  
  *mir māh ast o bokhārā āsemān / māh su-ye āsemān āyad hami* — The prince is the moon and Bukhara the sky: / the moon is coming back to the sky.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  میر *mir* prince, emir · ماه *māh* moon · آسمان *āsemān* sky · سوی *su-ye* towards

  </details>
- میر سرو است و بخارا بوستان / سرو سوی بوستان آید همی  
  *mir sarv ast o bokhārā bustān / sarv su-ye bustān āyad hami* — The prince is a cypress and Bukhara the garden: / the cypress is coming home to the garden.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  سرو *sarv* cypress – the tall, straight tree of Persian gardens and poems · بوستان *bustān* garden, orchard · سوی *su-ye* towards

  </details>

### Reading the poem

**The images.** Rudaki does not argue; he paints home. The poem begins with a scent – the water and gardens of the Muliyan in Bukhara – and with the memory of a friend. Then the road itself changes: the hard sand of the Oxus crossing feels like silk, and the great river comes only up to the horse's girth. In the last three couplets he turns to the city and its prince: Bukhara is told to rejoice, because its moon is coming back to its sky and its cypress to its garden. The emir is flattered, but the real subject of the poem is longing for home – which is exactly why it worked, on the homesick officers and on the emir alike.

**The rhythm.** The poem is in *ramal*, the metre Rumi later used for the *Masnavi*: *DUM-da-DUM-DUM DUM-da-DUM-DUM DUM-da-DUM*, as in *bu-ye ju-ye mu-li-yān ā-yad ha-mi*. Every couplet ends with the same two words, آید همی (*āyad hami*) 'is coming', a refrain (a ردیف (*radif*)) that sounds like hoofbeats on the road home. Before it comes the rhyme: مولیان (*muliyān*), مهربان (*mehrabān*), پرنیان (*parniyān*), میان (*miyān*), شادمان (*shādmān*), آسمان (*āsemān*), بوستان (*bustān*).

**The grammar.** This is the Persian of the tenth century, and almost every word is still in use. Two things are old: همی (*hami*) comes after the verb instead of می (*mi-*) before it – آید همی (*āyad hami*) is today's می‌آید (*mi-āyad*) – and زی (*zi*) has two meanings, 'live!' (from زیستن (*zistan*)) and 'towards'. میر (*mir*) is short for امیر (*amir*). The fourth couplet in modern prose: ای بخارا، شاد باش و عمرت دراز باد؛ امیر شادمان به سوی تو می‌آید (*ey bokhārā, shād bāsh va 'omr-at derāz bād; amir shādmān be su-ye to mi-āyad*).

**Using it.** بوی جوی مولیان آید همی (*bu-ye ju-ye muliyān āyad hami*) is quoted by Iranians, Afghans and Tajiks living far from home: it belongs to the whole Persian-speaking world, and Rudaki, born near Panjakent, is a national hero in Tajikistan. The story is also a favourite example of the power of poetry: the right words, at the right moment, can move a king.
