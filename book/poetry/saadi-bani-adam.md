# Children of Adam
## بنی‌آدم
Saadi (سعدی), Golestan, 13th century. [Listen on the website](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/poetry/saadi-bani-adam.html)

Saadi of Shiraz finished the *Golestan* ('The Rose Garden'), a book of short stories in prose mixed with verse, in 1258. In the tenth story of the first chapter, *On the conduct of kings*, he is praying at the tomb of John the Baptist in the Great Mosque of Damascus when a king known for his injustice asks for his prayers. Saadi tells him to be merciful to the weak, and ends with these six lines. They are probably the best-known Persian poem in the world: Iranian children learn them at school, people quote them after every earthquake and flood, and they are woven into a carpet at the United Nations in New York.

### The poem

> بنی‌آدم اعضای یکدیگرند &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; که در آفرینش ز یک گوهرند  
> چو عضوی به درد آورد روزگار &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; دگر عضوها را نماند قرار  
> تو کز محنت دیگران بی‌غمی &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; نشاید که نامت نهند آدمی  

### Line by line

- بنی‌آدم اعضای یکدیگرند / که در آفرینش ز یک گوهرند  
  *bani-ādam a'zā-ye yekdigar-and / ke dar āfarinesh ze yek gowhar-and* — The children of Adam are limbs of one another, / for in creation they are made of one essence.  
  _Many people quote the first half as «بنی‌آدم اعضای یک پیکرند» *bani-ādam a'zā-ye yek peykar-and* – 'are limbs of one body'. The standard editions of the *Golestan* have یکدیگرند 'of one another'._
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  بنی‌آدم *bani-ādam* the children of Adam, humankind (Arabic بنی 'sons of') · اعضا *a'zā* limbs, members (Arabic plural of عضو) · یکدیگر *yekdigar* one another · آفرینش *āfarinesh* creation (from آفریدن 'to create') · ز *ze* from, of – the poetic short form of از · گوهر *gowhar* jewel; essence, substance

  </details>
- چو عضوی به درد آورد روزگار / دگر عضوها را نماند قرار  
  *cho 'ozvi be dard āvarad ruzgār / degar 'ozv-hā rā namānad gharār* — When the world brings pain to one limb, / the other limbs can find no rest.
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  چو *cho* when; like – poetic form of چون · عضوی *'ozvi* a limb, one limb · به درد آوردن *be dard āvardan* to bring to pain, to hurt · روزگار *ruzgār* time, the world, fate · دگر *degar* other – poetic form of دیگر · نماند *namānad* does not remain (from ماندن) · قرار *gharār* rest, calm, stillness

  </details>
- تو کز محنت دیگران بی‌غمی / نشاید که نامت نهند آدمی  
  *to kaz mehnat-e digarān bi-ghami / nashāyad ke nām-at nahand ādami* — You who feel no grief at the suffering of others / do not deserve to be called human.  
  Literally: you who from the suffering of others are without-sorrow, it is not fitting that they put your name 'human'
  <details><summary>Word by word</summary>

  کز *kaz* who … from – که + از run together · محنت *mehnat* hardship, suffering · بی‌غم *bi-gham* without sorrow, carefree; بی‌غمی 'you are carefree' · نشاید *nashāyad* it is not fitting (from the old verb شایستن) · نام نهادن *nām nahādan* to give a name, to call · آدمی *ādami* a human being

  </details>

### Reading the poem

**The image.** Saadi takes a picture everyone understands – the body – and makes it a rule of conduct. When one finger is burnt, the whole body feels it and cannot sleep; in the same way, one person's pain should trouble everyone. The image echoes a well-known saying attributed to the Prophet Muhammad, that believers are like one body, and Saadi widens it from believers to all the children of Adam.

**The rhythm.** The lines are in *motaqāreb*, the metre of Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh* and of Saadi's own *Bustan*: four beats to a half-line, *da-DUM-DUM da-DUM-DUM da-DUM-DUM da-DUM*. Read the first half-line slowly: *ba-ni ā-dam a'-zā-ye yek-di-ga-rand*. Each couplet has its own rhyme – یکدیگرند (*yekdigar-and*) / گوهرند (*gowhar-and*), روزگار (*ruzgār*) / قرار (*gharār*), بی‌غمی (*bi-ghami*) / آدمی (*ādami*).

**The grammar.** Classical poetry uses short forms you will now recognise everywhere: ز (*ze*) for از (*az*), چو (*cho*) for چون (*chon*), دگر (*degar*) for دیگر (*digar*), کز (*kaz*) for که از (*ke az*). The verbs آورد (*āvarad*) and نماند (*namānad*) have no می (*mi*): in the classical language this simple present states a general truth – 'whenever the world hurts one limb, the others do not stay at rest'. In modern prose you would say وقتی روزگار عضوی را به درد می‌آورد، عضوهای دیگر آرام نمی‌مانند (*vaghti ruzgār 'ozvi rā be dard mi-āvarad, 'ozv-hā-ye digar ārām nemi-mānand*).

**Using it.** Iranians quote the first line, or just the words *bani-ādam*, to say that a disaster far away concerns us all. The last couplet is the sharp one: it is quoted at people who shrug at others' suffering.
