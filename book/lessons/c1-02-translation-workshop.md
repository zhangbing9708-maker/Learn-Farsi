# Translation workshop
## کارگاه ترجمه
*kārgāh-e tarjome* — Advanced (C1), lesson 2. [Open this lesson with audio and interactive exercises](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/course/c1-02-translation-workshop.html)

At this level you no longer translate words; you translate sentences, registers and whole ways of seeing. This workshop reads a short history of translation into Persian, then works through ten sentences into Persian and ten into English with a commentary on every choice: word order, ezafe chains, tone, idioms that have to be rethought from scratch. It ends with seven words that refuse to be translated – and what each of them really covers.

**In this lesson you will learn to:**

- restructure English sentences into natural Persian order, including long ezafe chains and relative clauses
- choose the right register in both languages, from a formal letter to a chat with friends
- replace idioms with real equivalents instead of translating them word for word
- explain what تعارف، دلتنگی، غیرت، حال، صفا، قسمت and خسته نباشید cover, and translate them in context
- comment on a translation: say why one version is better than another

### From Kalila and Dimna to today

_A short essay on the long history of translation into Persian. Watch the verbs of translating – ترجمه کردن، برگرداندن، فراهم آوردن – and the essay's question: should a translator be faithful to the words or to the meaning?_

- ترجمه در تاریخ زبان فارسی جایگاهی ویژه دارد.  
  *tarjome dar tārikh-e zabān-e fārsi jāygāh-i vizhe dārad.* — Translation has a special place in the history of the Persian language.
- داستان‌های «کلیله و دمنه» نخست در هند به زبان سنسکریت نوشته شده بودند.  
  *dāstān-hā-ye «Kalile o Demne» nakhost dar Hend be zabān-e sanskrit neveshte shode budand.* — The stories of Kalila and Dimna were first written in India, in Sanskrit.
- در قرن ششم میلادی، پزشکی ایرانی به نام برزویه این کتاب را به پارسی میانه برگرداند.  
  *dar gharn-e sheshom-e milādi, pezeshk-i Irāni be nām-e Borzuye in ketāb rā be pārsi-ye miyāne bargardānd.* — In the sixth century AD, an Iranian physician called Borzuya put the book into Middle Persian.
- دو قرن بعد، ابن مقفع آن را از پارسی میانه به عربی ترجمه کرد.  
  *do gharn ba'd, Ebn-e Moghaffa' ān rā az pārsi-ye miyāne be arabi tarjome kard.* — Two centuries later, Ibn al-Muqaffa' translated it from Middle Persian into Arabic.
- سرانجام در قرن ششم هجری، ترجمه‌ای فارسی از متن عربی فراهم آمد که هنوز از شاهکارهای نثر فارسی است.  
  *saranjām dar gharn-e sheshom-e hejri, tarjome-i fārsi az matn-e arabi farāham āmad ke hanuz az shāhkār-hā-ye nasr-e fārsi ast.* — Finally, in the sixth century of the Hijra (the 12th century AD), a Persian translation of the Arabic text was produced that is still one of the masterpieces of Persian prose.
- این کتاب در هر ترجمه رنگ زبان تازه‌ی خود را گرفت.  
  *in ketāb dar har tarjome rang-e zabān-e tāze-ye khod rā gereft.* — With each translation the book took on the colour of its new language.
- در دوره‌ی قاجار، با تأسیس دارالفنون، ترجمه‌ی کتاب‌های اروپایی به فارسی رونق گرفت.  
  *dar dowre-ye Ghājār, bā ta'sis-e Dār-ol-fonun, tarjome-ye ketāb-hā-ye orupāyi be fārsi rownagh gereft.* — In the Qajar period, with the founding of the Dar ol-Fonun college, the translation of European books into Persian flourished.
- مترجمان آن دوره گاه واژه‌های فرانسوی را به همان شکل به فارسی آوردند و گاه برای آن‌ها معادل ساختند.  
  *motarjemān-e ān dowre gāh vāzhe-hā-ye farānsavi rā be hamān shekl be fārsi āvardand va gāh barā-ye ān-hā mo'ādel sākhtand.* — The translators of that time sometimes brought French words into Persian unchanged, and sometimes made up equivalents for them.
- امروز نیز هر مترجمی با پرسش‌هایی قدیمی روبه‌رو است.  
  *emruz niz har motarjem-i bā porsesh-hā-yi ghadimi ru-be-ru ast.* — Today, too, every translator faces some very old questions.
- آیا باید به لفظ متن وفادار ماند یا به معنای آن؟  
  *āyā bāyad be lafz-e matn vafādār mānd yā be ma'nā-ye ān?* — Should one stay faithful to the words of a text, or to its meaning?
- ترجمه‌ی تحت‌اللفظی اغلب جمله‌ای می‌سازد که درست است، اما فارسی نیست.  
  *tarjome-ye taht-ol-lafzi aghlab jomle-i mi-sāzad ke dorost ast, ammā fārsi nist.* — A literal translation often produces a sentence that is correct, but is not Persian.
- اصطلاح انگلیسی «باران گربه و سگ» برای خواننده‌ی فارسی‌زبان هیچ معنایی ندارد.  
  *estelāh-e engelisi-ye «bārān-e gorbe o sag» barā-ye khānande-ye fārsi-zabān hich ma'nā-yi na-dārad.* — The English idiom 'raining cats and dogs' means nothing at all to a Persian-speaking reader.
- مترجم باید معادلی بیابد که همان حس را برساند، مانند «باران مثل سیل می‌بارد».  
  *motarjem bāyad mo'ādel-i biyābad ke hamān hess rā beresānad, mānand-e «bārān mesl-e seyl mi-bārad».* — The translator must find an equivalent that conveys the same feeling, such as 'it is raining like a flood'.
- دشوارتر از اصطلاحات، واژه‌هایی هستند که در زبان مقصد هیچ معادلی ندارند.  
  *doshvār-tar az estelāhāt, vāzhe-hā-yi hastand ke dar zabān-e maghsad hich mo'ādel-i na-dārand.* — Harder than idioms are the words that have no equivalent at all in the target language.
- «تعارف» و «دلتنگی» از این دسته‌اند و هر کدام دنیایی از رفتار و احساس را در خود دارند.  
  *«ta'ārof» o «deltangi» az in daste-and va har kodām donyā-yi az raftār o ehsās rā dar khod dārand.* — Ta'ārof and deltangi are of this kind, and each holds a whole world of behaviour and feeling.
- مترجم خوب در چنین مواردی گاه توضیح می‌دهد، گاه واژه را بی‌ترجمه می‌گذارد و گاه کل جمله را از نو می‌سازد.  
  *motarjem-e khub dar chenin mavāred-i gāh towzih mi-dahad, gāh vāzhe rā bi-tarjome mi-gozārad va gāh koll-e jomle rā az now mi-sāzad.* — In such cases a good translator sometimes explains, sometimes leaves the word untranslated, and sometimes rebuilds the whole sentence from scratch.
- به همین دلیل گفته‌اند که ترجمه نه فقط انتقال واژه‌ها، بلکه انتقال یک فرهنگ است.  
  *be hamin dalil gofte-and ke tarjome na faghat enteghāl-e vāzhe-hā, balke enteghāl-e yek farhang ast.* — That is why it has been said that translation is the transfer not just of words but of a culture.

### What a translator actually decides

Every translation between English and Persian forces the same decisions, and this workshop names them so you can make them on purpose.

1. **Order.** Persian puts the verb last, the adjectives and possessors *after* the noun, and the time before the place. English sentences have to be taken apart and rebuilt, not converted word by word.
2. **Ezafe chains.** *The director of the Persian Language Centre of the University of Tehran* is one chain in Persian – مدیر مرکز زبان فارسی دانشگاه تهران (*modir-e markaz-e zabān-e fārsi-ye dāneshgāh-e Tehrān*) – read from the head forward. English often needs to break it up with *'s* and *of*.
3. **Register.** Persian marks politeness in the verb (شما بفرمایید (*shomā befarmāyid*) vs تو بگو (*to begu*)) and has separate written and spoken forms. Decide who is talking to whom before you choose a single word.
4. **Idioms.** *A piece of cake* is not a cake in Persian; it is مثل آب خوردن (*mesl-e āb khordan*, ‘like drinking water’). Find the idiom the other language would use – or say it plainly.
5. **Culture words.** Some words carry customs with them. For these, the only honest method is to understand the whole situation and translate *that*.

The translator Nasrollah Monshi, who made the classical Persian *Kalila and Dimna* in the 12th century, did all five: he kept the stories, but filled them with Persian verses and Persian rhythms. His version is read today not as a translation but as Persian literature.

### Rebuilding the sentence: order and ezafe chains

The commonest mistake in English→Persian translation is keeping English order. Persian has its own: **subject – time – manner – place – object – verb**, adjectives and possessors **after** the noun, and relative clauses introduced by ـی … که (*-i … ke*) on the noun.

Ezafe chains run the opposite way to English *'s*: the first word is the head, and each later word narrows it. Read *the University of Tehran's language centre director* backwards and you have the Persian order: مدیر مرکز زبان دانشگاه تهران (*modir-e markaz-e zabān-e dāneshgāh-e Tehrān*). When a chain gets longer than five or six links, a good translator breaks it: مدیر مرکزی که در دانشگاه تهران زبان فارسی درس می‌دهد (*modir-e markaz-i ke dar dāneshgāh-e Tehrān zabān-e fārsi dars mi-dahad*).

Some English structures have no Persian twin and must be rethought: *I like it* becomes از آن خوشم می‌آید (*az ān khosh-am mi-āyad*, ‘it pleases me’); *I have been learning for two years* becomes دو سال است که یاد می‌گیرم (*do sāl ast ke yād mi-giram*, ‘it is two years that I learn’).

| English | Persian | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| a big old house | خانه‌ی بزرگ قدیمی (*khāne-ye bozorg-e ghadimi*) | adjectives follow, joined by ezafe |
| my grandfather's house | خانه‌ی پدربزرگم (*khāne-ye pedarbozorg-am*) | the possessor follows |
| the University of Tehran's language centre | مرکز زبان دانشگاه تهران (*markaz-e zabān-e dāneshgāh-e Tehrān*) | the chain starts from the head |
| the book (that) I bought | کتابی که خریدم (*ketāb-i ke kharidam*) | ـی on the noun + که, never left out |
| Tom goes to university by metro every morning | تام هر روز صبح با مترو به دانشگاه می‌رود (*Tām har ruz sobh bā metro be dāneshgāh mi-ravad*) | time – manner – place – verb |
| I like it | از آن خوشم می‌آید (*az ān khosh-am mi-āyad*) | the thing becomes the subject |
| I have been learning for two years | دو سال است که یاد می‌گیرم (*do sāl ast ke yād mi-giram*) | 'it is two years that' + present |
| There are three reasons | سه دلیل وجود دارد (*se dalil vojud dārad*) | 'exist' instead of 'there is' |

### Register and idioms that need rethinking

**Register first.** Before translating *Could you send me the file?* ask: to whom? A professor gets ممکن است لطفاً فایل را برایم بفرستید؟ (*momken ast lotfan fāyl rā barā-yam befrestid?*); a friend gets the spoken فایلو برام بفرست (*fāylo barām befrest*). English *you* has one form; Persian has two (to / shomā), plus the plural of respect and a written–spoken split. A translation that gets every word right and the register wrong is a bad translation.

**Idioms second.** An idiom is a picture, and the picture rarely survives the journey. Look for the idiom the other language would use in the same situation; if none exists, say it plainly. Literal versions of idioms are the surest sign of an amateur translation – in both directions.

| English idiom | Persian equivalent | Literally |
| --- | --- | --- |
| It's a piece of cake. | مثل آب خوردن است. (*mesl-e āb khordan ast.*) | it's like drinking water |
| to kill two birds with one stone | با یک تیر دو نشان زدن (*bā yek tir do neshān zadan*) | to hit two targets with one arrow |
| Speak of the devil! | حلال‌زاده است! (*halāl-zāde ast!*) | he's legitimately born! |
| to pull someone's leg | سر به سر کسی گذاشتن (*sar be sar-e kas-i gozāshtan*) | to put head to someone's head |
| Prices have gone through the roof. | قیمت‌ها سر به فلک زده است. (*gheymat-hā sar be falak zade ast.*) | prices have struck their head on the heavens |
| Good luck! / Break a leg! | موفق باشی! (*movaffagh bāshi!*) | may you be successful |
| It's not my cup of tea. | به سلیقه‌ی من نیست. (*be salighe-ye man nist.*) | it's not to my taste |
| It's too late now. | کار از کار گذشته است. (*kār az kār gozashte ast.*) | the matter has gone past the matter |

- امتحان مثل آب خوردن بود.  
  *emtehān mesl-e āb khordan bud.* — The exam was a piece of cake.
- با این کار با یک تیر دو نشان زدی.  
  *bā in kār bā yek tir do neshān zadi.* — With that you killed two birds with one stone.
- حلال‌زاده است! داشتیم درباره‌ات حرف می‌زدیم.  
  *halāl-zāde ast! dāshtim darbāre-at harf mi-zadim.* — Speak of the devil! We were just talking about you.  
  Spoken: حلال‌زاده‌ست! داشتیم درباره‌ت حرف می‌زدیم. *halāl-zāde-st! dāshtim darbāre-t harf mi-zadim.*
- سر به سرم نگذار!  
  *sar be sar-am na-gozār!* — Stop pulling my leg!  
  Spoken: سر به سرم نذار! *sar be sar-am na-zār!*
- در این چند سال قیمت‌ها سر به فلک زده است.  
  *dar in chand sāl gheymat-hā sar be falak zade ast.* — Prices have gone through the roof in the last few years.
- ممکن است لطفاً در را ببندید؟  
  *momken ast lotfan dar rā bebandid?* — Would you mind closing the door?  
  _Formal register: shomā verb + لطفاً + ممکن است. To a friend: «درو می‌بندی؟»_
- با سلام و احترام،  
  *bā salām o ehterām,* — Dear Sir or Madam,  
  Literally: with greetings and respect  
  _The standard opening of a formal Persian letter or email._

### Seven words that will not translate

Each of these words has a dictionary gloss, and each gloss is wrong somewhere. Learn them as situations, not as words – and when you translate them, translate the situation.

| Word | Reading | Nearest English | What it really covers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| تعارف | ta'ārof | ritual politeness | offering what you may not mean to give, refusing what you would like to accept, insisting three times; the choreography of modesty and hospitality |
| دلتنگی | deltangi | missing, longing, homesickness | the 'tightening of the heart' for a person, a place or a time; sadder than *missing*, warmer than *melancholy* |
| غیرت | gheyrat | honour, zeal, protective pride | the urge to stand up for one's own – family, friends, country; admired as loyalty and courage, criticised as possessive jealousy |
| حال | hāl | state, mood; fun | how you are (حالت چطور است؟), the mood of a moment (حال و هوا), and in speech pure enjoyment (حال کردیم 'we had a great time') |
| صفا | safā | purity, serenity, warmth | the clean, friendly, uplifting feel of a place or a gathering; said to guests: صفا آوردید 'you have brought joy' |
| قسمت | ghesmat | fate, one's lot | what was 'allotted' to you – used for meetings, marriages, missed chances: قسمت نبود 'it wasn't meant to be' |
| خسته نباشید | khaste na-bāshid | thanks for your hard work | 'may you not be tired': said to anyone who is working or has worked – a shopkeeper, a driver, a speaker, a colleague leaving at six; the answer is سلامت باشید |

- بفرمایید، تعارف نکنید!  
  *befarmāyid, ta'ārof na-konid!* — Please, help yourself – there's no need to be polite!
- دلم برای شیراز تنگ شده است.  
  *del-am barā-ye Shirāz tang shode ast.* — I miss Shiraz.  
  Spoken: دلم برای شیراز تنگ شده. *del-am barā-ye Shirāz tang shode.*  
  Literally: my heart has become tight for Shiraz
- او با غیرت از دوستانش دفاع کرد.  
  *u bā gheyrat az dustān-ash defā' kard.* — He stood up for his friends fiercely, as a point of honour.
- حال و هوای این شهر را دوست دارم.  
  *hāl o havā-ye in shahr rā dust dāram.* — I love the feel of this city.  
  Literally: I love the state and air of this city
- خوش آمدید، صفا آوردید!  
  *khosh āmadid, safā āvardid!* — Welcome – how lovely that you've come!  
  Literally: you came well, you brought purity
- می‌خواستیم به اصفهان برویم، اما قسمت نبود.  
  *mi-khāstim be Esfahān beravim, ammā ghesmat na-bud.* — We wanted to go to Isfahan, but it wasn't meant to be.
- به راننده گفتم «خسته نباشید» و پیاده شدم.  
  *be rānande goftam «khaste na-bāshid» va piyāde shodam.* — I thanked the driver and got out.  
  _An English translation cannot keep the words; it keeps the gesture. In a novel you might write: I said the usual thank-you to the driver._

### Workshop 1: ten sentences into Persian

_Each English sentence (in the translation line) is followed by a Persian version and a commentary. Cover the Persian, try your own version, then compare – and read the note even when you got it right._

- دو سال است که فارسی یاد می‌گیرم.  
  *do sāl ast ke fārsi yād mi-giram.* — I've been learning Persian for two years.  
  _The English present perfect continuous becomes 'it is two years that' + present. «دو سال است فارسی یاد گرفته‌ام» would suggest the learning is finished._
- مردی که در خانه‌ی کناری زندگی می‌کند، مترجم است.  
  *mard-i ke dar khāne-ye kenāri zendegi mi-konad, motarjem ast.* — The man who lives next door is a translator.  
  _ـی + که on the noun; the main verb waits until the end. A comma before the main verb helps the reader of a long subject._
- به من گفتند که جلسه لغو شده است.  
  *be man goftand ke jalase laghv shode ast.* — I was told that the meeting had been cancelled.  
  _Two changes: the English passive 'I was told' becomes the impersonal 'they told me', and Persian keeps the tense of the original words (شده است, not شده بود)._
- ممکن است لطفاً فایل را تا فردا برایم بفرستید؟  
  *momken ast lotfan fāyl rā tā fardā barā-yam befrestid?* — Could you possibly send me the file by tomorrow?  
  _Register: an email to a colleague or teacher – shomā form, لطفاً, ممکن است. 'By tomorrow' is تا فردا._
- مثل آب خوردن است.  
  *mesl-e āb khordan ast.* — It's a piece of cake.  
  _Idiom for idiom. «یک تکه کیک است» would puzzle any Iranian._
- دلش برای خانه خیلی تنگ شده است.  
  *del-ash barā-ye khāne kheyli tang shode ast.* — She's very homesick.  
  _Persian has no adjective for 'homesick'; the heart becomes the subject and the feeling becomes a whole clause._
- بهتر است پیش از آن‌که باران ببارد، راه بیفتیم.  
  *behtar ast pish az ān-ke bārān bebārad, rāh biyoftim.* — We'd better leave before it rains.  
  _'We'd better' = بهتر است + subjunctive. پیش از آن‌که always takes the subjunctive, even when English uses the present._
- هر چه بیشتر بخوانی، بیشتر می‌فهمی.  
  *har che bish-tar bekhāni, bish-tar mi-fahmi.* — The more you read, the more you understand.  
  _The pattern هر چه … ‑تر …، ‑تر …. Generic 'you' is the to-form, as in English._
- از مهمان‌نوازی شما سپاسگزارم.  
  *az mehmān-navāzi-ye shomā sepāsgozār-am.* — Thank you for your hospitality.  
  _Formal, for a letter or a speech. Face to face most people would say «دستتان درد نکند» or «خیلی زحمت کشیدید»._
- جسارت نباشد، ولی فکر می‌کنم اشتباه می‌کنید.  
  *jesārat na-bāshad, vali fekr mi-konam eshtebāh mi-konid.* — No offence, but I think you're wrong.  
  _جسارت نباشد 'may it not be impudence' is the polite cushion before disagreeing with someone older or senior._

### Workshop 2: ten sentences into English

_Now the other direction. Each Persian sentence has a literal reading and a translation that an English speaker would actually say. The commentary explains the jump from one to the other._

- دستت درد نکند.  
  *dast-at dard na-konad.* — Thanks so much (for doing that).  
  Literally: may your hand not hurt  
  _Thanks for an action – a meal, a favour, a lift. 'Thanks for all your trouble' often fits better than a bare 'thank you'._
- قدمتان روی چشم.  
  *ghadam-etān ru-ye cheshm.* — You're most welcome here.  
  Literally: your step (is) on my eye  
  _A host's welcome. Any image of eyes in English would sound strange; translate the warmth._
- چشمم آب نمی‌خورد که بیاید.  
  *cheshm-am āb nemi-khorad ke biyāyad.* — I doubt he'll come.  
  Literally: my eye doesn't drink water that he will come  
  _An idiom of doubt. English needs only a plain verb – or 'I wouldn't bet on him coming'._
- کار از کار گذشته است.  
  *kār az kār gozashte ast.* — It's too late now.  
  Literally: the work has passed from the work  
  _Also 'what's done is done' – choose by context._
- هر چه شما بفرمایید.  
  *har che shomā befarmāyid.* — Whatever you think best.  
  Literally: whatever you command  
  _Ta'ārof: politeness, not obedience. In English 'as you wish' can sound cold; 'whatever suits you' is closer._
- بی‌زحمت نمک را بدهید.  
  *bi-zahmat namak rā bedahid.* — Could you pass the salt, please?  
  Literally: without trouble, give the salt  
  _Persian softens with بی‌زحمت and an imperative; English softens with a question._
- مادربزرگم سال گذشته به رحمت خدا رفت.  
  *mādarbozorg-am sāl-e gozashte be rahmat-e khodā raft.* — My grandmother passed away last year.  
  Literally: went to God's mercy  
  _A respectful euphemism needs a respectful euphemism – not 'died', and not a word-for-word religious phrase._
- این‌جا خیلی صفا دارد.  
  *in-jā kheyli safā dārad.* — It's really lovely here.  
  Literally: here has much purity  
  _صفا is the feel of a place: peaceful, fresh, friendly. 'Lovely', 'such a nice atmosphere'._
- قسمت این بود که همدیگر را در تهران ببینیم.  
  *ghesmat in bud ke hamdigar rā dar Tehrān bebinim.* — We were meant to meet in Tehran.  
  Literally: the lot was this, that we see each other in Tehran  
  _'Fate' is too heavy for most contexts; 'meant to', 'it was written' (jokingly) are closer._
- خدا بد ندهد!  
  *khodā bad na-dahad!* — Oh no – I hope it's nothing serious!  
  Literally: may God not give bad  
  _Said on hearing that someone is ill or has had an accident. Translate the concern, not the blessing._

### A country of translators

Iran reads the world in translation. Walk down Enghelab Street in Tehran, opposite the university, and the bookshops are full of Persian versions of Tolstoy, Dostoevsky, Hemingway, Márquez and Kundera – often in several competing translations, each with its loyal readers. Well-known translators are public figures, and readers will tell you *whose* translation of a novel to buy before they tell you anything about the novel.

The traffic also runs the other way. The most famous translation from Persian is Edward FitzGerald's *Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám* (1859) – so free that scholars still argue whether it is a translation or a new English poem inspired by Khayyam. FitzGerald took quatrains apart, combined them and invented lines, and yet his version made Khayyam one of the best-known poets in the English-speaking world. It is the perfect case study for this lesson's question: لفظ یا معنا؟ (*lafz yā ma'nā?*, ‘the words or the meaning?’)

Iranian cinema and television add a third tradition: **dubbing** (دوبله (*dubleh*)). For decades foreign films have been dubbed into Persian by star voice actors, and a good dubbing team rewrites jokes, idioms and insults so that they land in Persian – a daily, very public lesson in translating the situation rather than the words.

### Tips

- Read your Persian translation aloud without looking at the English. If it sounds like a translation, rebuild it: move the verb, split a long clause, break the ezafe chain.
- Watch the false friends of structure: «توسط» for every 'by', «این است که» for 'the thing is', «در حال … بودن» for every -ing. Each is possible; none should be automatic.
- When you meet a culture word (تعارف، قسمت، صفا) in a text, ask three questions: who says it, to whom, and what would an English speaker say in the same moment?
- Keep a two-column notebook of idioms you meet – Persian on one side, the real English equivalent (not the literal one) on the other.
- For formal English → formal Persian, a reliable recipe: shomā verbs, لطفاً or خواهشمند است, a passive or impersonal where English has 'we'.
- Translating Persian into English, cut the blessings and courtesies that English does not use – but keep the warmth they carry somewhere in the sentence.

### Into English: choose the best version

**Choose the right answer**

1. چشمم آب نمی‌خورد که قبول کند. (*cheshm-am āb nemi-khorad ke ghabul konad.*) — My eye doesn't drink water that he accepts. / I doubt he'll agree. / I can't see him accepting water.
2. صفا آوردید! (*safā āvardid!*) – said by a host at the door. — You brought purity! / You've cleaned the house! / How lovely to see you – welcome!
3. خسته نباشید! (*khaste na-bāshid!*) – said to a teacher at the end of a class. — Thank you – that was a great class. / Don't be tired! / You look tired.
4. کار از کار گذشته است. (*kār az kār gozashte ast.*) — The work is finished. / It's too late now. / He has left his job.
5. بی‌زحمت در را ببندید. (*bi-zahmat dar rā bebandid.*) — Close the door without trouble. / Close the door! / Would you mind closing the door?
6. Which word is the best one-word English gloss for دلتنگی (*deltangi*) in دلتنگی شب‌های غربت (*deltangi-ye shab-hā-ye ghorbat*)? — homesickness / narrowness / boredom

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. I doubt he'll agree. — An idiom of doubt: translate the meaning, not the eye or the water.
2. How lovely to see you – welcome! — A warm formula of welcome; English expresses it with 'lovely to see you'.
3. Thank you – that was a great class. — It acknowledges someone's work. English thanks them for it.
4. It's too late now. — Nothing can be changed any more: 'it's too late', 'what's done is done'.
5. Would you mind closing the door? — بی‌زحمت softens the imperative; English softens with a question.
6. homesickness — غربت is being far from home, so here دلتنگی is homesickness. In other contexts it is 'missing someone' or 'longing'.

</details>
