# How Persian is written
## خط فارسی چگونه نوشته می‌شود؟
*khatt-e fārsi chegune neveshte mi-shavad?* — The script, lesson 1. [Open this lesson with audio and interactive exercises](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/course/script-01-how-persian-is-written.html)

Before you learn a single letter, take ten minutes to see how the whole system works. Persian writing looks mysterious from the outside, but it rests on a handful of simple rules – and once you know them, the 32 letters fall into place in a week.

**In this lesson you will learn to:**

- read from right to left and know where a word begins
- explain why most letters have several shapes
- know which vowels are written and which are left out
- recognise the Persian alphabet as a cousin of the Arabic one, with four extra letters

### Right to left

Persian is written and read from **right to left**. A book opens from what an English reader would call the back, and your eye starts at the right-hand edge of every line. Only numbers run the other way: in ۱۳۸۵ (*hezār-o-sisad-o-hashtād-o-panj*, ‘1385’) the digits are read left to right, exactly as in English.

There are **no capital letters**. Names, the first word of a sentence and the word for *God* all start with the same small shapes. Sentences end with a full stop, and questions with a mirrored question mark: ؟ (*?*).

### Letters that hold hands

Persian is a *cursive* script: inside a word most letters join the letter that follows them, even in print. Because a letter has to connect on different sides depending on where it stands, it can take up to **four shapes** – alone, at the start of a word, in the middle, and at the end. The shapes are usually small variations on one idea. The letter *be* is a dish with a dot under it: ب (*be*) alone, بـ (*be*) at the start, ـبـ (*be*) in the middle and ـب (*be*) at the end.

Seven letters are unsociable: they join the letter *before* them but never the one after. They are ا (*alef*), د (*dāl*), ذ (*zāl*), ر (*re*), ز (*ze*), ژ (*zhe*) and و (*vāv*). Whenever one of them appears, the pen lifts and the next letter starts fresh. That is why a word like دارو (*dāru*, ‘medicine’) looks like four separate pieces: every one of its letters is a non-joiner.

So a gap inside a word does **not** always mean a new word. You will learn to see the *baseline*: the connected run of letters that makes up each word.

### The vowels you see and the vowels you don't

Persian has six vowels. Three are **long** and always written: *ā* as in *father* (ا (*alef*)), *u* as in *moon* (و (*vāv*)) and *i* as in *see* (ی (*ye*)). Three are **short** – *a* as in *cat*, *e* as in *bed*, *o* as in *soft* – and these are usually **not written at all**.

So the word کتاب (*ketāb*, ‘book’) is spelled k-t-ā-b: the *e* between *k* and *t* is simply understood. Children's books and dictionaries add tiny marks above or below the letters to show the short vowels – کِتاب (*ketāb*) – and this course shows you the marks whenever you meet a new word, so you always know how it sounds. In newspapers, signs and messages they vanish, and you read the way you read *txt msgs*: from the consonants and from knowing the word.

This is the real skill of reading Persian, and it is why every lesson in this course gives you the pronunciation of each new word. Learn the sound together with the shape.

### Thirty-two letters, four of them Persian

The alphabet is the Arabic one, adopted after the 7th century, plus **four letters Persian invented** for sounds Arabic does not have: پ (*pe*) *p*, چ (*che*) *ch*, ژ (*zhe*) *zh* (the *s* of *pleasure*) and گ (*gāf*) *g*. Dots do a lot of the work: ب (*be*) *b* has one dot below, پ (*pe*) *p* three dots below; ج (*jim*) *j* has one dot inside, چ (*che*) *ch* has three.

In return, Persian kept several Arabic letters whose sounds it does not distinguish. There are four ways to write *z*, three ways to write *s*, two for *t*, two for *h* and two for *gh*. The spelling tells you a word's history – words with ص (*sād*), ض (*zād*), ط (*tā*), ظ (*zā*), ع (*eyn*) and ث (*se*) almost always came from Arabic – but the sound is the same. You learn the spelling with the word, just as English learners learn *night* and *knight*.

One more thing you will see everywhere: a tiny invisible space called the **ZWNJ** (zero-width non-joiner). It stops two parts of a word from joining without putting a real gap between them: می‌روم (*mi-ravam*, ‘I go’) is one word written as two unjoined pieces. Your keyboard makes it with Shift+Space on most Persian layouts.

### Look before you can read

You cannot sound these words out yet – that comes over the next seven lessons – but look at them with the rules above in mind. Every one is read from the right. In تاکسی (*tāksi*, ‘taxi’) you can see the baseline linking the letters; in رادیو (*rādiyo*, ‘radio’) the first letter *re* refuses to join, so the word breaks into pieces. Count the dots: three dots on top mark the *sh* of ش (*shin*), three below mark پ (*pe*) in *passport* and چ (*che*).

Notice too how Persian swallows loanwords: *hotel* and *doctor* are written exactly as they sound, without their English spelling quirks. Once you can read, every sign in an Iranian city gives you free vocabulary.

### How to learn the script

- Learn letters in **groups of look-alikes**, as the next lessons do. The dots are the difference, so count them every time.
- Write each letter by hand, right to left, saying its sound aloud. Your hand will remember the shapes long before your eyes do.
- Always learn the **pronunciation with the spelling**. Short vowels are not written, so the sound of a word is part of its spelling for you.
- Read everything you see: shop signs, menus, Instagram captions. Real words stick better than drills.
- Do not try to write beautifully yet. Clear and ugly beats slow and pretty.

### Check the rules

**Choose the right answer**

1. In which direction is a Persian line read? — left to right / right to left / top to bottom
2. How many of the six vowels are usually written? — all six / the three long ones / only ā
3. Which of these letters never joins the letter after it? — ب / د / س
4. Which letter was added by Persian and does not exist in Arabic? — ص / گ / ح
5. How many capital letters does Persian have? — 32 / 4 / none

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. right to left — Letters run right to left; only numbers run left to right.
2. the three long ones — The long vowels ā, u, i are letters; the short a, e, o are usually left out.
3. د — dāl is one of the seven non-joiners: ا د ذ ر ز ژ و.
4. گ — gāf (g), together with pe, che and zhe, is one of the four Persian additions.
5. none — There are no capitals – names look like any other word.

</details>
