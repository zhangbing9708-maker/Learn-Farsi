# How people really talk: spoken Tehrani Persian
## زبان کوچه و بازار
*zabān-e kuche o bāzār* — Elementary (A2), lesson 16. [Open this lesson with audio and interactive exercises](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/course/a2-16-how-people-really-talk.html)

Everything you have learned so far is real Persian – but it is the Persian of books, news and signs. Out loud, in shops, on the phone, in films and in every chat, Iranians use a spoken form, and in Tehran it differs from the written language in about a dozen regular ways: نان becomes نون, می‌رود becomes می‌ره, است becomes a simple -e. This lesson sets out those changes as a system, so that you can understand people when they talk – and talk like them.

**In this lesson you will learn to:**

- understand the regular sound changes between written and spoken Tehrani Persian
- turn written verbs into spoken ones: می‌رود → می‌ره، می‌خواهم → می‌خوام، می‌توانم → می‌تونم
- say 'is' the spoken way: خوبه، کیه، کجاس
- use the spoken endings: را → رو / ـو, -etān → -etun, -eshān → -eshun, -hā → -ā
- know when to use written and when spoken Persian

### Breakfast in Darband

_Saturday morning. Reza and Sara have come to take Tom up to Darband for breakfast, and Reza rings from the street. Every line has its spoken form – that is the conversation as it really sounded. The written line shows each word as it appears in a book._

- **Reza:** سلام تام! کجا هستی؟ ما جلوی خانه‌ات هستیم.  
  *salām Tām! kojā hasti? mā jelo-ye khāne-at hastim.* — Hi Tom! Where are you? We're outside your house.  
  Spoken: سلام تام! کجایی؟ ما جلوی خونه‌تیم. *salām Tām! kojāyi? mā jelo-ye khune-t-im.*
- **Tom:** سلام! ببخشید، الان می‌آیم. دارم کفش‌هایم را می‌پوشم.  
  *salām! bebakhshid, alān mi-āyam. dāram kafsh-hā-yam rā mi-pusham.* — Hi! Sorry, I'm just coming. I'm putting my shoes on.  
  Spoken: سلام! ببخشید، الان میام. دارم کفشامو می‌پوشم. *salām! bebakhshid, alān miyām. dāram kafsh-ā-mo mi-pusham.*
- **Sara:** تام، نان هم داری؟ ما برای صبحانه نان نخریدیم.  
  *Tām, nān ham dāri? mā barā-ye sobhāne nān na-kharidim.* — Tom, have you got any bread? We didn't buy bread for breakfast.  
  Spoken: تام، نون هم داری؟ ما واسه صبحونه نون نخریدیم. *Tām, nun ham dāri? mā vāse sobhune nun na-kharidim.*
- **Tom:** نه. اما نانوایی سر کوچه است. می‌توانم بخرم.  
  *na. ammā nānvāyi sar-e kuche ast. mi-tavānam bekharam.* — No. But the bakery is at the end of the street. I can buy some.  
  Spoken: نه. ولی نونوایی سر کوچه‌س. می‌تونم بخرم. *na. vali nunvāyi sar-e kuche-s. mi-tunam bekharam.*
- **Reza:** لازم نیست. سارا می‌گوید آن‌جا یک کافه‌ی خوب هست.  
  *lāzem nist. Sārā mi-guyad ān-jā yek kāfe-ye khub hast.* — No need. Sara says there's a good café up there.  
  Spoken: لازم نیس. سارا می‌گه اون‌جا یه کافه‌ی خوب هس. *lāzem nis. Sārā mi-ge un-jā ye kāfe-ye khub has.*
- **Sara:** بله، صبحانه‌اش خیلی خوب است. پدر و مادرم هم آن‌جا را خیلی دوست دارند.  
  *bale, sobhāne-ash kheyli khub ast. pedar o mādar-am ham ān-jā rā kheyli dust dārand.* — Yes, the breakfast there is really good. My parents love the place too.  
  Spoken: آره، صبحونه‌ش خیلی خوبه. بابا مامانم هم اون‌جا رو خیلی دوست دارن. *āre, sobhune-sh kheyli khub-e. bābā māmān-am ham un-jā ro kheyli dust dāran.*
- **Tom:** باشد. پس کیفم را برمی‌دارم و پایین می‌آیم.  
  *bāshad. pas kif-am rā bar-mi-dāram o pāyin mi-āyam.* — OK. Then I'll grab my bag and come down.  
  Spoken: باشه. پس کیفمو برمی‌دارم میام پایین. *bāshe. pas kif-am-o bar-mi-dāram miyām pāyin.*
- **Reza:** زود بیا! اگر دیر برسیم، جا پیدا نمی‌کنیم.  
  *zud biyā! agar dir berasim, jā peydā nemi-konim.* — Be quick! If we get there late, we won't find a table.  
  Spoken: زود بیا! اگه دیر برسیم، جا پیدا نمی‌کنیم. *zud biyā! age dir berasim, jā peydā nemi-konim.*
- **Tom:** دوستانتان هم می‌آیند؟  
  *dustān-etān ham mi-āyand?* — Are your friends coming too?  
  Spoken: دوستاتون هم میان؟ *dust-ā-tun ham miyān?*
- **Sara:** امیر و مهسا می‌آیند. ماشینشان خراب است، با تاکسی می‌آیند.  
  *Amir o Mahsā mi-āyand. māshin-eshān kharāb ast, bā tāksi mi-āyand.* — Amir and Mahsa are coming. Their car has broken down, so they're coming by taxi.  
  Spoken: امیر و مهسا میان. ماشینشون خرابه، با تاکسی میان. *Amir o Mahsā miyān. māshin-eshun kharāb-e, bā tāksi miyān.*
- **Tom:** می‌خواهم امروز فقط فارسی حرف بزنم. کمکم می‌کنید؟  
  *mi-khāham emruz faghat fārsi harf bezanam. komak-am mi-konid?* — I want to speak only Persian today. Will you help me?  
  Spoken: می‌خوام امروز فقط فارسی حرف بزنم. کمکم می‌کنین؟ *mi-khām emruz faghat fārsi harf bezanam. komak-am mi-konin?*
- **Reza:** حتماً! من هر چیزی را دو بار می‌گویم: یک بار تند، یک بار آهسته.  
  *hatman! man har chiz-i rā do bār mi-guyam: yek bār tond, yek bār āheste.* — Of course! I'll say everything twice: once fast, once slowly.  
  Spoken: حتماً! من هر چیزی رو دو بار می‌گم: یه بار تند، یه بار یواش. *hatman! man har chizi ro do bār mi-gam: ye bār tond, ye bār yavāsh.*
- **Sara:** این را هم بدان: در کتاب‌ها یک جور می‌نویسند، در خیابان یک جور دیگر حرف می‌زنند.  
  *in rā ham bedān: dar ketāb-hā yek jur mi-nevisand, dar khiyābān yek jur-e digar harf mi-zanand.* — And know this: in books people write one way, and in the street they talk another.  
  Spoken: اینو هم بدون: تو کتابا یه جور می‌نویسن، تو خیابون یه جور دیگه حرف می‌زنن. *in-o ham bedun: tu ketāb-ā ye jur mi-nevisan, tu khiyābun ye jur-e dige harf mi-zanan.*
- **Tom:** پس کتاب‌هایم را در خانه می‌گذارم و فقط گوش می‌کنم!  
  *pas ketāb-hā-yam rā dar khāne mi-gozāram o faghat gush mi-konam!* — Then I'll leave my books at home and just listen!  
  Spoken: پس کتابامو تو خونه می‌ذارم و فقط گوش می‌کنم! *pas ketāb-ā-mo tu khune mi-zāram o faghat gush mi-konam!*

### Two Persians in one

Read only the written lines of the dialogue and they sound like a newspaper; nobody talks like that. Read the spoken lines and you hear Tehran. Persian has two registers that live side by side:

- **Written Persian** – فارسی نوشتاری (*fārsi-ye neveshtāri*), or *ketābi*, 'bookish' – is used in books, newspapers, signs, official letters, the news and speeches. It is the same all over Iran, and it is what this course writes in the main line of every sentence.
- **Spoken Persian** – فارسی گفتاری (*fārsi-ye goftāri*), or محاوره (*mohāvere*, ‘conversation’) – is used by everyone in every conversation: at home, in shops, at work, in films and songs, and in chats. Its standard is the speech of Tehran.

The spoken forms are not slang and not mistakes. Professors, ministers and grandmothers all say می‌ره (*mi-re*), not می‌رود (*mi-ravad*), and someone who talks in the written forms sounds like a textbook – Iranians call it کتابی حرف زدن (*ketābi harf zadan*, ‘talking like a book’). Better still, the changes are regular. Learn the patterns in the three tables below and you can turn almost any written sentence into speech – and, just as important, understand what people say to you.

### Sounds and little words

**ā before n or m often becomes u.** This is the best-known Tehrani change: نان (*nān*) → نون (*nun*), خانه (*khāne*) → خونه (*khune*), آن (*ān*) → اون (*un*). It happens inside verbs too: می‌دانم (*mi-dānam*) → می‌دونم (*mi-dunam*, ‘I know’), می‌توانم (*mi-tavānam*) → می‌تونم (*mi-tunam*, ‘I can’), آمدم (*āmadam*) → اومدم (*umadam*, ‘I came’). It does **not** happen in every word: names (تهران (*Tehrān*), ایران (*Irān*)), learned words (زمان (*zamān*, ‘time’), امکان (*emkān*, ‘possibility’)), خانم (*khānom*) and even خانواده (*khānevāde*, ‘family’), from the same root as خانه, keep their ā. There is no rule for which words change – but the everyday words do, so you will learn them quickly.

**Final -ar becomes -e** in a few very common words: اگر (*agar*) → اگه (*age*), دیگر (*digar*) → دیگه (*dige*), مگر (*magar*) → مگه (*mage*).

**Little words get shorter or are replaced:** یک (*yek*) → یه (*ye*), چه (*che*) → چی (*chi*), در (*dar*) → تو (*tu*), برای (*barā-ye*) → واسه (*vāse*), and with friends بله (*bale*) → آره (*āre*).

**Places come after the verb.** In speech the destination usually follows the verb, without به (*be*): written به خانه می‌روم (*be khāne mi-ravam*), spoken می‌رم خونه (*mi-ram khune*, ‘I'm going home’).

### Verbs: shorter stems, softer endings and 'is'

**Person endings.** In the present tense *-ad* (he, she) becomes **-e**, *-and* (they) becomes **-an**, and *-id* (you, plural or polite) becomes **-in**: می‌رود (*mi-ravad*) → می‌ره (*mi-re*), می‌روند (*mi-ravand*) → می‌رن (*mi-ran*), می‌روید (*mi-ravid*) → می‌رین (*mi-rin*). The past changes the same way for *they* and *you*: رفتند (*raftand*) → رفتن (*raftan*), رفتید (*raftid*) → رفتین (*raftin*).

**Short stems.** The most common verbs lose a syllable in the present and the subjunctive: می‌روم (*mi-ravam*) → می‌رم (*mi-ram*), بروم (*beravam*) → برم (*beram*), and some imperatives shrink too: بگذار (*begozār*) → بذار (*bezār*), بنشین (*beneshin*) → بشین (*beshin*), بیاور (*biyāvar*) → بیار (*biyār*). The table lists the ones you will hear every day.

**است.** Spoken *is* depends on the sound before it:

- after a consonant it is **-e**: خوب است (*khub ast*) → خوبه (*khub-e*)
- after -i it is **-ye**: کی است؟ (*ki ast?*) → کیه؟ (*ki-ye?*, ‘who is it?’)
- after -o or -u it is **-e**, written ئه: مال تو است (*māl-e to ast*) → مال توئه (*māl-e to-e*, ‘it's yours’)
- after -ā or -e it is **-st**, or just **-s**: کجا است؟ (*kojā ast?*) → کجاس؟ (*kojā-s?*, ‘where is it?’), خسته است (*khaste ast*) → خسته‌س (*khaste-s*, ‘he's tired’)

The *t* also drops in نیست (*nist*) → نیس (*nis*) and هست (*hast*) → هس (*has*).

### Endings: را, ها and the possessives

**را** has two spoken forms. After a consonant it shrinks to **-o** and joins the word: کتاب را (*ketāb rā*) → کتابو (*ketāb-o*), این را (*in rā*) → اینو (*in-o*). After a vowel it is **ro**, written separately: خانه را (*khāne rā*) → خونه رو (*khune ro*). Many people say *ro* everywhere, and you can too.

**ها** loses its h after a consonant: کتاب‌ها (*ketāb-hā*) → کتابا (*ketāb-ā*), دوست‌ها (*dust-hā*) → دوستا (*dust-ā*). After a vowel it usually stays: بچه‌ها (*bacche-hā*).

**The possessive endings** change their vowels: *-at* → **-et**, *-ash* → **-esh**, and the plural ones swap ā for u: *-emān* → **-emun**, *-etān* → **-etun**, *-eshān* → **-eshun**. The singular ones are spelled as before – only the sound changes – while the plural ones are written with و in chats: کتابشون.

**Endings on prepositions.** Instead of a separate pronoun, speech hangs the ending on the preposition: به او (*be u*) → بهش (*behesh*, ‘to him, to her’), با تو (*bā to*) → باهات (*bāhāt*, ‘with you’), برای من (*barā-ye man*) → برام (*barām*, ‘for me’), از آن (*az ān*) → ازش (*azesh*, ‘from it’).

The endings stack in a fixed order – noun, plural, possessive, را: کتاب‌هایتان را (*ketāb-hā-yetān rā*) → کتاباتونو (*ketāb-ā-tun-o*, ‘your books (as the object)’).

| Written | Spoken | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| را (*rā*) after a consonant | ـو (*-o*) | کتاب را (*ketāb rā*) → کتابو (*ketāb-o*) |
| را (*rā*) after a vowel | رو (*ro*) | خانه را (*khāne rā*) → خونه رو (*khune ro*) |
| ـها (*-hā*) | ـا (*-ā*) | کتاب‌ها (*ketāb-hā*) → کتابا (*ketāb-ā*) |
| ـت (*-at*) your | ـت (*-et*) | کتابت (*ketāb-at*) → کتابت (*ketāb-et*) |
| ـش (*-ash*) his, her | ـش (*-esh*) | کتابش (*ketāb-ash*) → کتابش (*ketāb-esh*) |
| ـمان (*-emān*) our | ـمون (*-emun*) | کتابمان (*ketāb-emān*) → کتابمون (*ketāb-emun*) |
| ـتان (*-etān*) your (pl.) | ـتون (*-etun*) | کتابتان (*ketāb-etān*) → کتابتون (*ketāb-etun*) |
| ـشان (*-eshān*) their | ـشون (*-eshun*) | کتابشان (*ketāb-eshān*) → کتابشون (*ketāb-eshun*) |
| به او (*be u*) | بهش (*behesh*) | بهش گفتم (*behesh goftam*, ‘I told him’) |
| با تو (*bā to*) | باهات (*bāhāt*) | باهات میام (*bāhāt miyām*, ‘I'll come with you’) |
| برای من (*barā-ye man*) | برام (*barām*) | برام بفرست (*barām beferest*, ‘send it to me’) |
| به ما (*be mā*) | بهمون (*behemun*) | بهمون بگو (*behemun begu*, ‘tell us’) |

- کتاب‌هایتان را آوردید؟  
  *ketāb-hā-yetān rā āvardid?* — Have you brought your books?  
  Spoken: کتاباتونو آوردین؟ *ketāb-ā-tun-o āvordin?*
- دوستانشان را دیدم.  
  *dustān-eshān rā didam.* — I saw their friends.  
  Spoken: دوستاشونو دیدم. *dust-ā-shun-o didam.*
- اسمتان چیست؟  
  *esm-etān chist?* — What's your name?  
  Spoken: اسمتون چیه؟ *esm-etun chiye?*
- این را به او بگو.  
  *in rā be u begu.* — Tell him this.  
  Spoken: اینو بهش بگو. *in-o behesh begu.*
- خانه‌شان خیلی بزرگ است.  
  *khāne-shān kheyli bozorg ast.* — Their house is very big.  
  Spoken: خونه‌شون خیلی بزرگه. *khune-shun kheyli bozorg-e.*
- با تو می‌آیم.  
  *bā to mi-āyam.* — I'll come with you.  
  Spoken: باهات میام. *bāhāt miyām.*

### Tehrani on screen

Why Tehrani? Tehran has been the capital for more than two hundred years, and its speech is the voice of national television, radio, films and the pop songs everyone sings along to. Foreign films and series are dubbed into Persian in Tehrani speech too – Iranian dubbing, دوبله (*duble*), is famous, and generations grew up hearing cartoon heroes and Hollywood stars say می‌خوام (*mi-khām*) and بریم (*berim*, ‘let's go’).

So whether you are in Shiraz, Mashhad or Tabriz, everyone understands spoken Tehrani – though they may not speak it themselves. Isfahanis have a famous sing-song melody, Shirazis a slow, drawn-out one, and in many provinces people grow up with another language at home: Azerbaijani Turkish, Kurdish, Gilaki, Luri, Baluchi or Arabic (lesson 7 of B2 tours them). The written language, on the other hand, is the same everywhere – it is the glue between all these ways of speaking.

A practical rule: **understand both, write the written form, speak the spoken one.** Use written forms in emails to officials, in essays and on forms, and spoken forms everywhere else. And if a shopkeeper smiles when you ask این چقدر است؟ (*in cheghadr ast?*), try این چنده؟ (*in chande?*, ‘how much is this?’) next time.

### Tips

- Go back through earlier lessons and read each spoken line aloud after the written one: the pairs train your ear faster than any table.
- Keep to one register in a sentence. *mi-ram khune* and *be khāne mi-ravam* are both fine; *mi-ravam khune* sounds odd.
- The spoken -e has several jobs: *khub-e* (it is good), *mi-kone* (he does), and in *khune* it is simply part of the word. The context always tells you which.
- Stress does not move: *mi-ravad* and *mi-re* are both stressed on *mi*, and *ketāb-hā* and *ketāb-ā* on the last syllable.
- In chats people often write the spoken forms without the half-space – میره، میخوام – and with ه for the spoken -e: خوبه، کجاس. Now you can read them.
- When in doubt, use the written form. It is always understood: you will sound polite and a little bookish, never wrong.

### Written and spoken

**Choose the right answer**

1. What is the spoken form of می‌رود (*mi-ravad*)? — می‌ره / می‌رم / می‌رن
2. کتاب‌هایشان (*ketāb-hā-yeshān*) in speech is… — کتاباشون / کتابامون / کتاباتون
3. Someone asks رضا کجاس؟ (*Rezā kojā-s?*) What is the written form? — رضا کجاست؟ / رضا کجا رفت؟ / رضا کجا بود؟
4. Which word keeps its ā in spoken Tehrani? — خانه / نان / خانم
5. اینو بهش بگو. (*in-o behesh begu.*) means… — Tell him this. / Tell me this. / Did he tell you this?
6. Which is the spoken form of اگر می‌توانید، بیایید. (*agar mi-tavānid, biyāyid.*)? — اگه می‌تونین، بیاین. / اگه می‌تونم، بیام. / اگر می‌توانید، بیاین.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. می‌ره — -ad (he, she) becomes -e: mi-re. mi-ram is 'I go', mi-ran 'they go'.
2. کتاباشون — -hā → -ā and -eshān → -eshun: ketāb-ā-shun.
3. رضا کجاست؟ — After -ā, ast becomes -st or just -s: kojā-st, kojā-s.
4. خانم — khānom never changes; khāne → khune and nān → nun.
5. Tell him this. — in-o = in rā (this, as the object), behesh = be u (to him / her).
6. اگه می‌تونین، بیاین. — agar → age, mi-tavānid → mi-tunin, biyāyid → biyāyn. The third option mixes the two registers.

</details>
