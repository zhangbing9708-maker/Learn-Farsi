# My day: the daily routine
## روز من
*ruz-e man* — Beginner (A1), lesson 11. [Open this lesson with audio and interactive exercises](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/learn-farsi/course/a1-11-my-day.html)

What do you do every day? In this lesson you describe your daily routine – getting up, having breakfast, going to work or class, coming home and going to bed – with the present tense of the most common Persian verbs. You learn the stems that do not follow the rules (rav-, ā-, shav-), the words for the parts of the day, and how to put your day in order with avval 'first' and ba'd 'then'.

**In this lesson you will learn to:**

- describe your daily routine in the present tense
- use the stems of everyday verbs: rav-, ā-, khor-, khāb-, shav-
- put events in order with avval, ba'd, ba'd az and ghabl az
- say how often you do something: har ruz, hamishe, ma'mulan, gāhi, hich-vaght

### An early bird and a night owl

_Tom shares a flat in Tehran with Reza. Reza is always out of the house before Tom is even awake, and Tom wants to know why._

- **Tom:** رضا، تو هر روز صبح زود بیدار می‌شوی؟  
  *Rezā, to har ruz sobh zud bidār mi-shavi?* — Reza, do you get up early every morning?  
  Spoken: رضا، تو هر روز صبح زود بیدار می‌شی؟ *Rezā, to har ruz sobh zud bidār mi-shi?*
- **Reza:** بله، ساعت شش بیدار می‌شوم. اول دوش می‌گیرم، بعد صبحانه می‌خورم.  
  *bale, sā'at-e shesh bidār mi-shavam. avval dush mi-giram, ba'd sobhāne mi-khoram.* — Yes, I get up at six. First I take a shower, then I have breakfast.  
  Spoken: آره، ساعت شش بیدار می‌شم. اول دوش می‌گیرم، بعد صبحونه می‌خورم. *āre, sā'at-e shesh bidār mi-sham. avval dush mi-giram, ba'd sobhune mi-khoram.*
- **Tom:** چرا این‌قدر زود؟  
  *cherā in-ghadr zud?* — Why so early?
- **Reza:** کارم ساعت هشت شروع می‌شود و راه دور است. با مترو سر کار می‌روم.  
  *kār-am sā'at-e hasht shoru' mi-shavad va rāh dur ast. bā metro sar-e kār mi-ravam.* — My work starts at eight and it's a long way. I go to work by metro.
- **Tom:** ناهار کجا می‌خوری؟  
  *nāhār kojā mi-khori?* — Where do you have lunch?
- **Reza:** ظهر با همکارهایم در شرکت ناهار می‌خوریم.  
  *zohr bā ham-kār-hā-yam dar sherkat nāhār mi-khorim.* — At midday my colleagues and I have lunch at the office.
- **Tom:** عصر به خانه می‌آیی؟  
  *asr be khāne mi-āyi?* — Do you come home in the late afternoon?  
  Spoken: عصر میای خونه؟ *asr miyāy khune?*
- **Reza:** بله، ساعت شش به خانه می‌آیم. اول کمی استراحت می‌کنم، بعد شام می‌پزم.  
  *bale, sā'at-e shesh be khāne mi-āyam. avval kami esterāhat mi-konam, ba'd shām mi-pazam.* — Yes, I come home at six. First I rest a little, then I cook dinner.
- **Tom:** شب‌ها دیر می‌خوابی؟  
  *shab-hā dir mi-khābi?* — Do you go to bed late?
- **Reza:** نه، معمولاً ساعت یازده می‌خوابم. تو چطور؟  
  *na, ma'mulan sā'at-e yāzdah mi-khābam. to chetor?* — No, I usually go to bed at eleven. What about you?
- **Tom:** من ساعت دو می‌خوابم و ساعت ده بیدار می‌شوم.  
  *man sā'at-e do mi-khābam va sā'at-e dah bidār mi-shavam.* — I go to bed at two and get up at ten.
- **Reza:** ساعت ده؟ پس کلاس فارسی‌ات چه می‌شود؟  
  *sā'at-e dah? pas kelās-e fārsi-at che mi-shavad?* — At ten? Then what about your Persian class?
- **Tom:** کلاسم ساعت یازده است!  
  *kelās-am sā'at-e yāzdah ast!* — My class is at eleven!  
  Spoken: کلاسم ساعت یازدهه! *kelās-am sā'at-e yāzdah-e!*
- **Reza:** خوش به حالت!  
  *khosh be hāl-at!* — Lucky you!

### Talking about your day

Persian uses the ordinary present tense for things you do regularly: ساعت شش بیدار می‌شوم (*sā'at-e shesh bidār mi-shavam*, ‘I get up at six’). There is no extra word like English *do* in questions – Tom's زود بیدار می‌شوی؟ (*zud bidār mi-shavi?*, ‘do you get up early?’) is just the statement with a rising voice.

To say *at* a time, put ساعت (*sā'at*, ‘hour, o'clock’) before the number, linked with the little **-e**: ساعت هفت (*sā'at-e haft*, ‘at seven’). You will learn to tell the time properly in the next lesson; for now, whole hours are enough.

The parts of the day need no preposition: صبح (*sobh*, ‘(in the) morning’), ظهر (*zohr*, ‘(at) noon’), عصر (*asr*, ‘(in the) late afternoon’), شب (*shab*, ‘(at) night, (in the) evening’). Add the plural **-hā** and they mean *every morning, every evening*: شب‌ها (*shab-hā*, ‘in the evenings’).

Notice how Reza goes to work: سر کار (*sar-e kār*, ‘to work’), literally *to the head of work*. Iranians go *sar-e kār*, and when they are there they are *sar-e kār* too.

### Everyday verbs and their stems

You already know the present tense from lesson 6: **mi- + present stem + ending**. The endings never change (-am, -i, -ad, -im, -id, -and); the work is in the stem.

Verbs ending in **-idan** are easy: drop -idan and you have the stem – خوابیدن (*khābidan*) → خواب (*khāb*) → می‌خوابم (*mi-khābam*, ‘I sleep’). Many of the commonest verbs, though, have a stem you simply have to learn, the way English has *go – went*: رفتن (*raftan*, ‘to go’) → **rav-**, شدن (*shodan*, ‘to become’) → **shav-**, گرفتن (*gereftan*, ‘to take’) → **gir-**.

آمدن (*āmadan*, ‘to come’) has the tiny stem **ā-**, and a **y** slips in before the ending: می‌آیم (*mi-āyam*), می‌آیی (*mi-āyi*), می‌آید (*mi-āyad*). Two-word verbs such as بیدار شدن (*bidār shodan*, ‘to wake up’) keep their first word and change only the verb: بیدار می‌شوم (*bidār mi-shavam*, ‘I wake up’).

For the negative, **mi-** becomes **nemi-**: نمی‌روم (*nemi-ravam*, ‘I don't go’), بیدار نمی‌شوم (*bidār nemi-shavam*, ‘I don't wake up’).

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| رفتن (*raftan*) | رو (*rav*) | می‌روم (*mi-ravam*) | می‌رود (*mi-ravad*) | می‌روند (*mi-ravand*) | go |
| آمدن (*āmadan*) | آ (*ā*) | می‌آیم (*mi-āyam*) | می‌آید (*mi-āyad*) | می‌آیند (*mi-āyand*) | come |
| خوردن (*khordan*) | خور (*khor*) | می‌خورم (*mi-khoram*) | می‌خورد (*mi-khorad*) | می‌خورند (*mi-khorand*) | eat, drink |
| خوابیدن (*khābidan*) | خواب (*khāb*) | می‌خوابم (*mi-khābam*) | می‌خوابد (*mi-khābad*) | می‌خوابند (*mi-khāband*) | sleep |
| بیدار شدن (*bidār shodan*) | شو (*shav*) | بیدار می‌شوم (*bidār mi-shavam*) | بیدار می‌شود (*bidār mi-shavad*) | بیدار می‌شوند (*bidār mi-shavand*) | wake up |

### First…, then…: putting your day in order

To tell a sequence, Persian uses اول (*avval*, ‘first’) and بعد (*ba'd*, ‘then’), exactly like English: اول دوش می‌گیرم، بعد صبحانه می‌خورم. (*avval dush mi-giram, ba'd sobhāne mi-khoram.*, ‘First I take a shower, then I have breakfast.’)

With a noun, use بعد از (*ba'd az*, ‘after’) and قبل از (*ghabl az*, ‘before’): بعد از صبحانه (*ba'd az sobhāne*, ‘after breakfast’), قبل از شام (*ghabl az shām*, ‘before dinner’). For a stretch of time, use **az … tā …**: از صبح تا شب (*az sobh tā shab*, ‘from morning till night’).

Word order stays simple: time words usually come near the start, and the verb is always last. **Subject – time – place – object – verb**: من ظهر در شرکت ناهار می‌خورم. (*man zohr dar sherkat nāhār mi-khoram.*, ‘I have lunch at the office at noon.’)

| Persian | Reading | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| اول (*avval*) | avval | first |
| بعد (*ba'd*) | ba'd | then, afterwards |
| بعد از آن (*ba'd az ān*) | ba'd az ān | after that |
| بعد از ناهار (*ba'd az nāhār*) | ba'd az + noun | after lunch |
| قبل از خواب (*ghabl az khāb*) | ghabl az + noun | before bed |
| از صبح تا شب (*az sobh tā shab*) | az … tā … | from morning till night |

- بعد از صبحانه لباس می‌پوشم.  
  *ba'd az sobhāne lebās mi-pusham.* — After breakfast I get dressed.
- قبل از خواب کمی کتاب می‌خوانم.  
  *ghabl az khāb kami ketāb mi-khānam.* — Before bed I read a little.  
  Spoken: قبل از خواب یه کم کتاب می‌خونم. *ghabl az khāb ye kam ketāb mi-khunam.*
- او از صبح تا شب کار می‌کند.  
  *u az sobh tā shab kār mi-konad.* — She works from morning till night.
- اول به دانشگاه می‌روم، بعد به کتابخانه.  
  *avval be dāneshgāh mi-ravam, ba'd be ketāb-khāne.* — First I go to the university, then to the library.

### Always, usually, never: how often

Words for *how often* go before the verb, usually near the start of the sentence: من هر روز چای می‌خورم. (*man har ruz chāy mi-khoram.*, ‘I drink tea every day.’)

هر (*har*, ‘every’) works with any time word: هر روز (*har ruz*), هر شب (*har shab*), هر صبح (*har sobh*). Plural time words say the same thing more casually: صبح‌ها (*sobh-hā*, ‘in the mornings’), شب‌ها (*shab-hā*, ‘in the evenings’).

هیچ‌وقت (*hich-vaght*, ‘never’) needs a **negative** verb – Persian says *never I don't*: هیچ‌وقت قهوه نمی‌خورم. (*hich-vaght ghahve nemi-khoram.*, ‘I never drink coffee.’)

| Persian | Reading | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| همیشه (*hamishe*) | hamishe | always |
| هر روز (*har ruz*) | har ruz | every day |
| معمولاً (*ma'mulan*) | ma'mulan | usually |
| گاهی (*gāhi*) | gāhi | sometimes |
| هیچ‌وقت (*hich-vaght*) | hich-vaght + negative verb | never |

- من همیشه صبحانه می‌خورم.  
  *man hamishe sobhāne mi-khoram.* — I always have breakfast.
- ما معمولاً ساعت نه شام می‌خوریم.  
  *mā ma'mulan sā'at-e noh shām mi-khorim.* — We usually have dinner at nine.
- سارا گاهی با تاکسی سر کار می‌رود.  
  *Sārā gāhi bā tāksi sar-e kār mi-ravad.* — Sara sometimes goes to work by taxi.
- تام هیچ‌وقت زود بیدار نمی‌شود.  
  *Tām hich-vaght zud bidār nemi-shavad.* — Tom never gets up early.

### The rhythm of an Iranian day

An Iranian day keeps different hours from a European one. Breakfast is light – bread, cheese, butter or jam, walnuts and, above all, tea from the سماور (*samāvar*, ‘samovar’). The main meal is ناهار (*nāhār*, ‘lunch’), usually between one and two: rice, a stew or kebab, yoghurt and fresh herbs. Dinner comes late, at nine or even ten o'clock, and is often lighter.

In many cities, especially hot ones like Shiraz, Yazd and Isfahan, small shops close in the early afternoon and open again around five until late at night, and a nap after lunch is a respected habit. Tehran is different: offices work straight through, and people spend hours in traffic – any taxi driver will tell you that the city's real timetable is set by the ترافیک (*terāfik*, ‘traffic’).

The week has its own rhythm too. Saturday, شنبه (*shanbe*), is the first working day; Friday, جمعه (*jom'e*), is the weekly day off, and for most people Thursday is a short day or a holiday as well. You will learn the names of all the days in the next lesson.

### Tips

- **mi-āyam** has a glide: say *mi-ā-yam*. In speech it shrinks to **miyām, miyād** – *zohr miyād khune* 'he comes home at noon'.
- In speech the place often moves after the verb: *miyām khune* 'I'm coming home', *mi-ram sar-e kār* 'I go to work'. In writing, keep the verb last.
- **ba'd** (بعد) has a small catch in the voice for the ع. Without it, *bad* means 'bad' – and *ba'd az ghazā* 'after the meal' is much nicer than a bad meal.
- **sobh** (صبح) ends in a breathy **h** (ح) – say it: *sobh*, not *sob*.
- Learn new verbs in pairs, infinitive and stem: *raftan / rav-*, *āmadan / ā-*, *shodan / shav-*. The dictionary gives the infinitive; you speak with the stem.

### Listen and choose

**Listening**

1. (Listen on the website.) We have lunch at nine. / We have dinner at nine. / We have breakfast at nine.
2. (Listen on the website.) He always comes late. / He never comes late. / He always goes early.
3. (Listen on the website.) After breakfast I read. / Before bed I read. / Before bed I take a shower.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. ما ساعت نه شام می‌خوریم. — We have dinner at nine.
2. او همیشه دیر می‌آید. — He always comes late.
3. قبل از خواب کتاب می‌خوانم. — Before bed I read.

</details>

### Say it in Persian

**Say it in Persian**

1. I get up at six every day.
2. First I take a shower, then I get dressed.
3. Do you come home at noon? (to a friend)
4. We usually go to bed late.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. من هر روز ساعت شش بیدار می‌شوم. — *man har ruz sā'at-e shesh bidār mi-shavam.*
2. اول دوش می‌گیرم، بعد لباس می‌پوشم. — *avval dush mi-giram, ba'd lebās mi-pusham.*
3. ظهر به خانه می‌آیی؟ — *zohr be khāne mi-āyi?*
4. ما معمولاً دیر می‌خوابیم. — *mā ma'mulan dir mi-khābim.*

</details>
