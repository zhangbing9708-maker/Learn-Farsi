# Dialects and accents: the many voices of Iran
## لهجه‌ها و زبان‌های ایران
*lahje-hā o zabān-hā-ye Irān* — Upper intermediate (B2), lesson 7. [Open this lesson with audio and interactive exercises](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/course/b2-07-dialects-and-accents.html)

Everyone in Iran writes the same Persian, but nobody speaks it in quite the same way. Tehrani has become the spoken standard of radio, television and film; Isfahan, Shiraz, Mashhad, Yazd and Kerman each have a tune of their own; and millions of Iranians grow up with another mother tongue altogether – Azerbaijani Turkish, Kurdish, Gilaki, Lori, Baluchi, Arabic and more. This lesson teaches you to tell an accent from a language, to recognise the best-known regional features, and to talk about language and identity with tact.

**In this lesson you will learn to:**

- explain the difference between لهجه (accent), گویش (dialect) and زبان (language)
- recognise a few features of Tehrani, Isfahani, Shirazi, Mashhadi, Yazdi and Kermani speech
- name the other languages of Iran, where they are spoken and which family they belong to
- talk respectfully about mother tongues, bilingualism and accents

### Accent, dialect, language

Persian has three words you will need:

- لهجه (*lahje*) – an **accent** or local way of speaking Persian: *lahje-ye esfahāni*, the Isfahani accent. Same words and grammar, different sounds, tune and a few local words.
- گویش (*guyesh*) – a **dialect** or local speech that is further from standard Persian, often a whole local language. Iranians often call Gilaki or Mazandarani a *guyesh*; linguists usually treat them as languages of their own.
- زبان (*zabān*) – a **language**: Persian, Kurdish, Azerbaijani Turkish, Armenian.

Where the line falls between *guyesh* and *zabān* is partly a matter of habit and of politics, so when in doubt say *zabān* – nobody is offended to hear that their mother tongue is a language.

**The written standard** is the same all over Iran. **The spoken standard** is educated Tehrani – the Persian of the capital, of television news and of almost every film. It is what you learned in چطوری؟ (*chetowri?*) and می‌رم (*mi-ram*): *ān* becomes *un* (نون), *-ad* becomes *-e* (می‌ره), *ast* becomes *-e*. Iranians from other cities switch into it easily with strangers – and back into their own accent with family.

Iran's constitution makes Persian the official language and the language of schooling, and also allows the regional languages in the press and media and the teaching of their literature in schools. Most Iranians whose mother tongue is not Persian are bilingual.

### Accents of Persian: what you may hear

The differences between Iranian accents are mostly in the **tune** (آهنگ کلام), in a few **sounds**, and in a handful of **local words**; the grammar is the same. The descriptions below are what learners usually notice first. Accents vary from street to street and from one generation to the next – younger people everywhere sound more Tehrani – so treat every line as *you may hear*, not *everybody says*.

Two warnings. First, Iranian comedy loves accents, and so do Iranians themselves when they tease each other; as a foreigner, enjoy them but don't put them on for laughs. Second, nobody's accent is 'wrong' – an Isfahani speaking Isfahani is as correct as a Tehrani speaking Tehrani.

| Accent | What you may hear | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tehran (تهرانی) | The spoken standard: quick, with the voice falling at the end of a statement; *ān* → *un*, *-ad* → *-e*, *ast* → *-e*. | نون (*nun*) for نان, می‌ره (*mi-re*) for می‌رود |
| Isfahan (اصفهانی) | The best-known tune in Iran: a long rise and fall, with the last syllable of a phrase stretched – Iranians call it singing. You may also hear a *d* at the end of a word sound like *t*, and some people say Isfahanis use the old plural *-ān* more than Tehranis do. | you may hear چقز (*cheghaz*) for چقدر 'how much', *ziyāt* for زیاد |
| Shiraz (شیرازی) | Slow, relaxed and warm, with long drawn-out vowels; friendly forms of address. | کاکو (*kāku*) 'brother, mate' |
| Mashhad (مشهدی) | A rhythm of its own and a rounder *ā*; older people may use words of the Khorasan countryside. | you may hear older speakers say مو (*mo*) for من 'I' |
| Yazd (یزدی) | A calm, even tune; many older local words, and vowels that can sound closer to written Persian. | you may hear مو (*mo*) for 'I' among older people |
| Kerman (کرمانی) | A lilting tune that Iranians recognise at once; local words, especially among older speakers. | you may hear مو (*mo*) for 'I' here too |

- خانم، نان تازه دارید؟  
  *khānom, nān-e tāze dārid?* — Excuse me (madam), do you have fresh bread?  
  Spoken: خانم، نون تازه دارین؟ *khānum, nun-e tāze dārin?*  
  _Tehrani raises *ā* and *o* before n and m: *nun*, *khānum*. Isfahan does the same._
- این فرش چقدر است؟  
  *in farsh cheghadr ast?* — How much is this carpet?  
  Spoken: این فرش چنده؟ *in farsh chande?*  
  _In Isfahan you may hear *cheghaz?* – sung, with a long last syllable._
- کاکو، کجا می‌روی؟  
  *kāku, kojā mi-ravi?* — Where are you off to, mate?  
  Spoken: کاکو، کجا می‌ری؟ *kāku, kojā mi-ri?*  
  _Very Shirazi. Said slowly and warmly, it sounds friendly, not rude._
- من فردا به مشهد می‌روم.  
  *man fardā be Mashhad mi-ravam.* — I'm going to Mashhad tomorrow.  
  Spoken: من فردا می‌رم مشهد. *man fardā mi-ram Mashhad.*  
  _In Mashhad, Yazd or Kerman you may hear an older speaker begin with *mo* instead of *man*._
- شیرازی‌ها خیلی آرام حرف می‌زنند.  
  *Shirāzi-hā kheyli ārām harf mi-zanand.* — Shirazis speak very calmly.  
  Spoken: شیرازیا خیلی یواش حرف می‌زنن. *Shirāzi-yā kheyli yavāsh harf mi-zanan.*
- هر شهری لهجه‌ی خودش را دارد.  
  *har shahr-i lahje-ye khod-ash rā dārad.* — Every city has its own accent.  
  Spoken: هر شهری لهجه‌ی خودشو داره. *har shahr-i lahje-ye khod-esho dāre.*
- در تلویزیون بیشتر مجری‌ها با لهجه‌ی تهرانی حرف می‌زنند.  
  *dar televiziyon bishtar-e mojri-hā bā lahje-ye tehrāni harf mi-zanand.* — On television most presenters speak with a Tehrani accent.  
  _Local radio and TV stations in the provinces also broadcast in regional accents and languages._

### The other languages of Iran

Many Iranians grow up with a mother tongue other than Persian and learn Persian at school, so that by adulthood they are fully bilingual. These are **languages**, not accents of Persian. Some are close relatives of Persian (the **Iranian** languages: Kurdish, Lori, Gilaki, Mazandarani, Baluchi); others belong to entirely different families – **Turkic** (Azerbaijani, Turkmen, Qashqai), **Semitic** (Arabic, and the Neo-Aramaic of the Assyrian Christians) and Armenian, a branch of Indo-European of its own.

The table is a simple map, not a census. There are also smaller languages – Talysh and Tati in the north-west, Hawrami in the Kurdish mountains, the Turkic of the Qashqai nomads – and the borders between them are blurred: many villages are home to two languages at once.

| Language | Family | Mainly spoken in | Good to know |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ترکی آذربایجانی (*torki-ye āzarbāyjāni*) | Turkic | the north-west: East and West Azerbaijan, Ardabil, Zanjan; also many people in Tehran | After Persian, the most widely spoken language in Iran. 'How are you?' is *Necəsən?* |
| کردی (*kordi*) | Iranian (north-western) | the west: Kurdistan, Kermanshah, West Azerbaijan, Ilam; Kurmanji also in North Khorasan | Several varieties: Sorani around Sanandaj and Mahabad, Southern Kurdish around Kermanshah, Kurmanji in the north. 'How are you?' in Sorani: *Choni?* |
| گیلکی (*gilaki*) | Iranian (Caspian) | Gilan: Rasht, Lahijan | Close to Mazandarani; famous for its songs. |
| مازندرانی (*māzandarāni*) | Iranian (Caspian) | Mazandaran | Also called Tabari, after Tabarestan, the old name of the province. |
| لری (*lori*) | Iranian (south-western) | Lorestan and the Zagros; Bakhtiari is a close relative | The nearest relative of Persian in this table. |
| بلوچی (*baluchi*) | Iranian (north-western) | Sistan and Baluchestan | Also spoken in Pakistan, Afghanistan and Oman. |
| عربی (*arabi*) | Semitic | Khuzestan and the coast of the Persian Gulf | Close to the Arabic of southern Iraq; quite unlike the Arabic words in Persian. |
| ترکمنی (*torkamani*) | Turkic | Golestan (Gonbad-e Kavus, Bandar Torkaman) and North Khorasan | Close to the language of Turkmenistan. |
| ارمنی (*armani*) | Indo-European (a branch of its own) | Tehran, Isfahan's New Julfa, Tabriz, Urmia | Armenians have lived in Iran for many centuries and have their own schools and churches. 'Hello' is *Barev!* |

- در تبریز بیشتر مردم ترکی آذربایجانی حرف می‌زنند.  
  *dar Tabriz bishtar-e mardom torki-ye āzarbāyjāni harf mi-zanand.* — In Tabriz most people speak Azerbaijani Turkish.
- مادربزرگم فقط گیلکی و کمی فارسی بلد است.  
  *mādar-bozorg-am faghat gilaki o kam-i fārsi balad ast.* — My grandmother knows only Gilaki and a little Persian.  
  Spoken: مادربزرگم فقط گیلکی و یه کم فارسی بلده. *mādar-bozorg-am faghat gilaki o ye kam fārsi balade.*
- کردی و فارسی هر دو از زبان‌های ایرانی‌اند.  
  *kordi o fārsi har do az zabān-hā-ye irāni-and.* — Kurdish and Persian are both Iranian languages.
- ارمنی‌های اصفهان در محله‌ی جلفا زندگی می‌کنند.  
  *armani-hā-ye Esfahān dar mahalle-ye Jolfā zendegi mi-konand.* — The Armenians of Isfahan live in the Julfa quarter.  
  _Shah Abbas brought them from Julfa on the Aras river in the early 1600s; the Vank Cathedral stands in New Julfa._
- در خانه به لری حرف می‌زنیم و در مدرسه به فارسی.  
  *dar khāne be lori harf mi-zanim o dar madrese be fārsi.* — At home we speak Lori, and at school Persian.  
  Spoken: تو خونه لری حرف می‌زنیم، تو مدرسه فارسی. *tu khune lori harf mi-zanim, tu madrese fārsi.*
- عربی خوزستان به عربی جنوب عراق نزدیک است.  
  *arabi-ye Khuzestān be arabi-ye jonub-e Erāgh nazdik ast.* — The Arabic of Khuzestan is close to the Arabic of southern Iraq.
- بلوچی را در ایران، پاکستان و افغانستان حرف می‌زنند.  
  *baluchi rā dar Irān, Pākestān o Afghānestān harf mi-zanand.* — Baluchi is spoken in Iran, Pakistan and Afghanistan.

### 'What are you doing?' around the map

Here is one everyday question as you might hear it in different places. The Persian accents use the **same words** – what changes is the tune and a few sounds, which no spelling can show – so the transliterations below are **approximate**. The last rows are not accents but other languages, added so you can recognise them.

| Where | Approximately | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| written Persian | چه کار می‌کنی؟ (*che kār mi-koni?*) | the book form |
| Tehran | چی‌کار می‌کنی؟ (*chi-kār mi-koni?*) or داری چی‌کار می‌کنی؟ (*dāri chi-kār mi-koni?*) | quick; *dāri* adds 'right now' |
| Isfahan | *chi-kār mikoniii?* | same words, a long rise and fall on the last word (approximate) |
| Shiraz | *chi-kār mikoni, kāku?* | slower and warmer, often with *kāku* for a friend (approximate) |
| Mashhad | *chi-kār mikoni?* | same words, with a rounder *ā* in *kār* (approximate) |
| Yazd, Kerman | *chi-kār mikoni?* | same words with the local tune; vowels may sound closer to the written ones (approximate) |
| Kabul (Dari) | *chi kār mēkuni?* | mē- for mi-; see lesson 6 |
| Dushanbe (Tajik) | Чӣ кор мекунӣ? *chī kor mekunī?* | 'right now': Чӣ кор карда истодаӣ? |
| Tabriz – Azerbaijani Turkish | *Nə edirsən?* | another language (approximate) |
| Sanandaj – Kurdish (Sorani) | *Chi dekeyt?* | another language (approximate) |

- چه کار می‌کنی؟  
  *che kār mi-koni?* — What are you doing?  
  Spoken: چی‌کار می‌کنی؟ *chi-kār mi-koni?*
- داری چه کار می‌کنی؟  
  *dāri che kār mi-koni?* — What are you doing right now?  
  Spoken: داری چی‌کار می‌کنی؟ *dāri chi-kār mi-koni?*
- این روزها چه کار می‌کنی؟  
  *in ruz-hā che kār mi-koni?* — What are you up to these days?  
  Spoken: این روزا چی‌کار می‌کنی؟ *in ruzā chi-kār mi-koni?*
- هیچ کاری نمی‌کنم، استراحت می‌کنم.  
  *hich kār-i nemi-konam, esterāhat mi-konam.* — Nothing, I'm resting.  
  Spoken: هیچ‌کاری نمی‌کنم، دارم استراحت می‌کنم. *hich-kār-i nemi-konam, dāram esterāhat mi-konam.*
- دارم برای امتحان درس می‌خوانم.  
  *dāram barā-ye emtehān dars mi-khānam.* — I'm studying for an exam.  
  Spoken: دارم واسه امتحان درس می‌خونم. *dāram vāse emtehān dars mi-khunam.*
- شما در اصفهان چه کار می‌کنید؟  
  *shomā dar Esfahān che kār mi-konid?* — What do you do in Isfahan? (What's your work there?)  
  Spoken: شما اصفهان چی‌کار می‌کنین؟ *shomā Esfahān chi-kār mi-konin?*
- کاکو، چه کار می‌کنی؟ بیا چای بخوریم.  
  *kāku, che kār mi-koni? biyā chāy bokhorim.* — What are you up to, mate? Come and have some tea.  
  Spoken: کاکو، چی‌کار می‌کنی؟ بیا چایی بخوریم. *kāku, chi-kār mi-koni? biyā chāyi bokhorim.*

### Two languages at home

_Tom's classmate Mahsa grew up in Tabriz. He overheard her on the phone to her mother yesterday and asks about it over coffee._

- **Tom:** مهسا، دیروز شنیدم با مادرت تلفنی ترکی حرف می‌زدی.  
  *Mahsā, diruz shenidam bā mādar-at telefoni torki harf mi-zadi.* — Mahsa, yesterday I heard you speaking Turkish to your mother on the phone.
- **Mahsa:** بله، زبان مادری من ترکی آذربایجانی است. در خانه همیشه ترکی حرف می‌زنیم.  
  *bale, zabān-e mādari-ye man torki-ye āzarbāyjāni ast. dar khāne hamishe torki harf mi-zanim.* — Yes, my mother tongue is Azerbaijani Turkish. At home we always speak Turkish.  
  Spoken: آره، زبون مادریم ترکیه. تو خونه همیشه ترکی حرف می‌زنیم. *āre, zabun-e mādari-yam torki-ye. tu khune hamishe torki harf mi-zanim.*
- **Tom:** پس فارسی را کجا یاد گرفتی؟  
  *pas fārsi rā kojā yād gerefti?* — So where did you learn Persian?  
  Spoken: پس فارسی رو کجا یاد گرفتی؟ *pas fārsi ro kojā yād gerefti?*
- **Mahsa:** در مدرسه، و از تلویزیون. وقتی هفت سالم بود، فارسی‌ام کمی لهجه داشت.  
  *dar madrese, o az televiziyon. vaghti haft sāl-am bud, fārsi-am kam-i lahje dāsht.* — At school, and from television. When I was seven, my Persian had a bit of an accent.  
  Spoken: تو مدرسه، از تلویزیون. وقتی هفت سالم بود، فارسیم یه کم لهجه داشت. *tu madrese, az televiziyon. vaghti haft sāl-am bud, fārsi-yam ye kam lahje dāsht.*
- **Tom:** هنوز هم لهجه داری؟ من که چیزی نمی‌شنوم.  
  *hanuz ham lahje dāri? man ke chiz-i nemi-shenavam.* — Do you still have an accent? I can't hear anything.  
  Spoken: هنوزم لهجه داری؟ من که چیزی نمی‌شنوم. *hanuz-am lahje dāri? man ke chiz-i nemi-shenavam.*
- **Mahsa:** تهرانی‌ها می‌شنوند! وقتی هیجان‌زده می‌شوم، آهنگ ترکی وارد فارسی‌ام می‌شود.  
  *tehrāni-hā mi-shenavand! vaghti hayajān-zade mi-shavam, āhang-e torki vāred-e fārsi-am mi-shavad.* — Tehranis can! When I get excited, the Turkish tune creeps into my Persian.  
  Spoken: تهرونیا می‌شنون! وقتی هیجان‌زده می‌شم، آهنگ ترکی میاد تو فارسیم. *tehruni-yā mi-shenavan! vaghti hayajān-zade mi-sham, āhang-e torki miyād tu fārsi-yam.*
- **Tom:** یک جمله‌ی ترکی یادم بده.  
  *yek jomle-ye torki yād-am bede.* — Teach me a sentence in Turkish.  
  Spoken: یه جمله‌ی ترکی یادم بده. *ye jomle-ye torki yād-am bede.*
- **Mahsa:** اول یاد بگیر حال کسی را بپرسی؛ معنی‌اش می‌شود «چطوری؟».  
  *avval yād begir hāl-e kas-i rā beporsi; ma'ni-ash mi-shavad «chetowri?».* — First learn to ask how someone is – it means 'How are you?'.  
  Spoken: اول یاد بگیر حال یکی رو بپرسی؛ معنیش می‌شه «چطوری؟». *avval yād begir hāl-e yek-i ro beporsi; ma'ni-sh mi-she «chetowri?».*  
  _She teaches him *Necəsən?* 'How are you?' and the answer *Yaxşıyam* 'I'm fine'._
- **Tom:** اگر در تبریز با کسی فارسی حرف بزنم، ناراحت نمی‌شود؟  
  *agar dar Tabriz bā kas-i fārsi harf bezanam, nārāhat nemi-shavad?* — If I speak Persian to someone in Tabriz, won't they mind?  
  Spoken: اگه تو تبریز با یکی فارسی حرف بزنم، ناراحت نمی‌شه؟ *age tu Tabriz bā yek-i fārsi harf bezanam, nārāhat nemi-she?*
- **Mahsa:** نه بابا! همه فارسی بلدند. ولی اگر یک کلمه ترکی بگویی، دلشان را به دست می‌آوری.  
  *na bābā! hame fārsi baladand. vali agar yek kalame torki beguyi, del-eshān rā be dast mi-āvari.* — Not at all! Everyone knows Persian. But if you say one word of Turkish, you'll win their hearts.  
  Spoken: نه بابا! همه فارسی بلدن. ولی اگه یه کلمه ترکی بگی، دلشونو به دست میاری. *na bābā! hame fārsi baladan. vali age ye kalame torki begi, del-eshuno be dast miyāri.*
- **Tom:** پس یاد می‌گیرم. ولی لهجه‌ی تبریزی را تقلید نمی‌کنم!  
  *pas yād mi-giram. vali lahje-ye tabrizi rā taghlid nemi-konam!* — Then I'll learn. But I won't imitate the Tabriz accent!  
  Spoken: پس یاد می‌گیرم. ولی لهجه‌ی تبریزی رو تقلید نمی‌کنم! *pas yād mi-giram. vali lahje-ye tabrizi ro taghlid nemi-konam!*
- **Mahsa:** آفرین. تقلید لهجه برای خنده همیشه جالب نیست.  
  *āfarin. taghlid-e lahje barā-ye khande hamishe jāleb nist.* — Good for you. Imitating an accent for laughs isn't always funny.  
  Spoken: آفرین. ادای لهجه رو درآوردن همیشه بامزه نیست. *āfarin. adā-ye lahje ro dar-āvardan hamishe bā-maze nist.*

### Many mother tongues, one shared language

Walk through any Iranian university and you will hear students switch languages in mid-conversation: Azerbaijani Turkish with a friend from Ardabil, Kurdish on the phone to Sanandaj, Persian in the seminar. For most of them, Persian is not a foreign language but a second home – learnt at school, used at work, and often the language of their favourite poetry.

The regional languages have rich cultures of their own. Kurdish music, with its frame drums, is known all over Iran; Gilaki and Mazandarani folk songs are sung at weddings along the Caspian; Lori and Bakhtiari laments and dances belong to the Zagros. The poet Shahriyar of Tabriz wrote some of the best-loved modern Persian ghazals – and also *Heydar Babaya Salam*, a long poem in Azerbaijani Turkish about his childhood village that is recited in Iran, Azerbaijan and Turkey alike.

A few points of tact:

- Don't call Kurdish or Azerbaijani a 'dialect of Persian'. They are languages, and people care.
- If friends switch into their mother tongue at home, they are not shutting you out; ask them to teach you a greeting.
- Iranian comedy has a long tradition of jokes about towns and accents. Many people find them hurtful – it is wiser not to tell them yourself.
- شما اهل کجا هستید؟ (*shomā ahl-e kojā hastid?*, ‘where are you from?’) is a normal, friendly question in Iran. Asking it straight after someone has spoken can sound like a comment on their accent, though – let the conversation get there.

### Tips

- Learn Tehrani as your spoken model: it is understood everywhere and it is what you hear in films. But train your ear on other accents – podcasts and radio from Isfahan, Shiraz or Mashhad are a good start.
- When you can't follow someone's accent, say so politely: ببخشید، می‌شود کمی آرام‌تر بگویید؟ (*bebakhshid, mi-shavad kam-i ārām-tar beguyid?*, ‘sorry, could you say it a little more slowly?’) People will happily switch to a more standard Persian for you.
- A good way to tell an accent from a language: if you understand most of the words but the music is different, it is an accent; if you understand almost nothing, it is probably another language.
- ترکی (*torki*) in Iran usually means Azerbaijani Turkish, not the Turkish of Turkey (which Iranians call ترکی استانبولی (*torki-ye estānbuli*)). The two are close relatives and largely understand each other.
- Regional words travel. کاکو (*kāku*) is Shirazi, but Tehranis use it jokingly too; much of what you think of as 'Tehrani' slang came from other cities.
- In Afghanistan and Tajikistan Persian has its own standards – see the previous lesson. Kabul and Dushanbe are not 'accents of Tehran'.

### Accent or language?

**Choose the right answer**

1. Which of these is a separate language, not an accent of Persian? — Isfahani / Shirazi / Kurdish
2. Which family does Azerbaijani Turkish belong to? — Iranian / Turkic / Semitic
3. Which of these languages is the closest relative of Persian? — Lori / Turkmen / Arabic
4. What do Iranians notice first about the Isfahani accent? — Its Arabic grammar / Its melody – a long rise and fall / That it has no verbs
5. گویش (*guyesh*) usually means… — a dialect or local speech, often far from standard Persian / the written language / a foreign accent
6. Where do many of Isfahan's Armenians live? — in the Julfa quarter / in the bazaar / on Si-o-se-pol

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Kurdish — Kurdish is an Iranian language of its own, related to Persian but not mutually intelligible with it.
2. Turkic — Like Turkmen and the Turkish of Turkey, it is a Turkic language.
3. Lori — Lori is a south-western Iranian language like Persian itself; Turkmen is Turkic and Arabic Semitic.
4. Its melody – a long rise and fall — The 'singing' tune, with the end of the phrase stretched, is its trademark.
5. a dialect or local speech, often far from standard Persian — لهجه is an accent of Persian; گویش is used for local speech that is further away, such as Gilaki.
6. in the Julfa quarter — New Julfa, with the Vank Cathedral, was founded by Armenians brought by Shah Abbas in the early 1600s.

</details>
