# One, two, three: numbers, ages and prices
## یک، دو، سه
*yek, do, se* — Beginner (A1), lesson 4. [Open this lesson with audio and interactive exercises](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/course/a1-04-numbers.html)

Numbers open up daily life: how old you are, your phone number, the price of a book, how many people are in the room. In this lesson you learn to count from zero to a thousand, to ask and give ages and phone numbers, and to understand the prices you hear in an Iranian shop – where even a glass of tea costs thousands.

**In this lesson you will learn to:**

- count from 0 to 1,000
- ask and say how old someone is: chand sāl dārid?
- give a phone number the Iranian way
- ask prices with chand ast? and understand tomans
- count things with tā and a singular noun

### Signing up for an evening class

_Tom wants more practice, so he is signing up for an evening Persian course at a language school in Tehran. Ms Ahmadi, the secretary, fills in a form for him._

- **Ms Ahmadi:** خب، اسم شما تام است. چند سال دارید؟  
  *khob, esm-e shomā Tām ast. chand sāl dārid?* — Right, your name is Tom. How old are you?
- **Tom:** بیست و چهار سال دارم.  
  *bist o chahār sāl dāram.* — I'm twenty-four.
- **Ms Ahmadi:** شماره‌ی تلفن شما چند است؟  
  *shomāre-ye telefon-e shomā chand ast?* — What is your phone number?  
  Spoken: شماره‌ی تلفن شما چنده؟ *shomāre-ye telefon-e shomā chand-e?*
- **Tom:** صفر نهصد و دوازده، سیصد و چهل و پنج، شصت و هفت، هشتاد و نه.  
  *sefr nohsad o davāzdah, sisad o chehel o panj, shast o haft, hashtād o noh.* — 0912 – 345 – 67 – 89.
- **Ms Ahmadi:** ببخشید، یک بار دیگر، لطفاً.  
  *bebakhshid, yek bār-e digar, lotfan.* — Sorry – once more, please.  
  Spoken: ببخشید، یه بار دیگه، لطفاً. *bebakhshid, ye bār-e dige, lotfan.*
- **Tom:** صفر، نه، یک، دو… سه، چهار، پنج… شش، هفت… هشت، نه.  
  *sefr, noh, yek, do… se, chahār, panj… shesh, haft… hasht, noh.* — Zero, nine, one, two… three, four, five… six, seven… eight, nine.
- **Ms Ahmadi:** ممنون. کلاس شما دوازده دانشجو دارد.  
  *mamnun. kelās-e shomā davāzdah dāneshju dārad.* — Thanks. Your class has twelve students.
- **Tom:** ببخشید، این کتاب‌ها برای کلاس است؟  
  *bebakhshid, in ketāb-hā barā-ye kelās ast?* — Excuse me, are these books for the class?
- **Ms Ahmadi:** بله، این دو تا کتاب برای کلاس شماست.  
  *bale, in do tā ketāb barā-ye kelās-e shomā-st.* — Yes, these two books are for your class.
- **Tom:** قیمت کتاب‌ها چند است؟  
  *gheymat-e ketāb-hā chand ast?* — How much are the books?  
  Spoken: قیمت کتاب‌ها چنده؟ *gheymat-e ketāb-hā chand-e?*
- **Ms Ahmadi:** هر کتاب دویست و پنجاه هزار تومان است.  
  *har ketāb devist o panjāh hezār tomān ast.* — Each book is two hundred and fifty thousand tomans.  
  Spoken: هر کتاب دویست و پنجاه تومنه. *har ketāb devist o panjāh toman-e.*
- **Tom:** پس دو کتاب پانصد هزار تومان است. بفرمایید.  
  *pas do ketāb pānsad hezār tomān ast. befarmāyid.* — So two books are five hundred thousand tomans. Here you are.
- **Ms Ahmadi:** متشکرم. کلاس شما در اتاق شماره‌ی هفت است.  
  *motshakker-am. kelās-e shomā dar otāgh-e shomāre-ye haft ast.* — Thank you. Your class is in room number seven.

### Counting: Persian numbers are your cousins

Say the Persian numbers from one to ten aloud and several will sound familiar: دو (*do*, ‘two’), سه (*se*, ‘three’), شش (*shesh*, ‘six’), هفت (*haft*, ‘seven’) (Greek *hepta*), هشت (*hasht*, ‘eight’) (Latin *octo*), نه (*noh*, ‘nine’), ده (*dah*, ‘ten’) (Greek *deka*). Persian and English are distant relatives in the Indo-European family, and numbers are where the family likeness shows most. *Punjab* is Persian too: پنج (*panj*, ‘five’) + آب (*āb*, ‘water’), the land of five rivers.

The question word for anything you can count is چند (*chand*, ‘how many, how much’). Persians use it for ages – چند سال دارید؟ (*chand sāl dārid?*, ‘how old are you?’), literally *how many years do you have?* – for phone numbers – شماره‌ی تلفن شما چند است؟ (*shomāre-ye telefon-e shomā chand ast?*, ‘what is your phone number?’) – and, pointing at something in a shop, for prices: این چند است؟ (*in chand ast?*, ‘how much is this?’) In speech *chand ast* shrinks to *chand-e?*

When you don't catch a number, say یک بار دیگر، لطفاً (*yek bār-e digar, lotfan*, ‘once more, please’).

### From 11 to 100

The numbers 11 to 19 are a unit plus *-dah* (ten), with a few sound changes you simply learn by heart: یازده (*yāzdah*, ‘11’), دوازده (*davāzdah*, ‘12’), سیزده (*sizdah*, ‘13’)… Then come the tens.

For 21, 35, 99 and so on, put و (*o*, ‘and’) between the tens and the units – like old English *four-and-twenty*, but the other way round: بیست و یک (*bist o yek*, ‘21’), سی و پنج (*si o panj*, ‘35’), نود و نه (*navad o noh*, ‘99’). The *o* is short and unstressed, so *bist-o-yek* runs together like one word. In writing, و stands apart with a space on each side.

### Hundreds, thousands – and prices

Hundreds are a unit + صد (*sad*), with three irregular forms to learn: دویست (*devist*, ‘200’), سیصد (*sisad*, ‘300’) and پانصد (*pānsad*, ‘500’). A thousand, هزار (*hezār*), counts like a noun: دو هزار (*do hezār*, ‘2,000’), ده هزار (*dah hezār*, ‘10,000’), صد هزار (*sad hezār*, ‘100,000’).

Big numbers are said from left to right with *o* between every part: هزار و چهارصد و پنج (*hezār o chahārsad o panj*, ‘1405’) – the Iranian year that began in March 2026.

Prices in Iran are big numbers, so you will need *hezār* every day. Ask with چند است؟ (*chand ast?*, ‘how much is it?’) and the answer comes back with است (*ast*) at the end: هر کتاب دویست و پنجاه هزار تومان است (*har ketāb devist o panjāh hezār tomān ast*, ‘each book is 250,000 tomans’). هر (*har*) means *each, every*.

### How many? How old? – tā, the singular and dāshtan

After a number a noun stays **singular** – you met this in lesson 3: دو کتاب (*do ketāb*, ‘two books’), دوازده دانشجو (*davāzdah dāneshju*, ‘twelve students’). Never add *-hā* after a number.

In everyday speech Iranians slip a little counting word, تا (*tā*), between the number and the noun: دو تا کتاب (*do tā ketāb*), سه تا چای (*se tā chāy*). It adds no meaning – think of *two **pieces of** cake* – and you can leave it out, especially in writing. For people, نفر (*nafar*, ‘person’) does the same job: پنج نفر (*panj nafar*, ‘five people’).

To ask *how many?* use چند (*chand*) with a singular noun, or چند تا (*chand tā*): چند تا کتاب؟ (*chand tā ketāb?*, ‘how many books?’)

**Age.** Persians *have* their years: بیست سال دارم (*bist sāl dāram*, ‘I am twenty’) is literally *I have twenty years*. The verb is داشتن (*dāshtan*, ‘to have’) – the *dārad* you met in *kelās-e mā do panjare dārad*. Here are its forms; lesson 5 uses them for your family.

| تو چند سال داری؟ (*to chand sāl dāri?*) | dāri | how old are you? (friend) |
| او سی سال دارد (*u si sāl dārad*) | dārad | he / she is thirty |
| ما هجده سال داریم (*mā hejdah sāl dārim*) | dārim | we are eighteen |
| شما چند سال دارید؟ (*shomā chand sāl dārid?*) | dārid | how old are you? (polite) |
| آن‌ها ده سال دارند (*ān-hā dah sāl dārand*) | dārand | they are ten |

### Persian digits, tomans and rials

Look at any price tag, bus or banknote in Iran and you will see the Persian digits: ۰ (*sefr*) ۱ (*yek*) ۲ (*do*) ۳ (*se*) ۴ (*chahār*) ۵ (*panj*) ۶ (*shesh*) ۷ (*haft*) ۸ (*hasht*) ۹ (*noh*). Two surprises: zero is just a dot (۰), and the round shape that looks like a zero is five (۵). And although Persian is written from right to left, numbers run **left to right**, just like ours: ۲۵ is 25.

The currency is the **rial** (ریال (*riyāl*)), but almost nobody counts in rials. People think and speak in **tomans** – one toman is ten rials – so a price tag of 500,000 rials is *panjāh hezār tomān*, fifty thousand tomans. And because prices are so large, people often drop the thousands altogether: if a taxi driver says پنجاه تومان (*panjāh tomān*), he means fifty *thousand*. When in doubt, ask – every visitor, and many Iranians, have been confused at least once.

The word *tomān* itself is old: it comes from Mongolian *tümen*, 'ten thousand'.

### Tips

- Persian digits are written **left to right**, even in the middle of a right-to-left sentence: ۱۴۰۵ is 1405, with the ۱ on the left.
- Don't confuse **۰** (zero, a small dot) with **۵** (five, a rounded shape like an upside-down heart). In handwriting, ۲ and ۳ also look alike – ۳ has one more tooth.
- In speech, **chahār** is usually *chār* and **shesh** is *shish*: *chār-dah*, *shish sāl*. Write the full forms.
- Numbers are stressed on the last syllable – *yāz-DAH*, *haf-TĀD*, *pan-JĀH* – but the little *o* in *bist o yek* is never stressed.
- Iranians read phone numbers in groups: ۰۹۱۲ is *sefr nohsad o davāzdah*, 'zero, nine hundred and twelve'. If it is too fast, ask for the digits one by one, as Ms Ahmadi did.

**Say it in Persian**

1. How old are you? (polite)
2. I am thirty years old.
3. Two teas, please.
4. How much is this book?
5. There are twelve people in our class.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. شما چند سال دارید؟ — *shomā chand sāl dārid?*
2. من سی سال دارم. — *man si sāl dāram.*
3. دو تا چای، لطفاً. — *do tā chāy, lotfan.*
4. این کتاب چند است؟ — *in ketāb chand ast?*
5. در کلاس ما دوازده نفر هستند. — *dar kelās-e mā davāzdah nafar hastand.*

</details>
