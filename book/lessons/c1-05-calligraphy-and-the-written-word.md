# Calligraphy and the written word
## خوشنویسی و کلمه‌ی مکتوب
*khoshnevisi va kalame-ye maktub* — Advanced (C1), lesson 5. [Open this lesson with audio and interactive exercises](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/course/c1-05-calligraphy-and-the-written-word.html)

In Iran beautiful writing is not decoration added to a text – it is one of the highest arts, practised by kings and craftsmen, children and grandparents. This lesson introduces the five great scripts and what each is used for, teaches you to read Nastaliq with its sloping words, stacked letters and wandering dots, explains the reed pen, the ink and the long discipline of siyāh-mashq, meets three famous masters, and shows how to approach a decorative inscription. It ends with a visit to a calligraphy class.

**In this lesson you will learn to:**

- name the scripts کوفی، نسخ، ثلث، نستعلیق، شکسته‌نستعلیق and say what each is used for
- read short Nastaliq texts: follow the slope, untangle stacked letters and match loose dots
- talk about the tools and practice of calligraphy: قلم نی، مرکب، سرمشق، سیاه‌مشق
- say who Mir Ali Tabrizi, Mir Emad and Gholam-Hossein Amirkhani were
- work out where a decorative inscription begins, what script it uses and what it probably says

### The script, the soul of the word

_A short history of Persian calligraphy in the style of a museum guide. Look out for the verbs of creation – پدید آمدن، پدید آوردن، تدوین کردن، رساندن – and the many passives (نوشته شده‌اند، برگزیده می‌شد) typical of writing about the arts._

- خوشنویسی در ایران تنها هنر نوشتن نیست، بلکه بخشی از فرهنگ و آداب زندگی است.  
  *khoshnevisi dar Irān tanhā honar-e neveshtan nist, balke bakhsh-i az farhang o ādāb-e zendegi ast.* — In Iran calligraphy is not just the art of writing; it is part of the culture and the customs of life.
- پس از اسلام، ایرانیان الفبای عربی را با افزودن چهار حرف «پ»، «چ»، «ژ» و «گ» برای زبان فارسی به کار گرفتند.

  *ghor'ān-hā-ye kohan va katibe-hā-ye besyār-i az banā-hā-ye tārikhi bā khatt-e kufi neveshte shode-and.* — Early Qurans and the inscriptions of many historic buildings are written in Kufic.
- سپس خط نسخ پدید آمد که روشن و خوانا بود و هنوز در چاپ کتاب‌ها به کار می‌رود.

- خط ثلث، با حرف‌های بلند و درشت، بیشتر برای کتیبه‌ی مسجدها و سرلوحه‌ها برگزیده می‌شد.

- بنا بر روایت، میرعلی تبریزی نخستین کسی بود که قواعد این خط را تدوین کرد.

- نستعلیق از آمیختن دو خط «نسخ» و «تعلیق» پدید آمد و نامش نیز از همین آمیزش است.

- او سال‌ها «سیاه‌مشق» می‌کند، یعنی یک حرف یا یک واژه را آن‌قدر تکرار می‌کند که کاغذ سیاه شود.

- امروز نیز در بیشتر شهرهای ایران کلاس‌های خوشنویسی برپاست و هزاران نفر در آن‌ها شرکت می‌کنند.

### Why the script matters

A Persian reader meets several scripts every day without thinking about it. The newspaper, the school textbook and the phone are in **Naskh**-based type; the title of a poetry book, a wedding invitation, a shop sign or the label of a box of pastries is very likely in **Nasta'liq**; the band of writing round a mosque dome is in **Thuluth** or **Kufic**; and an old family deed or a Qajar letter may be in **Shekasteh**. Being able to read only the first of these is like reading print but not handwriting.

The good news: the letters are the same. What changes is **proportion** (how big each part of a letter is), **direction** (Nasta'liq words slope downwards from right to left), **arrangement** (letters may sit on top of one another) and **dots** (placed where they look best, not always where you expect). This lesson gives you the tools to cope with all four.

Persian has a rich vocabulary for the craft: خوشنویسی (*khoshnevisi*, ‘calligraphy, literally 'beautiful-writing'’), خوش‌خط (*khosh-khatt*, ‘having good handwriting’), بدخط (*bad-khatt*, ‘having bad handwriting’) – and the highest praise a calligrapher can receive, استاد (*ostād*, ‘master’).

### Five scripts and what they are for

The scripts form a family tree. **Kufic** is the angular ancestor; **Naskh** and **Thuluth** are the great rounded scripts of the Arabic tradition; **Ta'liq**, a Persian chancery hand, was blended with Naskh to make **Nasta'liq**, the Persian script par excellence; and **Shekasteh** ('broken') is Nasta'liq written at speed. Each has its jobs, and Iranians notice when the wrong script is used – a Kufic wedding invitation would be as odd as a gothic-letter email.

Learn to say what something is written in: به خط نستعلیق (*be khatt-e nasta'ligh*, ‘in Nasta'liq’), با خط کوفی تزئین شده است (*bā khatt-e kufi taz'in shode ast*, ‘it is decorated with Kufic’).

| Script | Reading | What it looks like | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| کوفی | kufi | angular, straight strokes, few curves | early Qurans, coins, pottery; brick and tile inscriptions (the square 'brick' Kufic, بنایی) |
| نسخ | naskh | rounded, even, sitting on one line | copying the Quran and books; the model for printed and digital type |
| ثلث | sols | tall, majestic, letters interlaced in tiers | monumental inscriptions in mosques and shrines, headings |
| تعلیق | ta'ligh | hanging, joined, chancery hand | official documents of the 13th–14th centuries; ancestor of Nasta'liq |
| نستعلیق | nasta'ligh | sloping, flowing, stacked, generous bowls | Persian poetry and books, titles, invitations, signs, art pieces |
| شکسته‌نستعلیق | shekaste-nasta'ligh | fast, compressed; letters joined that are normally separate | letters, decrees and documents (17th–19th c.); today an art script |

  *in kāse-ye sofāli bā khatt-e kufi taz'in shode ast.* — This pottery bowl is decorated with Kufic script.
- خط کوفی بنایی را با آجر و کاشی روی دیوارها می‌ساختند.

- تابلوی این مغازه را خوشنویسی به خط نستعلیق نوشته است.

### How to read Nasta'liq

Nasta'liq is built for the eye, not for speed-reading, and even educated Iranians slow down in front of a dense piece. Its rules are few, though, and once you know them the script stops looking like a puzzle.

1. **The slope.** Each word, or group of joined letters, starts high on the right and runs down to the left; the next word starts high again. A line looks like a row of little staircases. Read each group from its top right corner.
2. **Stacking.** Within a word, letters are placed on top of one another, and in a composed piece a short word may be lifted above a long one to fill space. Follow the joins of the pen, not an imaginary baseline.
3. **Loose dots.** Dots are placed where they balance the composition – sometimes far from their letter, sometimes shared or merged. Three dots are often written as one small curved stroke. Count dots; don't trust their position.
4. **Stretches and bowls.** One letter per line is often stretched very wide (a کشیده (*keshide*)), and final ن، ی، س، ل end in deep round bowls (دایره (*dāyere*)). The stretch has no meaning; the bowl tells you a word has ended.
5. **Context.** There are no vowel marks and spacing is irregular. Most Nasta'liq pieces are well-known poems – knowing the poem is half of reading it.

| Feature | What you see | How to cope |
| --- | --- | --- |
| slope | each word runs down from upper right to lower left | start every word at its highest right-hand point |
| stacking (ترکیب) | letters and short words sit on top of each other | follow the joins, not the line |
| loose dots | dots float away from their letters; three dots as one stroke | match dots by number, then by nearest letter |
| stretch (کشیده) | one letter – ب، س، ک، ف – pulled very wide | read it as a normal letter; it only fills the line |
| bowl (دایره) | deep round final ن، ی، س، ل | the bowl marks the end of a word |
| returning ye (یای برگشته) | final ی curls back under the word to the right | it is a final ی (often an ezafe or ending), not a new word |
| no vowels, loose spacing | words run together; no ZWNJ | use context – and knowledge of the poem |

  *dar nasta'ligh, har kalame az bālā va rāst be pāyin va chap mi-āyad.* — In Nasta'liq every word comes down from the top right to the bottom left.
- حروف این واژه روی هم سوار شده‌اند.  
  *horuf-e in vāzhe ru-ye ham savār shode-and.* — The letters of this word are stacked on top of each other.

- نقطه‌ها گاه از حرف خود دور می‌افتند.  
  *noghte-hā gāh az harf-e khod dur mi-oftand.* — Sometimes the dots end up far from their letter.
- سه نقطه را اغلب به شکل یک خط کوتاه می‌نویسند.  
  *se noghte rā aghlab be shekl-e yek khatt-e kutāh mi-nevisand.* — Three dots are often written as one short stroke.
- کشیده‌ی «ک» در این سطر بسیار بلند است.  
  *keshide-ye «kāf» dar in satr besyār boland ast.* — The stretch of the kāf in this line is very long.
- یای برگشته در پایان واژه به سمت راست بازمی‌گردد.  
  *yā-ye bargashte dar pāyān-e vāzhe be samt-e rāst bāz-mi-gardad.* — The returning ye at the end of a word curls back to the right.
- شاعر این بیت را می‌شناسم، پس خواندنش برایم آسان‌تر است.  
  *shā'er-e in beyt rā mi-shenāsam, pas khāndan-ash barā-yam āsān-tar ast.* — I know the poet of this verse, so it's easier for me to read.

### Reading a decorative inscription

Inscriptions on mosques, shrines, tiles and old houses are the hardest Persian (and Arabic) you will ever read – not because of the language but because the writing is composed like a picture. Professionals read them in a fixed order, and so can you.

The language matters as much as the script. **Thuluth and Kufic bands in religious buildings are usually Arabic** – Quranic verses, prayers, the names of God, the Prophet and the Imams. **Nasta'liq panels on tiles, in houses, gardens and tombs are usually Persian poetry.** So the first question is not *what does it say?* but *what is it likely to say?*

The signature formula is worth knowing: calligraphers sign with کتبه (*katabahu*, ‘(he) wrote it’) or حرره (*harrarahu*, ‘(he) penned it’) followed by their name, often with a humble title like العبد (*al-'abd*, ‘the servant’); craftsmen sign with عمل (*amal*, ‘the work of’). Dates are usually in the lunar Hijri calendar (قمری): subtract and convert carefully – ۱۰۲۵ قمری is AD 1616.

| Step | What to do | A useful Persian question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. find the start | panels start top right; bands round a dome or portal usually start at the right end | از کجا شروع می‌شود؟ (*az kojā shoru' mi-shavad?*, ‘where does it start?’) |
| 2. name the script | Thuluth or Kufic → probably Arabic; Nasta'liq → probably Persian | به چه خطی است؟ (*be che khatt-i ast?*, ‘what script is it in?’) |
| 3. find anchor words | the names of God, the Prophet and Ali; words like بسم; the signature کتبه | این کلمه چیست؟ (*in kalame chist?*, ‘what is this word?’) |
| 4. untangle the tiers | Thuluth stacks letters in two or three tiers; follow the joins of the pen | کدام حرف به کدام وصل است؟ (*kodām harf be kodām vasl ast?*, ‘which letter joins which?’) |
| 5. look for a date | digits or words near the signature; usually a lunar Hijri year | تاریخش چیست؟ (*tārikh-ash chist?*, ‘what is its date?’) |
| 6. guess, then check | think of the verse or poem it may be, and test your guess letter by letter | شعر از کیست؟ (*she'r az kist?*, ‘whose poem is it?’) |

- این کتیبه به خط ثلث و به زبان عربی است.  
  *in katibe be khatt-e sols va be zabān-e arabi ast.* — This inscription is in Thuluth script and in Arabic.
- کتیبه از سمت راست شروع می‌شود و دور گنبد می‌چرخد.  
  *katibe az samt-e rāst shoru' mi-shavad va dowr-e gonbad mi-charkhad.* — The inscription begins on the right and runs round the dome.
- نام خوشنویس معمولاً در پایان کتیبه و با واژه‌ی «کتبه» می‌آید.  
  *nām-e khoshnevis ma'mulan dar pāyān-e katibe va bā vāzhe-ye «katabahu» mi-āyad.* — The calligrapher's name usually comes at the end of the inscription, with the word katabahu, 'wrote it'.
- تاریخ کتیبه سال ۱۰۲۵ هجری قمری است.  
  *tārikh-e katibe sāl-e hezār-o-bist-o-panj-e hejri-ye ghamari ast.* — The inscription is dated 1025 of the lunar Hijri calendar (AD 1616).
- در این کاشی، بیتی از حافظ به خط نستعلیق نوشته شده است.  
  *dar in kāshi, beyt-i az Hāfez be khatt-e nasta'ligh neveshte shode ast.* — A verse of Hafez is written on this tile in Nasta'liq.
- اگر شعر را از پیش بدانید، خواندن کتیبه بسیار آسان‌تر می‌شود.  
  *agar she'r rā az pish bedānid, khāndan-e katibe besyār āsān-tar mi-shavad.* — If you already know the poem, reading the inscription becomes much easier.

### A visit to a calligraphy class

_Leila takes Tom to her Thursday calligraphy class with Ms Ahmadi. A narrative in written Persian, with a few spoken remarks in quotation marks kept in the written register._

- لیلا سه سال است که هر پنجشنبه به کلاس خوشنویسی خانم احمدی می‌رود.  
  *Leylā se sāl ast ke har panjshanbe be kelās-e khoshnevisi-ye khānom-e Ahmadi mi-ravad.* — For three years Leila has gone to Ms Ahmadi's calligraphy class every Thursday.
- این هفته تام را هم با خود برد.  
  *in hafte Tām rā ham bā khod bord.* — This week she took Tom with her.
- کلاس در اتاقی کوچک با پنجره‌های رنگی برگزار می‌شد و بوی مرکب در هوا بود.  
  *kelās dar otāgh-i kuchek bā panjare-hā-ye rangi bargozār mi-shod va bu-ye morakkab dar havā bud.* — The class was held in a small room with coloured windows, and the smell of ink hung in the air.
- شاگردان روی زمین نشسته بودند و کاغذ را روی زانو گذاشته بودند.  
  *shāgerdān ru-ye zamin neshaste budand va kāghaz rā ru-ye zānu gozāshte budand.* — The pupils were sitting on the floor with their paper resting on their knees.
- خانم احمدی نخست به تام نشان داد که چگونه قلم نی را با قلمتراش بتراشد و سر آن را قط بزند.  
  *khānom-e Ahmadi nakhost be Tām neshān dād ke chegune ghalam-e ney rā bā ghalam-tarāsh betarāshad va sar-e ān rā ghatt bezanad.* — First Ms Ahmadi showed Tom how to pare a reed pen with the penknife and cut its nib at an angle.
- سپس سرمشقی به او داد که فقط یک حرف داشت: «ب».  
  *sepas sarmashgh-i be u dād ke faghat yek harf dāsht: «be».* — Then she gave him a model sheet with just one letter on it: be.
- خانم احمدی گفت: «اندازه‌ی هر حرف را با نقطه می‌سنجیم.»  
  *khānom-e Ahmadi goft: «andāze-ye har harf rā bā noghte mi-sanjim.»* — Ms Ahmadi said, 'We measure the size of every letter in dots.'
- تام یک ساعت تمام فقط «ب» نوشت و کاغذش کم‌کم سیاه شد.  
  *Tām yek sā'at-e tamām faghat «be» nevesht va kāghaz-ash kam-kam siyāh shod.* — For a whole hour Tom wrote nothing but be, and little by little his paper turned black.
- لیلا خندید و گفت: «این همان سیاه‌مشق است؛ من در سال نخست هر حرف را هزار بار نوشتم.»  
  *Leylā khandid va goft: «in hamān siyāh-mashgh ast; man dar sāl-e nakhost har harf rā hezār bār neveshtam.»* — Leila laughed and said, 'That's siyāh-mashq. In my first year I wrote every letter a thousand times.'
- سپس خانم احمدی تصویری از قطعه‌ای اثر میرعماد را نشان داد.  
  *sepas khānom-e Ahmadi tasvir-i az ghet'e-i asar-e Mir-Emād rā neshān dād.* — Then Ms Ahmadi showed them a picture of a piece by Mir Emad.
- تام نمی‌توانست آن را بخواند، چون واژه‌ها روی هم سوار شده بودند و نقطه‌ها جای خود نبودند.  
  *Tām nemi-tavānest ān rā bekhānad, chon vāzhe-hā ru-ye ham savār shode budand va noghte-hā jā-ye khod na-budand.* — Tom couldn't read it, because the words were stacked on top of each other and the dots weren't in their places.
- خانم احمدی گفت: «نستعلیق را باید از بالا و راست، مثل آبی که از پله پایین می‌آید، خواند.»  
  *khānom-e Ahmadi goft: «nasta'ligh rā bāyad az bālā va rāst, mesl-e āb-i ke az pelle pāyin mi-āyad, khānd.»* — Ms Ahmadi said, 'Nasta'liq has to be read from the top right, like water running down a flight of steps.'
- او افزود که خواننده‌ی ایرانی هم شعرهای معروف را بیشتر از روی حافظه می‌خواند تا از روی حروف.  
  *u afzud ke khānande-ye Irāni ham she'r-hā-ye ma'ruf rā bish-tar az ru-ye hāfeze mi-khānad tā az ru-ye horuf.* — She added that Iranian readers, too, read famous poems more from memory than from the letters.
- در پایان کلاس، تام با خطی لرزان نام خود را به نستعلیق نوشت: «تام».  
  *dar pāyān-e kelās, Tām bā khatt-i larzān nām-e khod rā be nasta'ligh nevesht: «Tām».* — At the end of the class, in a shaky hand, Tom wrote his own name in Nasta'liq: Tām.
- خانم احمدی گفت: «خسته نباشید! خوشنویسی صبر می‌خواهد، و شما صبر را امروز آموختید.»  
  *khānom-e Ahmadi goft: «khaste na-bāshid! khoshnevisi sabr mi-khāhad, va shomā sabr rā emruz āmukhtid.»* — Ms Ahmadi said, 'Well done! Calligraphy needs patience – and today you have learned patience.'

3. Why couldn't Tom read Mir Emad's piece? — It was in Arabic / The words were stacked and the dots were out of place / The ink had faded

### Three masters and a reed

**Mir Ali Tabrizi** (میرعلی تبریزی (*Mir-Ali-ye Tabrizi*), active around 1400) is remembered as the father of Nasta'liq. Tradition says he laid down its rules; historians add that the script was taking shape in western Iran in his time, and that he gave it its first classic form.

**Mir Emad Hassani** (میرعماد حسنی (*Mir-Emād-e Hasani*), 1554–1615), born in Qazvin and active in Isfahan under Shah Abbas, is for most Iranians simply the greatest Nasta'liq calligrapher who ever lived. His pieces are balanced so perfectly that calligraphers still copy them as models (سرمشق). He was killed in Isfahan in 1615; the circumstances have been told in many versions.

**Gholam-Hossein Amirkhani** (غلامحسین امیرخانی (*Gholām-Hoseyn Amirkhāni*), born 1939) is the best-known living master. For decades he taught and led the Iranian Calligraphers' Association (انجمن خوشنویسان ایران), whose classes in every province turned calligraphy into a mass art.

All three wrote with the same tool: the **reed pen** (قلم نی (*ghalam-e ney*)). The calligrapher pares it with a penknife, then cuts the nib on a small hard plate at a slant (قط زدن (*ghatt zadan*)) – a cut so personal that teachers say you can recognise a pupil by the pen. The ink (مرکب (*morakkab*)) is soot ground with gum and water, kept in an inkwell (دوات (*davāt*)) stuffed with silk fibres (لیقه (*lighe*)) so that the pen takes up just enough. Paper was traditionally coated and burnished until the pen glided over it. Mistakes can be scraped away – but a master, the saying goes, writes a letter once.

### Tips

- Start reading Nasta'liq with things you already know: book titles, shop signs, the lines of Hafez and Saadi from earlier lessons. Recognition comes before reading.
- Copy before you read. Ten minutes of writing a word in Nasta'liq (even with a felt-tip cut flat) teaches the slope and the stacking faster than an hour of staring.
- In a stacked word, find the first letter by looking for the highest point on the right, then trace the joins with your finger.
- Don't trust dot positions. If a word makes no sense, try moving its dots to the neighbouring letters – that solves most puzzles.
- On a religious building, expect Arabic; on a tile in a garden or a teahouse, expect Persian poetry. The language you expect is half the reading.
- Iranians compliment handwriting freely: «چه خط قشنگی دارید!» (what beautiful handwriting you have!). The polite answer is «لطف دارید» or «خواهش می‌کنم، هنوز خیلی مانده» (you're kind – I still have a long way to go).

### Scripts, masters and meanings

**Choose the right answer**

1. A wedding invitation, a poetry book title and a shop sign are most likely written in… — کوفی / نستعلیق / ثلث
2. In a Nasta'liq word, where do you start reading? — at the lowest point on the left / at the highest point on the right / in the middle, where the dots are
3. The best English for حروف این واژه روی هم سوار شده‌اند. (*horuf-e in vāzhe ru-ye ham savār shode-and.*) — The letters of this word have ridden away. / The letters of this word are stacked on top of each other. / This word has too many letters.
4. Which word does a calligrapher use to sign an inscription? — کتبه / بسم / سنه
5. Who is traditionally credited with laying down the rules of Nasta'liq? — Mir Emad Hassani / Gholam-Hossein Amirkhani / Mir Ali Tabrizi
6. The best English for خوشنویسی صبر می‌خواهد. (*khoshnevisi sabr mi-khāhad.*) — Calligraphy wants you to wait. / Calligraphy takes patience. / Calligraphy is slow.

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. نستعلیق — Nasta'liq is the Persian script for anything elegant and Persian.
2. at the highest point on the right — Words slope down from upper right to lower left.
3. The letters of this word are stacked on top of each other. — سوار شدن 'to mount, get on' – here, letters sitting on top of one another.
4. کتبه — کتبه 'wrote it', followed by the name. سنه means 'year' and introduces the date.
5. Mir Ali Tabrizi — Mir Ali Tabrizi (c. 1400); Mir Emad perfected it two centuries later.
6. Calligraphy takes patience. — X می‌خواهد with a thing as subject = 'X needs, takes'.

</details>
