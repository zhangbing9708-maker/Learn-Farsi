# This and that: what is it?
## این چیست؟
*in chist?* — Beginner (A1), lesson 3. [Open this lesson with audio and interactive exercises](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/course/a1-03-this-and-that.html)

Point at anything and ask what it is. In this lesson you learn to name the things around you, to say 'a book' and 'the books', and to link words together with the ezafe – the small sound that holds Persian sentences together.

**In this lesson you will learn to:**

- ask and say what something is: in chist? in ketāb ast
- use in / ān (this / that) and injā / ānjā
- make plurals with -hā
- say 'a …' with yek and -i
- link two nouns with the ezafe: ketāb-e Sārā

### What is this?

_After class, Tom and Sara look at the things on the teacher's desk._

- **Tom:** سارا، این چیست؟  
  *Sārā, in chist?* — Sara, what is this?  
  Spoken: سارا، این چیه؟ *Sārā, in chi-ye?*
- **Sara:** این یک کتاب است. کتاب فارسی.  
  *in yek ketāb ast. ketāb-e fārsi.* — This is a book. A Persian book.
- **Tom:** و آن چیست؟  
  *va ān chist?* — And what is that?  
  Spoken: اون چیه؟ *un chi-ye?*
- **Sara:** آن کیف آقای کریمی است.  
  *ān kif-e āghā-ye Karimi ast.* — That is Mr Karimi's bag.
- **Tom:** این‌ها چه هستند؟  
  *in-hā che hastand?* — What are these?
- **Sara:** این‌ها مداد هستند. و آن‌ها خودکارند.  
  *in-hā medād hastand. va ān-hā khodkār-and.* — These are pencils. And those are pens.
- **Tom:** این کلید کیست؟  
  *in kelid-e kist?* — Whose key is this?
- **Sara:** کلید من است. ممنون!  
  *kelid-e man ast. mamnun!* — It's my key. Thanks!
- **Tom:** «پنجره» یعنی چه؟  
  *«panjare» ya'ni che?* — What does 'panjare' mean?  
  Spoken: «پنجره» یعنی چی؟ *«panjare» ya'ni chi?*
- **Sara:** پنجره یعنی این. و آن در است.  
  *panjare ya'ni in. va ān dar ast.* — Panjare means this (she points at the window). And that is a door.
- **Tom:** آهان! کلاس ما یک در و دو پنجره دارد.  
  *āhān! kelās-e mā yek dar o do panjare dārad.* — Aha! Our classroom has one door and two windows.

### Pointing at things

این (*in*, ‘this’) is for things near you, آن (*ān*, ‘that’) for things further away – spoken *un*. Both work as a word on their own (این چیست؟ (*in chist?*, ‘what is this?’)) and in front of a noun (این کتاب (*in ketāb*, ‘this book’)). The plurals are این‌ها (*in-hā*, ‘these’) and آن‌ها (*ān-hā*, ‘those’) – the same *ān-hā* you learned for *they*.

For places, use اینجا (*injā*, ‘here’) and آنجا (*ānjā*, ‘there’).

The useful question یعنی چه؟ (*ya'ni che?*, ‘what does it mean?’) and the word یعنی (*ya'ni*, ‘it means’) will carry you through many conversations: *«ketāb» ya'ni book*. To ask for the English word, say به انگلیسی چه می‌شود؟ (*be engelisi che mi-shavad?*, ‘what does it become in English?’).

### Plurals: add -hā

To make almost any noun plural, add ها (*-hā*): کتاب (*ketāb*) → کتاب‌ها (*ketāb-hā*, ‘books’), میز (*miz*) → میزها (*miz-hā*, ‘tables’). In careful writing, *-hā* is kept slightly apart from the word with the zero-width joiner, so the letters do not run together: کتاب‌ها.

Two things make Persian plurals easy. After a number you **do not** use the plural: دو پنجره (*do panjare*, ‘two windows’), سه کتاب (*se ketāb*, ‘three books’). And a plural subject that is not a person usually takes a **singular** verb in formal Persian: این‌ها مداد هستند (*in-hā medād hastand*) is fine in speech, but you will also read کتاب‌ها روی میز است (*ketāb-hā ru-ye miz ast*, ‘the books are on the table’).

| Singular | Plural | After a number |
| --- | --- | --- |
| کتاب (*ketāb*) | کتاب‌ها (*ketāb-hā*) | سه کتاب (*se ketāb*) |
| صندلی (*sandali*) | صندلی‌ها (*sandali-hā*) | چهار صندلی (*chahār sandali*) |
| خانه (*khāne*) | خانه‌ها (*khāne-hā*) | دو خانه (*do khāne*) |
| دانشجو (*dāneshju*) | دانشجوها (*dāneshju-hā*) | پنج دانشجو (*panj dāneshju*) |

- این‌ها کتاب‌های من هستند.  
  *in-hā ketāb-hā-ye man hastand.* — These are my books.
- کلاس ما دو پنجره دارد.  
  *kelās-e mā do panjare dārad.* — Our classroom has two windows.
- دانشجوها اینجا هستند.  
  *dāneshju-hā injā hastand.* — The students are here.

### 'A book' and 'the book'

Persian has no word for *the*. A bare noun is definite or general from context: کتاب روی میز است (*ketāb ru-ye miz ast*, ‘the book is on the table’).

To say *a* …, either put یک (*yek*, ‘one’) before the noun, or add ی (*-i*) to the end, or both: یک کتاب (*yek ketāb*), کتابی (*ketāb-i*), یک کتابی (*yek ketāb-i*) all mean *a book*. In speech *yek* is usually *ye*: یه کتاب (*ye ketāb*).

After a vowel the ending needs a *y*: خانه‌ای (*khāne-i*, ‘a house’) (written with a separate ای), دانشجویی (*dāneshju-yi*, ‘a student’).

- این یک کتاب است.  
  *in yek ketāb ast.* — This is a book.  
  Spoken: این یه کتابه. *in ye ketāb-e.*
- کتابی روی میز است.  
  *ketāb-i ru-ye miz ast.* — There is a book on the table.
- کتاب روی میز است.  
  *ketāb ru-ye miz ast.* — The book is on the table.

### The ezafe: linking words together

The **ezafe** (اضافه (*ezāfe*, ‘addition’)) is an unstressed **-e** that links a noun to whatever describes or owns it. The owner or description comes **after** the noun: کتاب سارا (*ketāb-e Sārā*, ‘Sara's book’), کیف آقای کریمی (*kif-e āghā-ye Karimi*, ‘Mr Karimi's bag’), کلاس ما (*kelās-e mā*, ‘our class’), کتاب فارسی (*ketāb-e fārsi*, ‘a Persian book’).

After a consonant the ezafe is **not written** – you simply say it. After a vowel it is written as ی (*-ye*): خانه‌ی سارا (*khāne-ye Sārā*, ‘Sara's house’), آقای کریمی (*āghā-ye Karimi*, ‘Mr Karimi’), دانشجوی خوب (*dāneshju-ye khub*, ‘a good student’).

Links can chain: کلید در کلاس ما (*kelid-e dar-e kelās-e mā*, ‘the key of the door of our classroom’).

| Persian | Reading | English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| کتاب من (*ketāb-e man*) | ketāb-**e** man | my book |
| کیف سارا (*kif-e Sārā*) | kif-**e** Sārā | Sara's bag |
| خانه‌ی تام (*khāne-ye Tām*) | khāne-**ye** Tām | Tom's house |
| معلم فارسی (*mo'allem-e fārsi*) | mo'allem-**e** fārsi | the Persian teacher |
| در کلاس (*dar-e kelās*) | dar-**e** kelās | the classroom door |

- این کیف لیلا است.  
  *in kif-e Leylā ast.* — This is Leila's bag.
- آن گوشی امیر است.  
  *ān gushi-ye Amir ast.* — That is Amir's phone.
- این کلید کیست؟  
  *in kelid-e kist?* — Whose key is this?

### Our classroom

_Sara describes the classroom._

- این کلاس ماست.  
  *in kelās-e mā-st.* — This is our classroom.
- کلاس ما بزرگ است.  
  *kelās-e mā bozorg ast.* — Our classroom is big.
- یک در و دو پنجره دارد.  
  *yek dar o do panjare dārad.* — It has one door and two windows.
- این میز آقای کریمی است.  
  *in miz-e āghā-ye Karimi ast.* — This is Mr Karimi's desk.
- روی میز یک کتاب، یک دفتر و یک تلفن است.  
  *ru-ye miz yek ketāb, yek daftar o yek telefon ast.* — On the desk there is a book, a notebook and a telephone.
- آن‌ها صندلی‌های دانشجوها هستند.  
  *ān-hā sandali-hā-ye dāneshju-hā hastand.* — Those are the students' chairs.
- کیف من اینجا نیست. کیف من کجاست؟  
  *kif-e man injā nist. kif-e man kojā-st?* — My bag is not here. Where is my bag?

1. How many windows does the classroom have? — one / two / three
2. Whose desk is it? — Sara's / Tom's / Mr Karimi's
3. What is NOT on the desk? — a book / a key / a telephone

1. two
2. Mr Karimi's

### The bazaar: where everything has a name

The best place to practise *in chist?* is a bazaar. Every Iranian city has one – Tehran's بازار بزرگ (*bāzār-e bozorg*, ‘Grand Bazaar’) is a roofed town of its own, with lanes for copper, carpets, spices, gold and paper. Shopkeepers are used to foreigners pointing and asking; many will tell you the word, make you repeat it, and offer tea.

The English word *bazaar* is itself Persian (بازار (*bāzār*)), one of a long list of words that travelled west – you will meet the others in the advanced course.

### Watch out

- **در** is two words in one spelling: *dar* 'in' and *dar* 'door'. Context always tells you which: *dar kelās* (in the class) vs *dar-e kelās* (the class door).
- Learn each noun with its ezafe habit: after a final **-e** (خانه) or **-ā/-u/-i** the link sounds *-ye* and is written ی.
- Spoken Persian turns **ān** into **un** everywhere: *un, unjā, un-hā*. Written Persian keeps **ān**.
- Don't translate *the*. Say the bare noun and let context do the work.
