# Body and health: my head hurts
## سرم درد می‌کند
*sar-am dard mi-konad* — Beginner (A1), lesson 18. [Open this lesson with audio and interactive exercises](https://zhangbing9708-maker.github.io/Learn-Farsi/course/a1-18-body-and-health.html)

Sooner or later every traveller needs a pharmacy. In this lesson you learn the parts of the body, how to say what hurts – in Persian your head 'does pain' – how to describe a cold or a fever, and how to understand the advice you get: you should rest, you must drink water. That last one uses باید, Persian's word for 'must', with the be- form of the verb you met in the imperative.

**In this lesson you will learn to:**

- name the main parts of the body
- say what hurts with درد کردن: سرم درد می‌کند
- say how you feel and buy medicine at a pharmacy
- give and understand simple advice with باید + subjunctive: باید استراحت کنی

### At the pharmacy

_Tom wakes up feeling ill and goes to the pharmacy on his street._

- **Pharmacist:** سلام، بفرمایید.  
  *salām, befarmāyid.* — Hello, how can I help you?
- **Tom:** سلام. حالم خوب نیست. سرم خیلی درد می‌کند.  
  *salām. hāl-am khub nist. sar-am kheyli dard mi-konad.* — Hello. I don't feel well. I have a bad headache.  
  Spoken: سلام. حالم خوب نیست. سرم خیلی درد می‌کنه. *salām. hāl-am khub nist. sar-am kheyli dard mi-kone.*
- **Pharmacist:** تب هم دارید؟  
  *tab ham dārid?* — Do you have a fever too?  
  Spoken: تب هم دارین؟ *tab ham dārin?*
- **Tom:** فکر می‌کنم بله. گلویم هم درد می‌کند.  
  *fekr mi-konam bale. galu-yam ham dard mi-konad.* — I think so. My throat hurts too.  
  Spoken: فکر می‌کنم آره. گلوم هم درد می‌کنه. *fekr mi-konam āre. galu-m ham dard mi-kone.*
- **Pharmacist:** سرفه هم می‌کنید؟  
  *sorfe ham mi-konid?* — Are you coughing as well?  
  Spoken: سرفه هم می‌کنین؟ *sorfe ham mi-konin?*
- **Tom:** نه، سرفه نمی‌کنم. اما خیلی خسته‌ام.  
  *na, sorfe nemi-konam. ammā kheyli khaste-am.* — No, I'm not coughing. But I'm very tired.  
  Spoken: نه، سرفه نمی‌کنم. ولی خیلی خسته‌ام. *na, sorfe nemi-konam. vali kheyli khaste-am.*
- **Pharmacist:** سرما خورده‌اید. این قرص خوب است.  
  *sarmā khorde-id. in ghors khub ast.* — You've caught a cold. This tablet is good.  
  Spoken: سرما خوردین. این قرص خوبه. *sarmā khordin. in ghors khube.*
- **Tom:** چند بار در روز بخورم؟  
  *chand bār dar ruz bekhoram?* — How many times a day should I take it?  
  Spoken: روزی چند بار بخورم؟ *ruz-i chand bār bekhoram?*
- **Pharmacist:** روزی سه بار، بعد از غذا. باید زیاد آب بخورید و استراحت کنید.  
  *ruz-i se bār, ba'd az ghazā. bāyad ziyād āb bekhorid o esterāhat konid.* — Three times a day, after meals. You should drink a lot of water and rest.  
  Spoken: روزی سه بار، بعد از غذا. باید زیاد آب بخورین و استراحت کنین. *ruz-i se bār, ba'd az ghazā. bāyad ziyād āb bekhorin o esterāhat konin.*
- **Tom:** باید پیش دکتر بروم؟  
  *bāyad pish-e doktor beravam?* — Do I need to see a doctor?  
  Spoken: باید برم دکتر؟ *bāyad beram doktor?*
- **Pharmacist:** فعلاً نه. دو سه روز استراحت کنید.  
  *fe'lan na. do se ruz esterāhat konid.* — Not for now. Rest for two or three days.  
  Spoken: فعلاً نه. دو سه روز استراحت کنین. *fe'lan na. do se ruz esterāhat konin.*
- **Tom:** خیلی ممنون. چقدر می‌شود؟  
  *kheyli mamnun. cheghadr mi-shavad?* — Thanks a lot. How much is it?  
  Spoken: خیلی ممنون. چقدر می‌شه؟ *kheyli mamnun. cheghadr mi-she?*
- **Pharmacist:** پنجاه هزار تومان. خدا بد ندهد!  
  *panjāh hezār tumān. khodā bad na-dahad!* — Fifty thousand tomans. Get well soon!  
  Spoken: پنجاه تومن. خدا بد نده! *panjāh toman. khodā bad nade!*
- **Tom:** ممنون، بد نبینید!  
  *mamnun, bad na-binid!* — Thank you – and may you never be ill!  
  Spoken: ممنون، بد نبینین! *mamnun, bad na-binin!*

### My head 'does pain'

English says *I have a headache* or *my head hurts*. Persian says that your head **does pain**: سرم درد می‌کند. (*sar-am dard mi-konad.*, ‘My head hurts.’) The body part is the subject, with the possessive ending you learned in lesson 5 (*sar-am*, my head), and درد کردن (*dard kardan*, ‘to ache’) is the verb.

For how you feel in general, use حال (*hāl*, ‘state, condition’): حالم خوب نیست. (*hāl-am khub nist.*, ‘I don't feel well.’) – literally *my state is not good*. The answer to حالت چطور است؟ (*hāl-at chetor ast?*) is now more than *khubam*.

Two phrases every Iranian uses when someone is ill: خدا بد ندهد! (*khodā bad na-dahad!*, ‘may God give you nothing bad’), the Persian *get well soon*, and the reply بد نبینی! (*bad na-bini!*, ‘may you see nothing bad’). A cold, by the way, is something you **eat**: سرما خورده‌ام. (*sarmā khorde-am.*, ‘I've caught a cold’) – literally *I have eaten cold*.

### Saying what hurts: سرم درد می‌کند

Put the possessive ending on the body part and follow it with درد می‌کند (*dard mi-konad*, ‘it hurts’). The verb stays in the *it* form whoever is in pain, because the subject is the head, the tooth or the leg – not you.

After a vowel the endings take a **y**: پایم (*pā-yam*, ‘my leg’), گلویم (*galu-yam*, ‘my throat’). With two eyes or two feet, written Persian uses a plural verb – چشم‌هایم درد می‌کنند (*chashm-hā-yam dard mi-konand*, ‘my eyes hurt’) – but in speech the singular is normal.

The negative is regular: سرم درد نمی‌کند. (*sar-am dard nemi-konad.*, ‘My head doesn't hurt.’) And the doctor's first question is کجایتان درد می‌کند؟ (*kojā-yetān dard mi-konad?*, ‘Where does it hurt?’) – literally *where of yours hurts?*

| Person | head | leg (after a vowel) | English |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | سرم درد می‌کند (*sar-am dard mi-konad*) | پایم درد می‌کند (*pā-yam dard mi-konad*) | my head / leg hurts |
| you | سرت درد می‌کند (*sar-at dard mi-konad*) | پایت درد می‌کند (*pā-yat dard mi-konad*) | your head / leg hurts |
| he / she | سرش درد می‌کند (*sar-ash dard mi-konad*) | پایش درد می‌کند (*pā-yash dard mi-konad*) | his / her head / leg hurts |
| we | سرمان درد می‌کند (*sar-emān dard mi-konad*) | پایمان درد می‌کند (*pā-yemān dard mi-konad*) | our heads / legs hurt |
| you (polite, pl.) | سرتان درد می‌کند (*sar-etān dard mi-konad*) | پایتان درد می‌کند (*pā-yetān dard mi-konad*) | your head / leg hurts |
| they | سرشان درد می‌کند (*sar-eshān dard mi-konad*) | پایشان درد می‌کند (*pā-yeshān dard mi-konad*) | their heads / legs hurt |

- دندانم خیلی درد می‌کند.  
  *dandān-am kheyli dard mi-konad.* — My tooth really hurts.  
  Spoken: دندونم خیلی درد می‌کنه. *dandun-am kheyli dard mi-kone.*
- کجایتان درد می‌کند؟  
  *kojā-yetān dard mi-konad?* — Where does it hurt?  
  Spoken: کجاتون درد می‌کنه؟ *kojā-tun dard mi-kone?*
- کمر پدرم درد می‌کند.  
  *kamar-e pedar-am dard mi-konad.* — My father's back hurts.
- دیگر سرم درد نمی‌کند.  
  *digar sar-am dard nemi-konad.* — My head doesn't hurt any more.
- دلم درد می‌کند.  
  *del-am dard mi-konad.* — I have a stomach ache.  
  _del is the heart of poetry and the tummy of everyday life_

### How do you feel? Symptoms

Most symptoms use verbs you already know. A fever is something you **have**, with داشتن (*dāshtan*): تب دارم. (*tab dāram.*) A cough is something you **do**, with کردن (*kardan*): سرفه می‌کنم. (*sorfe mi-konam.*) Learn سرما خورده‌ام (*sarmā khorde-am*, ‘I've caught a cold’) as one chunk for now – it is the present perfect, which you will meet properly at A2.

To ask a friend how they feel, say حالت چطور است؟ (*hāl-at chetor ast?*); to a stranger, حالتان چطور است؟ (*hāl-etān chetor ast?*) The good news is بهترم. (*behtar-am.*, ‘I'm better.’)

| Persian | English | literally |
| --- | --- | --- |
| حالم خوب نیست. (*hāl-am khub nist.*) | I don't feel well. | my state is not good |
| تب دارم. (*tab dāram.*) | I have a fever. | I have fever |
| سرفه می‌کنم. (*sorfe mi-konam.*) | I'm coughing. | I do cough |
| سرما خورده‌ام. (*sarmā khorde-am.*) | I've caught a cold. | I have eaten cold |
| سرم گیج می‌رود. (*sar-am gij mi-ravad.*) | I feel dizzy. | my head goes dizzy |
| خسته‌ام. (*khaste-am.*) | I'm tired. | tired-I-am |
| بهترم. (*behtar-am.*) | I'm better. | better-I-am |

- حالت چطور است؟ بهتری؟  
  *hāl-at chetor ast? behtar-i?* — How are you feeling? Are you better?  
  Spoken: حالت چطوره؟ بهتری؟ *hāl-et chetore? behtar-i?*
- مادرم سرما خورده است و تب دارد.  
  *mādar-am sarmā khorde ast o tab dārad.* — My mother has caught a cold and has a fever.
- امروز حالم خوب نیست، سر کار نمی‌روم.  
  *emruz hāl-am khub nist, sar-e kār nemi-ravam.* — I don't feel well today, I'm not going to work.  
  Spoken: امروز حالم خوب نیست، سر کار نمی‌رم. *emruz hāl-am khub nist, sar-e kār nemi-ram.*
- بچه شب‌ها سرفه می‌کند.  
  *bacche shab-hā sorfe mi-konad.* — The child coughs at night.

### You should rest: باید + subjunctive

باید (*bāyad*) means *must, have to, should*, and it never changes. The verb after it takes the **be-** form you met in the imperative (lesson 16), now with a person ending: باید بروم (*bāyad beravam*, ‘I must go’), باید بروی (*bāyad beravi*, ‘you must go’). This form is called the *subjunctive* (التزامی (*eltezāmi*)); you will use it a lot from now on.

Compound verbs drop the **be-**, just as in the imperative: باید استراحت کنید (*bāyad esterāhat konid*, ‘you should rest’). For *must not, should not*, add **na-** to bāyad: نباید بروی (*na-bāyad beravi*, ‘you shouldn't go’).

| Person | go (raftan) | eat, take (khordan) | rest (esterāhat kardan) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | باید بروم (*bāyad beravam*) | باید بخورم (*bāyad bekhoram*) | باید استراحت کنم (*bāyad esterāhat konam*) |
| you | باید بروی (*bāyad beravi*) | باید بخوری (*bāyad bekhori*) | باید استراحت کنی (*bāyad esterāhat koni*) |
| he / she | باید برود (*bāyad beravad*) | باید بخورد (*bāyad bekhorad*) | باید استراحت کند (*bāyad esterāhat konad*) |
| we | باید برویم (*bāyad beravim*) | باید بخوریم (*bāyad bekhorim*) | باید استراحت کنیم (*bāyad esterāhat konim*) |
| you (polite, pl.) | باید بروید (*bāyad beravid*) | باید بخورید (*bāyad bekhorid*) | باید استراحت کنید (*bāyad esterāhat konid*) |
| they | باید بروند (*bāyad beravand*) | باید بخورند (*bāyad bekhorand*) | باید استراحت کنند (*bāyad esterāhat konand*) |

- باید پیش دکتر بروی.  
  *bāyad pish-e doktor beravi.* — You should see a doctor.  
  Spoken: باید بری دکتر. *bāyad beri doktor.*
- باید روزی سه بار قرص بخورم.  
  *bāyad ruz-i se bār ghors bekhoram.* — I have to take a tablet three times a day.
- شما نباید امروز کار کنید.  
  *shomā na-bāyad emruz kār konid.* — You shouldn't work today.  
  Spoken: شما نباید امروز کار کنین. *shomā na-bāyad emruz kār konin.*
- باید زیاد آب بخوریم.  
  *bāyad ziyād āb bekhorim.* — We should drink a lot of water.
- داروخانه کجاست؟ باید قرص بخرم.  
  *dāru-khāne kojāst? bāyad ghors bekharam.* — Where is the pharmacy? I need to buy some tablets.

### Mina is ill

_Mina wakes up with a cold. Read about her day at home._

- امروز مینا مریض است.  
  *emruz Minā mariz ast.* — Mina is ill today.
- سرش درد می‌کند و تب دارد.  
  *sar-ash dard mi-konad o tab dārad.* — She has a headache and a fever.
- او سر کار نمی‌رود و در خانه می‌ماند.

### Warm foods, cold foods and Avicenna

Tell an Iranian grandmother you have a cold and she will not only say خدا بد ندهد (*khodā bad na-dahad*) – she will tell you what to eat. In traditional Persian medicine every food has a nature, طبع (*tab'*), that is either گرم (*garm*, ‘warm’) or سرد (*sard*, ‘cold’), whatever its temperature. Dates, honey, walnuts and ginger are *warm*; yoghurt, cucumber, fish and watermelon are *cold*. Too much of one upsets your balance, so a cold is treated with warm things: barley soup, آش (*āsh*), a hot drink of نبات (*nabāt*, ‘rock sugar’) with saffron, and quince or mint distillates from the herbalist.

The system goes back to Galen and to **Avicenna** – ابن سینا (*Ebn-e Sinā*) (980–1037) – whose *Canon of Medicine* was the standard medical textbook in Europe for centuries. Modern Iran has excellent doctors and a pharmacy on every street, but the warm–cold idea is everywhere: ask why someone won't eat fish with yoghurt and you will hear about it.

### Tips

- The body part is the subject, so the verb is always the *it* form: **sar-am dard mi-konad** (my head hurts) – never *dard mi-konam*.
- **del** is the heart of poetry and the tummy of everyday life: *del-am dard mi-konad* is a stomach ache, but *del-am tang ast* means 'I miss someone'.
- In speech *mi-konad* becomes *mi-kone* and *dandān* becomes *dandun*: *dandun-am dard mi-kone*. The polite *-id* becomes *-in*: *esterāhat konin*.
- When someone tells you they are ill, say **khodā bad na-dahad!** (spoken *khodā bad nade*). The answer is **bad na-bini!** – 'may you see nothing bad'.
- Iranian pharmacists give advice freely and many simple medicines are sold over the counter. A prescription is a **noskhe** (نسخه).
