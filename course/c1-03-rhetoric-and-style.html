بلاغت و سبکRhetoric and style: figures of speech and making a speech

balāghat o sabk

Persians have studied the art of speaking well for a thousand years, and the figures their scholars named – simile, metaphor, indirect expression, exaggeration, antithesis, rhymed prose, wordplay, allusion – are not museum pieces: you will hear every one of them at a family dinner. This lesson names the nine main figures with classical and everyday examples, then shows how Iranians give a short speech or a toast at a wedding or a farewell, ending with a model wedding speech.

In this lesson you will learn to

  • recognise and name تشبیه، استعاره، کنایه، مبالغه، تضاد، تکرار، سجع، جناس and تلمیح in poetry, prose and conversation
  • explain how a classical line by Saadi, Ferdowsi, Rumi or Hafez works
  • use everyday figures of speech naturally and understand them when you hear them
  • give a short speech or toast at a wedding or farewell with the right formulas

Speaking well, an old art

An essay on Persian rhetoric (بلاغت). Notice that the essay itself uses the devices it describes – an example, a quotation, a contrast – and that Arabic technical terms (تشبیه، استعاره، سجع…) sit inside very Persian sentences.

در سنت ادبی فارسی، سخن گفتن تنها وسیله‌ی رساندن پیام نیست، بلکه هنری است که آموختنی است.

dar sonnat-e adabi-ye fārsi, sokhan goftan tanhā vasile-ye resāndan-e payām nist, balke honar-i ast ke āmukhtani ast.

In the Persian literary tradition, speaking is not merely a way of conveying a message but an art that can be learned.

دانشمندان قدیم این هنر را «بلاغت» می‌نامیدند و آن را در سه دانش «معانی»، «بیان» و «بدیع» بررسی می‌کردند.

dāneshmandān-e ghadim in honar rā «balāghat» mi-nāmidand va ān rā dar se dānesh-e «ma'āni», «bayān» o «badi'» barrasi mi-kardand.

Scholars of old called this art balāghat (eloquence) and studied it in three sciences: ma'āni, bayān and badi'.

«بیان» از شیوه‌های گوناگون گفتن یک معنا سخن می‌گوید: تشبیه، استعاره، مجاز و کنایه.

«bayān» az shive-hā-ye gunāgun-e goftan-e yek ma'nā sokhan mi-guyad: tashbih, este'āre, majāz o kenāye.

Bayān deals with the different ways of saying one and the same thing: simile, metaphor, figurative usage and indirect expression.

«بدیع» به زیبایی‌های لفظی و معنوی کلام می‌پردازد، مانند سجع، جناس، تضاد و تلمیح.

«badi'» be zibāyi-hā-ye lafzi va ma'navi-ye kalām mi-pardāzad, mānand-e saj', jenās, tazādd o talmih.

Badi' deals with the beauties of sound and sense in speech, such as rhymed prose, wordplay, antithesis and allusion.

ساده‌ترین این شگردها تشبیه است: «چهره‌اش مثل ماه است.»

sāde-tarin-e in shegerd-hā tashbih ast: «chehre-ash mesl-e māh ast.»

The simplest of these devices is the simile: 'Her face is like the moon.'

در استعاره، یکی از دو سوی تشبیه حذف می‌شود و شاعر به جای «یار»، تنها از «سرو» یا «ماه» سخن می‌گوید.

dar este'āre, yek-i az do su-ye tashbih hazf mi-shavad va shā'er be jā-ye «yār», tanhā az «sarv» yā «māh» sokhan mi-guyad.

In a metaphor one of the two sides of the comparison is dropped, and instead of 'the beloved' the poet speaks only of 'the cypress' or 'the moon'.

کنایه معنایی پنهان را از راهی غیرمستقیم می‌رساند؛ «در خانه‌اش همیشه باز است» یعنی او مهمان‌نواز است.

kenāye ma'nā-yi penhān rā az rāh-i gheyr-e-mostaghim mi-resānad; «dar-e khāne-ash hamishe bāz ast» ya'ni u mehmān-navāz ast.

Kenāye conveys a hidden meaning indirectly: 'his door is always open' means that he is hospitable.

سعدی در گلستان با سجع، نثر را به موسیقی نزدیک کرده است.

Sa'di dar Golestān bā saj', nasr rā be musighi nazdik karde ast.

In the Golestan, Saadi uses rhymed prose to bring prose close to music.

این شگردها تنها به کتاب‌ها محدود نیستند و در گفت‌وگوی روزانه‌ی ایرانیان نیز فراوان به کار می‌روند.

in shegerd-hā tanhā be ketāb-hā mahdud nistand va dar goft-o-gu-ye ruzāne-ye Irāniyān niz farāvān be kār mi-ravand.

These devices are not confined to books; Iranians use them constantly in everyday conversation too.

مردم به جای «خیلی خسته‌ام» می‌گویند «از پا افتادم» و به جای «خیلی خندیدیم» می‌گویند «از خنده مردیم».

mardom be jā-ye «kheyli khaste-am» mi-guyand «az pā oftādam» va be jā-ye «kheyli khandidim» mi-guyand «az khande mordim».

Instead of 'I'm very tired' people say 'I've fallen off my feet', and instead of 'we laughed a lot' they say 'we died laughing'.

این مبالغه‌ها کسی را فریب نمی‌دهند، بلکه به سخن گرمی و رنگ می‌بخشند.

in mobālaghe-hā kas-i rā farib nemi-dahand, balke be sokhan garmi o rang mi-bakhshand.

These exaggerations deceive no one; they give warmth and colour to speech.

هنر سخن در مراسم رسمی، از عروسی تا بدرقه‌ی همکاران، اهمیتی ویژه دارد.

honar-e sokhan dar marāsem-e rasmi, az arusi tā badraghe-ye hamkārān, ahammiyat-i vizhe dārad.

The art of speaking matters particularly at formal occasions, from weddings to farewells for colleagues.

سخنران خوب سخن خود را با سپاس و خوشامدگویی آغاز می‌کند و با آرزوی خیر به پایان می‌برد.

sokhanrān-e khub sokhan-e khod rā bā sepās o khoshāmad-guyi āghāz mi-konad va bā ārezu-ye kheyr be pāyān mi-barad.

A good speaker begins with thanks and a welcome and ends with good wishes.

او شعری کوتاه از حافظ یا سعدی می‌خواند تا سخنش را دلنشین‌تر کند.

u she'r-i kutāh az Hāfez yā Sa'di mi-khānad tā sokhan-ash rā delneshin-tar konad.

He or she recites a short poem by Hafez or Saadi to make the speech more moving.

با این همه، زیاده‌روی در آرایه‌ها سخن را از صداقت تهی می‌کند.

bā in hame, ziyāde-ravi dar ārāye-hā sokhan rā az sedāghat tohi mi-konad.

Even so, overdoing the ornaments empties a speech of sincerity.

شیوه‌ی سعدی را «سهل و ممتنع» خوانده‌اند: سخنی که ساده به نظر می‌رسد، اما تقلید آن ناممکن است.

shive-ye Sa'di rā «sahl o momtane'» khānde-and: sokhan-i ke sāde be nazar mi-resad, ammā taghlid-e ān nā-momken ast.

Saadi's style has been called 'easy and impossible': speech that looks simple but cannot be imitated.

Nine figures, one tradition

Classical Persian rhetoric was built on Arabic models, so its terms are Arabic: تشبیه tashbih simile, استعاره este'āre metaphor, کنایه kenāye indirect expression, مبالغه mobālaghe exaggeration, تضاد tazādd antithesis, تکرار tekrār repetition, سجع saj' rhymed prose, جناس jenās wordplay, تلمیح talmih allusion. Iranian schoolchildren learn them all in literature classes, and the modern Persian umbrella word for them is آرایه‌های ادبی ārāye-hā-ye adabi literary ornaments.

Two things make them worth learning even if you never write a poem:

  • They are everywhere in speech. A Tehrani who says سرم شلوغ است sar-am sholugh ast my head is crowded (I'm busy) is using kenāye; a mother who says صد بار گفتم! sad bār goftam! I've said it a hundred times! is using mobālaghe.
  • They are the grammar of a good speech. Weddings, farewells, memorial gatherings, birthdays and staff parties all call for a few words – and Iranians expect those words to be shaped: a contrast, an image, a line of Hafez, a wish.

The first two language sections below take the figures in two families – comparison and indirection, then sound and sense. The third gives you the building blocks of a speech.

Comparison and indirection: تشبیه، استعاره، کنایه

تشبیه (simile) has four parts, and Persian school grammars name them all: the thing compared (مشبه moshabbah), what it is compared to (مشبه‌به moshabbah-on-beh), the comparison word (ادات تشبیه adāt-e tashbih: مثل، مانند، چون، همچون) and the shared quality (وجه شبه vajh-e shabah). In her face is like the moon in its brightness all four are present. Drop the comparison word and the quality and you get the strong, compact simile that Persian poets love: قد سرو ghadd-e sarv a cypress stature.

استعاره (metaphor) goes one step further: only the image remains. In classical poetry the images are a fixed code – سرو sarv cypress = the beloved's tall figure, نرگس narges narcissus = the eye, لعل la'l ruby = the lips, ماه māh moon = the face or the beloved. Everyday Persian keeps the code: ماه من māh-e man my moon is still my darling.

کنایه (indirect expression) says one thing and means another that follows from it: an open door means hospitality, a crowded head means being busy. Most Persian idioms are kenāye, and many have a moral sting.

بنی‌آدم اعضای یکدیگرند / که در آفرینش ز یک گوهرند

bani-ādam a'zā-ye yekdigar-and / ke dar āfarinesh ze yek gowhar-and

The children of Adam are limbs of one another, / for in creation they are of one essence.

Saadi, Golestan. A تشبیه بلیغ: people are limbs, with no 'like'. The image is carried through the poem: when one limb hurts, the others cannot rest.

چهره‌اش مثل ماه است.

chehre-ash mesl-e māh ast.

Her face is like the moon.

مشبه چهره، مشبه‌به ماه، ادات مثل; the shared quality (beauty, radiance) is left to the listener.

ماه من، کجایی؟

māh-e man, kojā-yi?

Where are you, my darling?

Literally: my moon, where are you?

استعاره: the person has disappeared behind the image.

سرش خیلی شلوغ است.

sar-ash kheyli sholugh ast.

He's very busy.

Literally: his head is very crowded

Spoken سرش خیلی شلوغه. sar-esh kheyli sholugh-e.

نمک خورد و نمکدان شکست.

namak khord o namakdān shekast.

He bit the hand that fed him.

Literally: he ate the salt and broke the salt-cellar

Sharing salt (bread and salt) is the old sign of hospitality and trust.

پدربزرگم خیلی دست و دلباز بود.

pedarbozorg-am kheyli dast o delbāz bud.

My grandfather was very generous.

Literally: very open of hand and heart

از پا افتادم.

az pā oftādam.

I'm exhausted.

Literally: I fell from my feet

Kenāye and mild exaggeration together – the normal state of conversational Persian.

Sound and sense: مبالغه، تضاد، تکرار، سجع، جناس، تلمیح

The second family plays with sound (rhyme, echo, repetition) and with sense (opposites, impossibilities, references to stories everyone knows).

  • مبالغه stretches a claim beyond belief – and nobody objects.
  • تضاد puts opposites side by side: old and young, night and day, laughter and tears.
  • تکرار repeats for emphasis or music. In the ghazal it is built into the form as the ردیف radif, the refrain that follows every rhyme.
  • سجع is rhyme in prose: two clauses end in words that chime. Saadi's Golestan is the model.
  • جناس pairs words that look or sound alike but mean different things – from exact homonyms to words that differ in one letter.
  • تلمیح alludes to a story, a verse or a person the listener knows: Joseph and Jacob, Leyli and Majnun, Rostam.

اگر آن ترک شیرازی به دست آرد دل ما را / به خال هندویش بخشم سمرقند و بخارا را

agar ān tork-e Shirāzi be dast ārad del-e mā rā / be khāl-e hendu-yash bakhsham Samarghand o Bokhārā rā

If that Turk of Shiraz would take my heart in her hand, / for her dark mole I would give away Samarkand and Bukhara.

Hafez. مبالغه: two great cities for a beauty spot. ترک (a fair-skinned beauty) and هندو (dark) also make a تضاد.

توانا بود هر که دانا بود / ز دانش دل پیر برنا بود

tavānā bovad har ke dānā bovad / ze dānesh del-e pir bornā bovad

Powerful is he who is wise; / through knowledge an old heart is young.

Ferdowsi, Shahnameh. تضاد between پیر 'old' and برنا 'young'; توانا and دانا chime.

یوسف گم‌گشته باز آید به کنعان غم مخور / کلبه‌ی احزان شود روزی گلستان غم مخور

Yusof-e gom-gashte bāz āyad be Kan'ān gham makhor / kolbe-ye ahzān shavad ruzi golestān gham makhor

Lost Joseph will come back to Canaan – do not grieve; / the house of sorrows will one day be a rose garden – do not grieve.

Hafez. تلمیح to Joseph and his grieving father Jacob; تکرار in the refrain غم مخور; تضاد between کلبه‌ی احزان and گلستان.

بشنو این نی چون شکایت می‌کند / از جدایی‌ها حکایت می‌کند

beshnow in ney chon shekāyat mi-konad / az jodāyi-hā hekāyat mi-konad

Listen to this reed, how it complains, / telling the tale of separations.

Rumi, the opening of the Masnavi. جناس: شکایت and حکایت differ in one letter. Many editions print «بشنو از نی».

منت خدای را عز و جل که طاعتش موجب قربت است و به شکر اندرش مزید نعمت.

mennat khodāy rā 'azza va jall ke tā'at-ash mujeb-e ghorbat ast va be shokr andar-ash mazid-e ne'mat.

Praise be to God, mighty and glorious, obedience to whom brings one near Him, and in thanking whom lies an increase of blessing.

Saadi, the first sentence of the Golestan. سجع: قربت / نعمت. The second verb (است) is left out of the second clause.

هر نفسی که فرو می‌رود ممد حیات است و چون برمی‌آید مفرح ذات.

har nafasi ke foru mi-ravad momedd-e hayāt ast va chon bar-mi-āyad mofarreh-e zāt.

Every breath that goes in sustains life, and every breath that comes out gladdens the soul.

سجع: حیات / ذات; تضاد: فرو رفتن 'go down' / برآمدن 'come up'.

آرام آرام، قدم به قدم، به هدف نزدیک شدیم.

ārām ārām, ghadam be ghadam, be hadaf nazdik shodim.

Slowly, step by step, we got close to our goal.

Everyday تکرار: doubling makes Persian adverbs gentler or more vivid.

The shape of a short speech

Whether it is a wedding, a colleague's retirement or a friend leaving for abroad, an Iranian speech of a few minutes tends to follow the same path: address → permission and modesty → thanks → a story or praise → an image or a line of poetry → a wish → (a toast) → a humble close. The formulas below are formal but warm; use the shomā forms throughout, even to people you know well, because you are speaking in public.

Two cultural notes. First, modesty is expected: من اهل سخنرانی نیستم man ahl-e sokhanrāni nistam I'm no speech-maker is said even by professional speakers. Second, عرض کردن arz kardan is the humble verb for your own words, and فرمودن farmudan the honorific verb for other people's: همان‌طور که آقای کریمی فرمودند hamān-towr ke āghā-ye Karimi farmudand as Mr Karimi said.

I'm no speech-maker, so I'll keep it short.

به پای هم پیر شوید!

To the bride and groom!

آقای کریمی، جای شما در این اداره همیشه خالی خواهد بود.

āghā-ye Karimi, jā-ye shomā dar in edāre hamishe khāli khāhad bud.

Mr Karimi, this office will always miss you.

سی سال زحمت کشیدید؛ دستتان درد نکند.

si sāl zahmat keshidid; dast-etān dard na-konad.

You have worked so hard for thirty years – thank you for everything.

برای خانم احمدی در کشور تازه‌شان آرزوی موفقیت می‌کنیم.

barā-ye khānom-e Ahmadi dar keshvar-e tāze-shān ārezu-ye movaffaghiyat mi-konim.

We wish Ms Ahmadi every success in her new country.

تازه‌شان 'their new' – the plural of respect for one person.

زیاده عرضی نیست.

ziyāde arz-i nist.

That's all I have to say.

Literally: there is no further petition

A model speech: Reza's toast at Sara and Amir's wedding

Reza, Amir's oldest friend, gives a short speech at the wedding reception. It is spoken, but in the careful register of a public occasion. Each note names the step or the figure of speech.

مهمانان عزیز، خانواده‌های محترم، سلام عرض می‌کنم.

mehmānān-e aziz, khānevāde-hā-ye mohtaram, salām arz mi-konam.

Dear guests, honoured families, good evening.

Address: guests first, then the two families.

با اجازه‌ی بزرگترها، چند کلمه‌ای درباره‌ی دوستانم سارا و امیر عرض می‌کنم.

bā ejāze-ye bozorg-tar-hā, chand kalame-i darbāre-ye dustān-am Sārā va Amir arz mi-konam.

With the elders' permission, I'd like to say a few words about my friends Sara and Amir.

Permission – a nod to the older generation – and the humble verb عرض کردن.

راستش من اهل سخنرانی نیستم، پس قول می‌دهم کوتاه حرف بزنم.

rāst-ash man ahl-e sokhanrāni nistam, pas ghowl mi-daham kutāh harf bezanam.

To be honest I'm no speech-maker, so I promise to keep it short.

Modesty.

ده سال پیش، امیر را در کلاس زبان دانشگاه شناختم.

dah sāl pish, Amir rā dar kelās-e zabān-e dāneshgāh shenākhtam.

Ten years ago I got to know Amir in a language class at university.

The story begins.

او آن روزها می‌گفت: «من هرگز ازدواج نمی‌کنم؛ من با کتاب‌هایم ازدواج کرده‌ام!»

u ān ruz-hā mi-goft: «man hargez ezdevāj nemi-konam; man bā ketāb-hā-yam ezdevāj karde-am!»

In those days he used to say, 'I'll never marry – I'm married to my books!'

Gentle humour at the groom's expense – expected, but never at the bride's.

اما یک روز سارا وارد کتابخانه شد و کتاب‌ها از دست امیر افتاد.

ammā yek ruz Sārā vāred-e ketābkhāne shod va ketāb-hā az dast-e Amir oftād.

But one day Sara walked into the library, and the books fell out of Amir's hands.

Kenāye: the falling books say what nobody needs to spell out.

از آن روز، امیر دیگر نه درس می‌خواند و نه خواب داشت.

az ān ruz, Amir digar na dars mi-khānd va na khāb dāsht.

From that day on Amir could neither study nor sleep.

مبالغه, and the balanced نه … نه … .

سارا برای او مثل ماه بود: دور، روشن و زیبا.

Sārā barā-ye u mesl-e māh bud: dur, roshan o zibā.

To him Sara was like the moon: far away, bright and beautiful.

تشبیه with the shared qualities spelled out.

امروز آن ماه به خانه‌ی امیر آمده است.

emruz ān māh be khāne-ye Amir āmade ast.

Today that moon has come to Amir's house.

The simile becomes a metaphor (استعاره): Sara is now simply 'the moon'.

سعدی می‌گوید: «بنی‌آدم اعضای یکدیگرند»؛ و امروز این دو نفر به راستی عضو یک خانواده شده‌اند.

Sa'di mi-guyad: «bani-ādam a'zā-ye yekdigar-and»; va emruz in do nafar be rāsti ozv-e yek khānevāde shode-and.

Saadi says, 'The children of Adam are limbs of one another' – and today these two have truly become limbs of one family.

تلمیح and quotation, with a play on اعضا / عضو.

در زندگی روزهای شاد و روزهای سخت، هر دو، خواهد آمد.

dar zendegi ruz-hā-ye shād va ruz-hā-ye sakht, har do, khāhad āmad.

Life will bring both happy days and hard ones.

تضاد: شاد / سخت.

آرزو می‌کنم که در روزهای شاد با هم بخندید و در روزهای سخت دست یکدیگر را بگیرید.

ārezu mi-konam ke dar ruz-hā-ye shād bā ham bekhandid va dar ruz-hā-ye sakht dast-e yekdigar rā begirid.

I wish you laughter together on the happy days, and on the hard days, each other's hand to hold.

The wish, built on تکرار and parallel clauses.

از پدر و مادر عروس و داماد هم برای این جشن زیبا سپاسگزارم.

az pedar o mādar-e arus o dāmād ham barā-ye in jashn-e zibā sepāsgozār-am.

I'd also like to thank the parents of the bride and groom for this lovely celebration.

Thanks to the hosts.

حالا لطفاً همه جام‌هایتان را بالا ببرید.

hālā lotfan hame jām-hā-yetān rā bālā bebarid.

Now would you all please raise your glasses.

The toast – here with sharbat or juice.

به سلامتی سارا و امیر! به پای هم پیر شوید!

be salāmati-ye Sārā va Amir! be pā-ye ham pir shavid!

To Sara and Amir! May you grow old together!

Toast and traditional wedding wish.

زیاده عرضی نیست.

ziyāde arz-i nist.

That's all from me.

Speeches, toasts and the bride who went to pick flowers

At an Iranian wedding the most quoted words are not in a speech at all. During the ceremony (عقد aghd), seated before the decorated sofreh, the bride is asked three times whether she consents. The first two times the guests call out عروس رفته گل بچیند! arus rafte gol bechinad! the bride has gone to pick flowers! – and only the third time does she answer بله bale yes, to applause and ululation. It is a ritual of modesty, and a small piece of rhetoric in itself: a pause made meaningful.

Speeches come later, at the reception, and are usually short. A relative or an old friend of the couple speaks, often with a few lines of Hafez or Saadi. Many Iranian celebrations are alcohol-free, so the glasses raised for به سلامتی be salāmati may hold sharbat, juice or tea – the toast is about the words, not the drink.

Farewells (مهمانی خداحافظی mehmāni-ye khodāhāfezi a goodbye party) have their own style. Colleagues bring flowers and a box of pastries, someone says جایتان خالی خواهد بود jā-yetān khāli khāhad bud, and the person leaving answers with thanks and a ritual apology for any trouble they caused: اگر بدی دیدید، حلال کنید agar bad-i didid, halāl konid if I ever wronged you, forgive me. For a friend going abroad, a classic touch is to hold a copy of the Quran or a mirror over the doorway and to pour a bowl of water after the traveller – so that they come back as smoothly as water flows.

Tips

  • To tell تشبیه from استعاره, ask: are both things named? «چشمانش مثل نرگس است» is a simile; «نرگس مستش» (her drunken narcissus) is a metaphor.
  • Kenāye is the hardest figure for learners because it looks literal. If «دستش کج است» (his hand is crooked) seems to make no sense, it is kenāye: he steals.
  • When you give a speech, write it in the formal register but read it slowly and warmly. Speak the poem, don't rush it – people will often say the second half with you.
  • One line of poetry is perfect; three is a lecture. Choose a line everyone knows: Saadi's بنی‌آدم, Hafez's یوسف گم‌گشته or a line from the Shahnameh.
  • Use عرض کردن for your own words and فرمودن for other people's. Mixing them up is a classic learner's slip that Iranians will find charming – but notice.
  • Everyday mobālaghe is not lying. «یک دنیا ممنون» (a world of thanks) and «صد بار» (a hundred times) are just the normal volume of Persian feeling.
