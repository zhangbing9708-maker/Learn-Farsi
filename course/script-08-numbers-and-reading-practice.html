عددها و تمرین خواندنNumbers and reading practice

'adad-hā va tamrin-e khāndan

You know all 32 letters – now put them to work. This lesson covers the Persian digits (which, surprisingly, run left to right), the punctuation marks, and twenty signs you will see in any Iranian street, station or shop. It ends with practical notes on handwriting, on typing Persian, and on the two great styles of the script, Naskh and Nastaliq.

In this lesson you will learn to

  • read and write the digits ۰–۹ and read numbers left to right inside right-to-left text
  • recognise the Persian comma, semicolon, question mark and quotation marks
  • read twenty common signs, from ورود to داروخانه
  • type Persian on a computer or phone, including the ZWNJ
  • tell Naskh from Nastaliq

Digits that run the other way

Persian has its own set of digits, used on prices, phone numbers, car plates, clocks and banknotes, and in every Persian text in this course:

۰ ۱ ۲ ۳ ۴ ۵ ۶ ۷ ۸ ۹

The surprise is the direction. Persian is written right to left, but numbers are written and read left to right, exactly as in English: ۲۵ bist-o-panj 25 is twenty-five, not fifty-two, and the year ۱۴۰۰ hezār-o-chahārsad 1400 starts with ۱, on the left. When your eye, travelling leftwards along a line, meets a number, it jumps to the number's left end, reads it rightwards, and then carries on to the left. Computers arrange this for you automatically.

A few shapes need care:

  • ۰ zero is just a dot, and ۵ five is a little loop, like an upside-down heart. So never write zero as a circle in Iran – it will be read as 5.
  • ۲ two and ۳ three are hooks on a stem: two has one hook, three has two.
  • ۷ seven is a V and ۸ eight the same V upside down – the easiest pair to mix up.
  • ۴ ۵ ۶ are the three digits that Arabic writes differently, so you may see other shapes for them on Arabic products and in some fonts.

Western digits are well known too – banknotes show both kinds – but Persian digits are what you will read on most signs and price tags.

The ten digits

Here are the digits with their names. You don't need to learn to count yet – numbers get a whole lesson in A1 – but knowing the shapes lets you read prices, times and room numbers from day one. You could already read شش shesh six and دو do two; notice نه noh nine, spelled exactly like نه na no.

When a number names something – a room, a metro line, a year – it comes after the noun, joined to it by the ezafe: room 204 is 'room-of 204'.

Punctuation

The full stop and the exclamation mark are the same as in English, but three marks are turned round to suit right-to-left writing: the comma and the semicolon are upside down, and the question mark is mirrored. Quotation marks are the angled guillemets, pointing outwards as in French; besides quotations, they set off names and titles, such as the name of a station or a café.

Handwriting

Persian handwriting differs from print more than English does, so here is what to expect when an Iranian writes something down for you:

  • Dots are simplified. Two dots are often written as a short dash and three dots as a little caret, like ^. So پ may have a tiny ^ underneath, and ش one on top.
  • Teeth disappear. The three teeth of س sin and ش shin are often smoothed into one flat stroke.
  • Dots and strokes come last. Write the body of the whole word first, then go back and add the dots and the top strokes of ک kāf and گ gāf.
  • Mind the line. Most letters sit on it; re, ze, vāv and the tails of final letters such as ن, ی and ع dip below it.
  • Digits: zero is a dot and five is a loop – never write zero as a circle.

Everyday handwriting leans towards the flowing Nastaliq style described below. In old letters and documents you will also meet shekaste, 'broken' Nastaliq, which even Iranians find hard to read.

Typing Persian

Every computer and phone can type Persian: add the keyboard called Persian (sometimes Farsi) in your settings. Don't use an Arabic keyboard. It types the Arabic ye and kāf – with two dots under the final ye and a squiggle inside the final kāf – and Arabic digits. They look almost the same as the Persian ones but are different characters, so searches and spell-checkers stumble over them.

The letters are not in English order. On the standard layout, A types ش, S types س, D types ی and F types ب, and alef is on H. The number row types Persian digits.

The one special key to learn on day one is the ZWNJ, which Iranians call نیم‌فاصله nim-fāsele half-space. On the standard layout it is Shift+Space; phone keyboards have it too, usually near the space bar or on the symbols page. It is what makes می‌روم mi-ravam I go and کتاب‌ها ketāb-hā books come out right.

You will also see Persian written in Latin letters – salām, chetori? – especially in older text messages and in chat. Iranians call it Finglish. It is handy when there is no Persian keyboard, but it has no fixed rules, and the Persian script is always clearer.

Naskh and Nastaliq

Persian is printed in two main styles. Naskh (نسخ naskh) is the upright, even script of newspapers, books, websites and road signs – and of this course. It is built for clarity, and it is the one to learn first.

Nastaliq (نستعلیق nasta'liq) is the glory of Persian calligraphy. It grew up in Iran in the 14th and 15th centuries – tradition names the calligrapher Mir Ali Tabrizi as its inventor – and its name blends naskh with ta'liq, an older 'hanging' script. Words slope down from right to left, letters stretch into long sweeping curves, the teeth of sin vanish and dots float wherever there is room. Iranians call it 'the bride of scripts', and it is still everywhere: on book covers and poetry collections, wedding invitations, shop signs, and the titles of films and newspapers.

Reading Nastaliq takes practice even for good readers of Naskh. Start with words you already know – a shop sign, a line of Hafez you have read in print – and let your eye follow the slope. The C1 lesson on calligraphy goes further.

Reading tips

  • To write a number by hand, write its digits left to right, even in the middle of a Persian sentence.
  • A slash inside a number is a decimal point: ۲/۵ means 2.5, not a fraction.
  • Shops usually quote prices in toman, while banknotes count in rials: 1 toman is 10 rials. If a price looks ten times too high, it is in rials.
  • Many signs in the Tehran metro and on main roads also give the English. Read the Persian first, then check yourself.
  • Read everything: shop signs, menus, product labels, film subtitles. Every one is free practice.
  • Write by hand every day for a week – your name, your city, a shopping list. Your hand teaches your eye.
