سلام!Hello! Greetings and goodbyes

salām!

Your first conversation in Persian. By the end of this lesson you can greet people at any time of day, ask how they are, answer, and say goodbye – and you will have met the two most important words in Iranian life: salām and merci.

In this lesson you will learn to

  • greet people and say goodbye
  • ask 'how are you?' and answer
  • say please and thank you
  • use the polite plural 'you' (shomā)

Two neighbours in the morning

Mina and Mr Karimi live in the same building in Tehran. They meet at the door in the morning.

Mina

سلام، آقای کریمی!

salām, āghā-ye Karimi!

Hello, Mr Karimi!

Mr Karimi

سلام، مینا خانم. صبح به خیر!

salām, Minā khānom. sobh be kheyr!

Hello, Ms Mina. Good morning!

Mina

صبح به خیر. حال شما چطور است؟

sobh be kheyr. hāl-e shomā chetor ast?

Good morning. How are you?

Spoken صبح به خیر. حالتون چطوره؟ sobh be kheyr. hāletun chetore?

Mr Karimi

خوبم، متشکرم. شما چطورید؟

khub-am, motshakker-am. shomā chetor-id?

I'm well, thank you. How are you?

Spoken خوبم، مرسی. شما چطورین؟ khub-am, mersi. shomā chetor-in?

Mina

من هم خوبم. ممنون.

man ham khub-am. mamnun.

I'm well too. Thanks.

Mr Karimi

خانواده خوب هستند؟

khānevāde khub hastand?

Is the family well?

Mina

بله، همه خوب هستند. سلام می‌رسانند.

bale, hame khub hastand. salām mi-resānand.

Yes, everyone is well. They send their regards.

Mr Karimi

خدا را شکر. روز خوبی داشته باشید!

khodā rā shokr. ruz-e khub-i dāshte bāshid!

Thank God. Have a good day!

Mina

شما هم. خدا حافظ!

shomā ham. khodā hāfez!

You too. Goodbye!

Mr Karimi

خدا حافظ.

khodā hāfez.

Goodbye.

Salām – the word that opens every door

سلام salām hello literally means peace. Iranians say it to everyone – the taxi driver, the shopkeeper, a child, a stranger on the stairs – and it works at any hour. If you learn one word today, learn this one.

You will also hear مرسی mersi thanks (from French) and ممنون mamnun grateful everywhere; both mean thanks. The fuller متشکرم motshakker-am I thank you is a little more formal.

Persian has two words for you: تو to you (one person, informal) and شما shomā you (plural, or one person politely). With anyone you do not know well, use shomā – it is the safe, respectful choice, like French vous. Friends and family use to.

'I am', 'you are': the little endings

Persian usually does not use a separate word for am / are / is. Instead a short ending attaches to the adjective or noun:

So خوبم khub-am is one word meaning I am well, and خوبید khub-id means you are well. In writing, the ending is simply joined to the word. The one exception is he/she/it is, which is a separate word, است ast: حال شما چطور است؟ hāl-e shomā chetor ast? How is your state?

In speech, ast shrinks to -e: chetor-e? You will see the spoken forms marked 🗣 throughout this course.

PersianReadingEnglish
من خوبم man khub-am-amI am well
تو خوبی to khub-i-iyou (one friend) are well
او خوب است u khub astasthe / she is well
ما خوبیم mā khub-im-imwe are well
شما خوبید shomā khub-id-idyou (polite / plural) are well
آن‌ها خوبند ān-hā khub-and-andthey are well

من خوبم.

man khub-am.

I am well.

شما خوبید؟

shomā khub-id?

Are you well?

او معلم است.

u mo'allem ast.

He / she is a teacher.

Spoken او معلمه. u mo'alleme.

ما ایرانی هستیم.

mā irāni hastim.

We are Iranian.

hastim is the full, stressed form of -im: 'we ARE'.

Questions need no extra words

To ask a yes/no question, keep the word order and raise your voice at the end – exactly like English You're well? شما خوبید. shomā khub-id. You are well. becomes شما خوبید؟ shomā khub-id? Are you well? Notice the Persian question mark ؟ ? faces the other way.

Question words such as چطور chetor how go where the answer would go: حال شما چطور است؟ hāl-e shomā chetor ast? – your state how is?

خوبید؟

khub-id?

Are you well?

خانواده خوب هستند؟

khānevāde khub hastand?

Is the family well?

حالت چطور است؟

hāl-at chetor ast?

How are you? (to a friend)

Spoken حالت چطوره؟ hālet chetore?

Ta'ārof: the art of politeness

Iranians have a word for their famous politeness: تعارف ta'ārof. A shopkeeper may wave away your money with قابل ندارد ghābel nadārad it's not worthy (of you) – you are still expected to pay, and to insist. A host offers food three times; a guest refuses twice. Everyone knows the rules, and the dance is part of the pleasure of talking.

Two words from the bakery carry this spirit: بفرمایید befarmāyid (please – go ahead, here you are, come in, sit down) is the all-purpose polite invitation, and نوش جان nush-e jān (may it nourish your soul) is said to someone about to eat. You will hear both every day.

Notice also جان jān dear, soul after a name: Rezā jān. It is warm but not romantic – friends, parents, teachers and shopkeepers all use it.

Pronunciation

  • ā (as in salām, khodā) is a long, deep 'a' like the vowel in British father – keep it long.
  • kh (خ) is the rasping sound of Scottish loch or German Bach: khub, khodā hāfez.
  • Stress falls on the last syllable of most words: sa-LĀM, kho-DĀ, mam-NUN – but on the ending-less part of a verb: KHUB-am.
  • Persian 'r' is tapped like Spanish r, never swallowed like American r.
