Course / Beginner (A1) / Lesson 18

سرم درد می‌کندBody and health: my head hurts

sar-am dard mi-konad

Sooner or later every traveller needs a pharmacy. In this lesson you learn the parts of the body, how to say what hurts – in Persian your head 'does pain' – how to describe a cold or a fever, and how to understand the advice you get: you should rest, you must drink water. That last one uses باید, Persian's word for 'must', with the be- form of the verb you met in the imperative.

In this lesson you will learn to

  • name the main parts of the body
  • say what hurts with درد کردن: سرم درد می‌کند
  • say how you feel and buy medicine at a pharmacy
  • give and understand simple advice with باید + subjunctive: باید استراحت کنی

My head 'does pain'

English says I have a headache or my head hurts. Persian says that your head does pain: سرم درد می‌کند. sar-am dard mi-konad. My head hurts. The body part is the subject, with the possessive ending you learned in lesson 5 (sar-am, my head), and درد کردن dard kardan to ache is the verb.

For how you feel in general, use حال hāl state, condition: حالم خوب نیست. hāl-am khub nist. I don't feel well. – literally my state is not good. The answer to حالت چطور است؟ hāl-at chetor ast? is now more than khubam.

Two phrases every Iranian uses when someone is ill: خدا بد ندهد! khodā bad na-dahad! may God give you nothing bad, the Persian get well soon, and the reply بد نبینی! bad na-bini! may you see nothing bad. A cold, by the way, is something you eat: سرما خورده‌ام. sarmā khorde-am. I've caught a cold – literally I have eaten cold.

Saying what hurts: سرم درد می‌کند

Put the possessive ending on the body part and follow it with درد می‌کند dard mi-konad it hurts. The verb stays in the it form whoever is in pain, because the subject is the head, the tooth or the leg – not you.

After a vowel the endings take a y: پایم pā-yam my leg, گلویم galu-yam my throat. With two eyes or two feet, written Persian uses a plural verb – چشم‌هایم درد می‌کنند chashm-hā-yam dard mi-konand my eyes hurt – but in speech the singular is normal.

The negative is regular: سرم درد نمی‌کند. sar-am dard nemi-konad. My head doesn't hurt. And the doctor's first question is کجایتان درد می‌کند؟ kojā-yetān dard mi-konad? Where does it hurt? – literally where of yours hurts?

How do you feel? Symptoms

Most symptoms use verbs you already know. A fever is something you have, with داشتن dāshtan: تب دارم. tab dāram. A cough is something you do, with کردن kardan: سرفه می‌کنم. sorfe mi-konam. Learn سرما خورده‌ام sarmā khorde-am I've caught a cold as one chunk for now – it is the present perfect, which you will meet properly at A2.

To ask a friend how they feel, say حالت چطور است؟ hāl-at chetor ast?; to a stranger, حالتان چطور است؟ hāl-etān chetor ast? The good news is بهترم. behtar-am. I'm better.

You should rest: باید + subjunctive

باید bāyad means must, have to, should, and it never changes. The verb after it takes the be- form you met in the imperative (lesson 16), now with a person ending: باید بروم bāyad beravam I must go, باید بروی bāyad beravi you must go. This form is called the subjunctive (التزامی eltezāmi); you will use it a lot from now on.

Compound verbs drop the be-, just as in the imperative: باید استراحت کنید bāyad esterāhat konid you should rest. For must not, should not, add na- to bāyad: نباید بروی na-bāyad beravi you shouldn't go.

Warm foods, cold foods and Avicenna

Tell an Iranian grandmother you have a cold and she will not only say خدا بد ندهد khodā bad na-dahad – she will tell you what to eat. In traditional Persian medicine every food has a nature, طبع tab', that is either گرم garm warm or سرد sard cold, whatever its temperature. Dates, honey, walnuts and ginger are warm; yoghurt, cucumber, fish and watermelon are cold. Too much of one upsets your balance, so a cold is treated with warm things: barley soup, آش āsh, a hot drink of نبات nabāt rock sugar with saffron, and quince or mint distillates from the herbalist.

The system goes back to Galen and to Avicenna – ابن سینا Ebn-e Sinā (980–1037) – whose Canon of Medicine was the standard medical textbook in Europe for centuries. Modern Iran has excellent doctors and a pharmacy on every street, but the warm–cold idea is everywhere: ask why someone won't eat fish with yoghurt and you will hear about it.

Tips

  • The body part is the subject, so the verb is always the it form: sar-am dard mi-konad (my head hurts) – never dard mi-konam.
  • del is the heart of poetry and the tummy of everyday life: del-am dard mi-konad is a stomach ache, but del-am tang ast means 'I miss someone'.
  • In speech mi-konad becomes mi-kone and dandān becomes dandun: dandun-am dard mi-kone. The polite -id becomes -in: esterāhat konin.
  • When someone tells you they are ill, say khodā bad na-dahad! (spoken khodā bad nade). The answer is bad na-bini! – 'may you see nothing bad'.
  • Iranian pharmacists give advice freely and many simple medicines are sold over the counter. A prescription is a noskhe (نسخه).
