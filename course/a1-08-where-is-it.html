کجاست؟Where is it? Places and positions

kojā-st?

Where is the bank? Is there a pharmacy near here? And where are my keys? In this lesson you learn the places of an Iranian neighbourhood and the things in a flat, the little words that say where things are – on, under, next to, behind, in front of, between – and how to say 'there is' and 'there isn't'.

In this lesson you will learn to

  • ask where something is: … kojā-st?
  • say where things are with dar, ru-ye, zir-e, kenār-e, posht-e, jelo-ye, beyn-e, nazdik-e
  • say 'there is / there are' with hast and 'there isn't' with nist
  • name places in a city and things in a home

Tom has just moved into a flat in Mina's building. Mina comes by to say hello, and Tom asks her about the neighbourhood.

What's that, next to the door?

āhān! kelid-am injā-st! mamnun, Minā khānom.

Aha! My key is here! Thank you, Ms Mina.

Where is it? Is there one?

Two questions will get you round any Iranian neighbourhood. To ask where a particular place is, add ast to kojā: داروخانه کجاست؟ dāru-khāne kojā-st? where is the pharmacy? To ask whether there is one at all, use هست hast: این نزدیکی بانک هست؟ in nazdiki bānk hast? is there a bank near here? The answer is بله، هست bale, hast yes, there is or نه، نیست na, nist no, there isn't. این نزدیکی in nazdiki means round here, nearby.

The answers use small place words – روبه‌روی ru-be-ru-ye opposite, پشت posht-e behind, جلوی jelo-ye in front of, کنار kenār-e next to – and almost all of them are followed by the ezafe. That is the main grammar point of this lesson.

Notice also راستی rāsti by the way, the word Iranians use to change the subject.

On, under, next to: place words and the ezafe

Most Persian place words are really nouns – رو ru top, face, زیر zir underside, پشت posht back, کنار kenār side, edge – so they link to the next word with the ezafe: روی میز ru-ye miz on the table is literally the top of the table, and پشت در posht-e dar behind the door is the back of the door. After a vowel the ezafe is written ی: ru-ye, jelo-ye.

Only a handful of little words take no ezafe – در dar in, به be to, از az from, با bā with: در اتاق dar otāgh in the room.

بین beyn-e between takes two things joined by o: بین بانک و پارک beyn-e bānk o pārk between the bank and the park. With a pronoun you can use the endings from lesson 5: کنارم kenār-am next to me, زیرش zir-ash under it.

Where is it? – kojā-st

Where is …? is کجاست؟ kojā-st? – kojā + ast, written as one word: بانک کجاست؟ bānk kojā-st? where is the bank? For more than one thing, use the full verb: کلیدها کجا هستند؟ kelid-hā kojā hastand? where are the keys?

The answers follow the same pattern: اینجاست injā-st it's here, آنجاست ānjā-st it's there (spoken unjā-st), or a place word + ast: کنار پارک است kenār-e pārk ast it's next to the park. You don't need a word for it – the verb says it all.

Start with ببخشید bebakhshid when you ask a stranger, and finish with ممنون mamnun. In speech ast becomes -e: kenār-e pārk-e, nazdik-e.

There is, there are: hast and nist

To say that something exists somewhere – there is, there are – Persian uses هست hast, the form of to be you met in lesson 2, and usually puts the place first: روی میز یک کتاب هست ru-ye miz yek ketāb hast there is a book on the table. Things in the plural still take hast: در اتاق دو صندلی هست dar otāgh do sandali hast there are two chairs in the room; people usually take hastand.

The negative is نیست nist: اینجا بانک نیست injā bānk nist there is no bank here.

Compare the two: ast tells you where a known thing is; hast tells you that something is there.

Squares, streets and lanes: finding an address

Iranian addresses read like directions. They start big and get smaller: the city, a well-known square or main street, a smaller street, then the lane – کوچه kuche – and the house number, sometimes with the floor and the flat. A taxi driver needs the landmarks more than the number, so you will often hear an address given as Valiasr Street, after the square, opposite the mosque, the second lane on the left.

The great landmarks of Tehran are its squares, میدان meydān: Azadi Square in the west with its famous white tower, Enghelab in the centre, Vanak and Tajrish in the north. People arrange to meet سر کوچه sar-e kuche at the head of the lane or جلوی نانوایی jelo-ye nānvāyi in front of the bakery – exactly the language of this lesson.

If you get lost, ask. Iranians give directions generously – often with a long explanation, and sometimes by walking you there themselves.

Tips

  • Almost every place word takes the ezafe: ru-ye, zir-e, kenār-e, posht-e, jelo-ye, beyn-e, nazdik-e. Forgetting it is the classic beginner's mistake – dar is the main exception.
  • In speech ru-ye often shrinks to ru, and dar becomes tu: ru miz-e 'it's on the table', tu otāgh-e 'it's in the room'.
  • در is dar 'in' and also dar 'door' – posht-e dar is 'behind the door', dar otāgh is 'in the room'.
  • nazdik and dur also work on their own: bānk nazdik ast 'the bank is near'. 'Round here' is in nazdiki.
  • ast says where a known thing is (bānk kojā-st?); hast says that something exists (bānk hast?). Choose the verb by what you want to know.
