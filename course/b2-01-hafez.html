Course / Upper intermediate (B2) / Lesson 1

حافظ و نخستین غزل دیوانHafez: the first ghazal

Hāfez o nakhostin ghazal-e divān

Every edition of the Divan of Hafez opens with the same ghazal – and its very first half-line is Arabic, not Persian. In this lesson you read all seven couplets half-line by half-line, put each one into modern Persian, and learn the handful of images – the cupbearer, wine, the cup, the tress, the tavern, the rogue and the elder of the Magi – that carry a double meaning through the whole of Persian lyric poetry. Then you visit the poet's tomb in Shiraz and see how Iranians still open his book to ask about their future.

In this lesson you will learn to

  • read the seven couplets of Hafez's first ghazal aloud and follow each one in a modern Persian paraphrase
  • recognise the key images – ساقی، می، جام، زلف، میخانه، رند، پیر مغان – and give both their earthly and their mystical reading
  • untangle the old forms of poetry: مرا، گرت، ز، کز، زان، نبود 'is not', همی‌، مشو
  • take a fāl-e Hāfez and talk about Yalda night and the Hafezieh in Shiraz

The first ghazal of the Divan

Read each half-line (مصراع mesrā') aloud before you look at the English. Only the first and the last half-lines are Arabic; everything in between is the Persian of fourteenth-century Shiraz – and most of it is still understood today.

الا یا ایها الساقی ادر کأساً و ناولها

alā yā ayyohā as-sāghi ader ka'san va nāvelhā

Ho, cupbearer, pass the cup around and hand it to me –

Arabic. In recitation the vowel of the article is swallowed: ayyohas-sāghi. ادر ader pass round and ناولها nāvelhā hand it over are Arabic commands; the final -hā is the Arabic pronoun 'it', which sounds exactly like the Persian plural – and gives the whole poem its rhyme.

که عشق آسان نمود اول ولی افتاد مشکل‌ها

ke eshgh āsān nemud avval vali oftād moshkel-hā

for love seemed easy at first, but then the troubles came.

نمود nemud here means 'seemed', not 'did'. افتاد مشکل‌ها oftād moshkel-hā puts the verb before its subject; today we would say مشکل‌ها پیش آمد moshkel-hā pish āmad.

به بوی نافه‌ای کاخر صبا زان طره بگشاید

be bu-ye nāfe-i k-ākhar sabā z-ān torre bogshāyad

Waiting for the musk that the east wind will at last shake loose from those locks,

Literally: in hope of the scent of a musk-pod which at last the morning breeze may open from that lock

کاخر kākhar = که آخر ke ākhar; زان zān = از آن az ān. صبا sabā, the morning wind, carries messages between lovers in Persian poetry.

ز تاب جعد مشکینش چه خون افتاد در دل‌ها

ze tāb-e ja'd-e meshkin-ash che khun oftād dar del-hā

how many hearts have filled with blood from the twist of those musk-black curls!

Literally: from the twist of its musky curl what blood fell into hearts

A heart 'full of blood' (دل پرخون del-e por-khun) is a heart in torment. The beloved's hair is beautiful and it is a trap.

مرا در منزل جانان چه امن عیش چون هر دم

marā dar manzel-e jānān che amn-e eysh chon har dam

What safety, what pleasure can I have at the beloved's lodging, when every moment

مرا marā = 'for me'. A منزل manzel is a stopping place on a caravan road; جانان jānān, 'soul of souls', is the beloved.

جرس فریاد می‌دارد که بربندید محمل‌ها

jaras faryād mi-dārad ke barbandid mahmel-hā

the caravan bell cries out, 'Tie on your litters!'

The جرس jaras hangs on the lead camel; a محمل mahmel is the litter a traveller rode in. Life is a caravan that never lets you stay.

به می سجاده رنگین کن گرت پیر مغان گوید

be mey sajjāde rangin kon gar-at pir-e moghān guyad

Stain your prayer mat with wine if the elder of the Magi tells you to,

The most scandalous half-line of the poem: wine on a prayer mat. گرت garat = اگر به تو agar be to – the pronoun hangs on the 'if'.

که سالک بی‌خبر نبود ز راه و رسم منزل‌ها

ke sālek bi-khabar nabvad ze rāh o rasm-e manzel-hā

for the traveller on the path is not ignorant of the ways and customs of its stages.

نبود nabvad is the old present 'is not', from بود bovad – not the past nabud 'was not'. The metre tells you which: it needs two long syllables, nab-vad. A سالک sālek is a traveller on the Sufi path.

شب تاریک و بیم موج و گردابی چنین هایل

shab-e tārik o bim-e mowj o gerdāb-i chenin hāyel

A dark night, the fear of the waves and a whirlpool so terrible –

There is no verb at all: three nouns piled up like the waves themselves.

کجا دانند حال ما سبکباران ساحل‌ها

kojā dānand hāl-e mā sabokbārān-e sāhel-hā

how could they know our state, those who walk lightly laden on the shore?

کجا kojā 'where?' makes a rhetorical question: 'how could they?' means 'they cannot'. سبکبار sabokbār 'with a light load' – people with nothing at stake.

همه کارم ز خودکامی به بدنامی کشید آخر

hame kār-am ze khodkāmi be badnāmi keshid ākhar

Going my own way has brought all my affairs, in the end, to disgrace:

خودکامی khodkāmi 'doing as one pleases', بدنامی badnāmi 'a bad name'. The rend accepts a bad reputation as the price of being honest.

نهان کی ماند آن رازی کز او سازند محفل‌ها

nahān key mānad ān rāz-i k-az u sāzand mahfel-hā

how could a secret stay hidden when people hold gatherings to talk about it?

Literally: when will that secret stay hidden of which they make gatherings

کی key 'when?' is another question that means 'never'. کز او kaz u = که از آن ke az ān.

حضوری گر همی‌خواهی از او غایب مشو حافظ

hozur-i gar hami-khāhi az u ghāyeb masho Hāfez

If you want his presence, Hafez, do not be absent from him:

The poet names himself in the last couplet – his pen name, تخلص takhallos. همی‌خواهی hami-khāhi = می‌خواهی mi-khāhi; مشو masho = نشو na-sho.

متی ما تلق من تهوی دع الدنیا و اهملها

matā mā talgha man tahvā da' ed-donyā va ahmelhā

when you meet the one you love, leave the world and let it go.

Arabic again. The last word, اهملها ahmelhā abandon it, rhymes with ناولها nāvelhā in the first line: the poem ends where it began.

How the ghazal works – and why this one comes first

A ghazal (غزل ghazal) is a short lyric, usually of five to twelve couplets (بیت beyt), each made of two half-lines (مصراع mesrā'). In the first couplet, the مطلع matla', both halves rhyme; after that only the second half of each couplet does. Here the rhyme is -elhā: ناولها nāvelhā, مشکل‌ها moshkel-hā, دل‌ها del-hā, محمل‌ها mahmel-hā, منزل‌ها manzel-hā, ساحل‌ها sāhel-hā, محفل‌ها mahfel-hā, اهملها ahmelhā. Hafez is playing a game: the Arabic pronoun -hā 'it' at the start and at the end of the poem sounds exactly like the Persian plural -hā in between.

Each couplet is complete in itself – a string of pearls rather than a story – and in the last one the poet signs with his pen name, the تخلص takhallos. The metre is hazaj: four times da-DUM-DUM-DUM in every half-line. Tap the beat as you read the first line: a-LĀ-YĀ-AY / yo-HAS-SĀ-GHI / a-DER-KA'-SAN / va-NĀ-VEL-HĀ.

Why does the Divan begin here? After Hafez's death his poems were arranged in the alphabetical order of the last letter of their rhyme, so the ghazals that rhyme on alef come first, and this one stands at their head. Its theme suits an opening: love looks easy and is not; life is a caravan that will not let you rest, a night sea in a storm; the only way through is to let go of the world.

Two readings at once. On the surface the poem is about wine, a beautiful beloved with dark curls and a drinker who no longer cares what people say. Read as the Sufis read it, the wine is the love of God, the beloved is God, the caravan is the soul's journey and the elder of the Magi is the spiritual master. Hafez never tells you which reading is the right one – and Iranians have argued about it for six hundred years.

The ghazal in modern Persian

Iranian schoolchildren learn to understand a classical couplet the way you should: by turning it into prose (به نثر برگرداندن be nasr bargardāndan). Four moves do most of the work:

  1. Put the verb at the end and the subject first: افتاد مشکل‌ها oftād moshkel-hā → مشکل‌ها پیش آمد moshkel-hā pish āmad.
  2. Open the contractions: زان zān → از آن az ān, کز kaz → که از ke az, گرت garat → اگر به تو agar be to.
  3. Swap old words for today's: جانان jānān → معشوق ma'shugh, نهان nahān → پنهان penhān, هایل hāyel → هولناک howlnāk, می mey → شراب sharāb.
  4. Add what the poem leaves out – a verb, a 'when', a 'because'.

Couplet 7: Hafez, if you want to be with him, do not be absent from him; whenever you reach the one you love, let go of the world.

Seven images, two readings

Persian lyric poetry works with a small, fixed set of images, and Hafez uses them all. Each has an earthly meaning – a real tavern, real wine, a real beautiful face – and a mystical one that Sufi readers find beneath it. The double reading is not a code to be cracked once and for all; it is what lets one couplet of Hafez fit a wedding, a funeral and a prayer.

Watch also for the poem's villains: the زاهد zāhed (the ascetic), the صوفی sufi in his patched cloak and the محتسب mohtaseb, the inspector of public morals. In Hafez they are almost always hypocrites, while the drinker in the tavern is honest. That reversal is the heart of his poetry.

در خوانش عرفانی، شراب همان عشق الهی است.

dar khānesh-e erfāni, sharāb hamān eshgh-e elāhi ast.

In the mystical reading, wine is the love of God itself.

زلف یار هم زیباست و هم دامی برای دل عاشق.

zolf-e yār ham zibā-st o ham dām-i barā-ye del-e āshegh.

The beloved's hair is both beautiful and a trap for the lover's heart.

در شعر حافظ، میخانه جای صداقت است و صومعه جای ریا.

dar she'r-e Hāfez, meykhāne jā-ye sedāghat ast o sowme'e jā-ye riyā.

In Hafez's poetry the tavern is the place of honesty and the cloister the place of hypocrisy.

رند به حرف مردم اهمیت نمی‌دهد، اما از ریا بیزار است.

The rend does not care what people say, but he hates hypocrisy.

پیر مغان در اصل پیرمردی زرتشتی بود که شراب می‌فروخت.

pir-e moghān dar asl pirmard-i zartoshti bud ke sharāb mi-forukht.

The 'elder of the Magi' was originally an old Zoroastrian who sold wine.

زاهد مردم را از شراب می‌ترساند، ولی خودش ریاکار است.

zāhed mardom rā az sharāb mi-tarsānad, vali khod-ash riyākār ast.

The ascetic frightens people away from wine, but he himself is a hypocrite.

هر بیت حافظ را می‌توان هم زمینی خواند و هم آسمانی.

har beyt-e Hāfez rā mi-tavān ham zamini khānd o ham āsemāni.

Every couplet of Hafez can be read both as earthly and as heavenly.

Poetic grammar: the old forms in this ghazal

Hafez's Persian is close enough to today's that a Tehran newspaper reader follows most of it, but a handful of old forms turn up in almost every couplet. Learn the ones in this table and most classical poetry opens up (lesson 4 of this level goes further).

The examples are other famous couplets of Hafez that use the same forms. Many of them are quoted every day – a half-line of Hafez is the Iranian answer to a proverb.

یوسف گم‌گشته بازآید به کنعان غم مخور / کلبه‌ی احزان شود روزی گلستان غم مخور

Yusof-e gom-gashte bāz-āyad be Kan'ān gham ma-khor / kolbe-ye ahzān shavad ruz-i golestān gham ma-khor

Lost Joseph will come back to Canaan – do not grieve; the hut of sorrows will one day be a rose garden – do not grieve.

بازآید bāz-āyad = برمی‌گردد bar-mi-gardad (a bare present for the future); مخور makhor = نخور na-khor. غم خوردن gham khordan 'to grieve' is still in daily use.

هرگز نمیرد آن که دلش زنده شد به عشق / ثبت است بر جریده‌ی عالم دوام ما

hargez namirad ān ke del-ash zende shod be eshgh / sabt ast bar jaride-ye ālam davām-e mā

He whose heart has come alive through love will never die: our lasting is written in the register of the world.

نمیرد namirad = نمی‌میرد nemi-mirad. In زنده شد به عشق zende shod be eshgh the phrase with به follows the verb; in the second half the subject دوام ما davām-e mā comes last.

دوش دیدم که ملایک در میخانه زدند / گل آدم بسرشتند و به پیمانه زدند

dush didam ke malāyek dar-e meykhāne zadand / gel-e ādam besereshtand o be peymāne zadand

Last night I saw the angels knocking at the tavern door; they kneaded the clay of Adam and cast it in the wine measure.

دوش dush 'last night' is a poetic word. In بسرشتند besereshtand the be- on a past verb is common in classical Persian and adds nothing to the meaning.

ساقی به نور باده برافروز جام ما / مطرب بگو که کار جهان شد به کام ما

sāghi be nur-e bāde barafruz jām-e mā / motreb begu ke kār-e jahān shod be kām-e mā

Cupbearer, light up our cup with the glow of wine; minstrel, sing that the world has gone the way we wished.

شد به کام ما shod be kām-e mā = به کام ما شد be kām-e mā shod: the verb comes early again. بگو begu here means 'sing'.

سال‌ها دل طلب جام جم از ما می‌کرد / وان چه خود داشت ز بیگانه تمنا می‌کرد

sāl-hā del talab-e jām-e Jam az mā mi-kard / v-ān che khod dāsht ze bigāne tamannā mi-kard

For years my heart asked me for Jamshid's cup – begging from strangers for what it already had.

وان چه vān che = و آنچه va ān-che 'and that which'; ز ze = از az. The cup that shows the world was in the heart all along.

Hafez of Shiraz

A short life of the poet in plain modern Persian – the kind of text you would find in a school book or on a board at the tomb.

خواجه شمس‌الدین محمد حافظ شیرازی در قرن هشتم هجری در شیراز به دنیا آمد.

khāje Shams-od-din Mohammad Hāfez-e Shirāzi dar gharn-e hashtom-e hejri dar Shirāz be donyā āmad.

Khwaja Shams al-Din Mohammad Hafez of Shiraz was born in Shiraz in the eighth century of the Hijra (the fourteenth century AD).

او را «حافظ» می‌نامیدند، چون قرآن را از بر داشت.

u rā «Hāfez» mi-nāmidand, chon Ghor'ān rā az bar dāsht.

He was called 'Hafez' because he knew the Quran by heart.

حافظ hāfez means 'keeper, one who has memorised'; it comes from the same root as حفظ کردن hefz kardan to memorise.

تقریباً همه‌ی عمرش را در شیراز گذراند و کمتر از این شهر بیرون رفت.

taghriban hame-ye omr-ash rā dar Shirāz gozarānd o kam-tar az in shahr birun raft.

He spent almost his whole life in Shiraz and seldom left the city.

در روزگار او شیراز بارها دست به دست شد و شاهان و امیران می‌آمدند و می‌رفتند.

dar ruzgār-e u Shirāz bār-hā dast be dast shod o shāhān o amirān mi-āmadand o mi-raftand.

In his time Shiraz changed hands again and again; kings and princes came and went.

حافظ در شعرهایش از بعضی از این فرمانروایان ستایش کرده است.

Hāfez dar she'r-hā-yash az ba'zi az in farmānravāyān setāyesh karde ast.

In his poems Hafez praised some of these rulers.

اما بیشتر شعرهای او درباره‌ی عشق، شراب، ریا و آزادی است.

ammā bishtar-e she'r-hā-ye u darbāre-ye eshgh, sharāb, riyā o āzādi ast.

But most of his poems are about love, wine, hypocrisy and freedom.

دیوان او نزدیک به پانصد غزل دارد و بیشتر این غزل‌ها کوتاه‌اند.

divān-e u nazdik be pānsad ghazal dārad o bishtar-e in ghazal-hā kutāh-and.

His Divan contains nearly five hundred ghazals, and most of them are short.

می‌گویند دوستش محمد گلندام شعرهای او را پس از مرگش جمع کرد.

mi-guyand dust-ash Mohammad Golandām she'r-hā-ye u rā pas az marg-ash jam' kard.

It is said that his friend Mohammad Golandam collected his poems after his death.

حافظ در سال ۷۹۲ هجری قمری، یعنی ۱۳۹۰ میلادی، در شیراز درگذشت.

Hāfez dar sāl-e haftsad-o-navad-o-do-ye hejri-ye ghamari, ya'ni hezār-o-sisad-o-navad-e milādi, dar Shirāz dargozasht.

Hafez died in Shiraz in the year 792 of the lunar Hijri calendar, that is, 1390 AD.

آرامگاه او در باغی به نام حافظیه است و هر روز هزاران نفر به دیدنش می‌روند.

ārāmgāh-e u dar bāgh-i be nām-e Hāfeziye ast o har ruz hezārān nafar be didan-ash mi-ravand.

His tomb is in a garden called the Hafezieh, and every day thousands of people go to visit it.

بنای کنونی آرامگاه را در سال ۱۳۱۴ خورشیدی ساختند.

banā-ye konuni-ye ārāmgāh rā dar sāl-e hezār-o-sisad-o-chahārdah-e khorshidi sākhtand.

The present building of the tomb was put up in the solar year 1314 (1935).

روی سنگ قبر او بیت‌هایی از غزل‌هایش کنده شده است.

ru-ye sang-e ghabr-e u beyt-hā-yi az ghazal-hā-yash kande shode ast.

Couplets from his ghazals are carved on his tombstone.

می‌گویند در هر خانه‌ی ایرانی دو کتاب پیدا می‌شود: قرآن و دیوان حافظ.

mi-guyand dar har khāne-ye irāni do ketāb peydā mi-shavad: Ghor'ān o divān-e Hāfez.

They say that in every Iranian home you will find two books: the Quran and the Divan of Hafez.

خیلی‌ها هنوز در لحظه‌های تردید دیوان را باز می‌کنند و از حافظ فال می‌گیرند.

kheyli-hā hanuz dar lahze-hā-ye tardid divān rā bāz mi-konand o az Hāfez fāl mi-girand.

Many people still open the Divan in moments of doubt and take an omen from Hafez.

به همین دلیل به او «لسان‌الغیب» یعنی «زبان غیب» می‌گویند.

be hamin dalil be u «lesān-ol-gheyb» ya'ni «zabān-e gheyb» mi-guyand.

That is why he is called Lesān al-Gheyb, 'the Tongue of the Unseen'.

گوته، شاعر آلمانی، چنان شیفته‌ی حافظ شد که «دیوان غربی‌شرقی» را با الهام از او سرود.

Gote, shā'er-e ālmāni, chonān shifte-ye Hāfez shod ke «divān-e gharbi-sharghi» rā bā elhām az u sorud.

Goethe, the German poet, was so taken with Hafez that he wrote his West–Eastern Divan inspired by him.

At the Hafezieh

Tom is spending the Nowruz holidays in Shiraz. At sunset Leila takes him to the Hafezieh, where people sit by the tomb reading poems. Listen for how Leila explains the fāl, and for the words people say to Hafez before they open the book.

Tom

چرا این‌قدر شلوغ است؟ امروز تعطیل است؟

cherā in-ghadr sholugh ast? emruz ta'til ast?

Why is it so crowded? Is today a holiday?

Spoken چرا این‌قدر شلوغه؟ امروز تعطیله؟ cherā in-ghadr sholugh-e? emruz ta'til-e?

Leila

نه، غروب‌ها همیشه همین‌طور است. مردم به این‌جا می‌آیند تا کنار حافظ شعر بخوانند.

na, ghorub-hā hamishe hamin-towr ast. mardom be in-jā mi-āyand tā kenār-e Hāfez she'r bekhānand.

No, it's always like this at sunset. People come here to read poetry beside Hafez.

Spoken نه، غروبا همیشه همین‌طوره. مردم میان این‌جا که کنار حافظ شعر بخونن. na, ghorub-ā hamishe hamin-towr-e. mardom miyān in-jā ke kenār-e Hāfez she'r bekhunan.

Tom

آن آقا کنار قبر چه کار می‌کند؟ دارد گریه می‌کند؟

ān āghā kenār-e ghabr che kār mi-konad? dārad gerye mi-konad?

What is that man doing by the grave? Is he crying?

Spoken اون آقا کنار قبر چی کار می‌کنه؟ داره گریه می‌کنه؟ un āghā kenār-e ghabr chi kār mi-kone? dāre gerye mi-kone?

Leila

نه، دارد غزل می‌خواند. بعضی‌ها همه‌ی دیوان را حفظ‌اند.

na, dārad ghazal mi-khānad. ba'zi-hā hame-ye divān rā hefz-and.

No, he's reciting a ghazal. Some people know the whole Divan by heart.

Spoken نه، داره غزل می‌خونه. بعضیا کل دیوون رو حفظن. na, dāre ghazal mi-khune. ba'zi-yā koll-e divun ro hefz-an.

Tom

شنیده‌ام که این‌جا فال حافظ هم می‌گیرند. تو تا حالا فال گرفته‌ای؟

shenide-am ke in-jā fāl-e Hāfez ham mi-girand. to tā hālā fāl gerefte-i?

I've heard people take a fāl of Hafez here too. Have you ever done it?

Spoken شنیدم این‌جا فال حافظ هم می‌گیرن. تو تا حالا فال گرفتی؟ shenidam in-jā fāl-e Hāfez ham mi-giran. to tā hālā fāl gerefti?

Leila

معلوم است! هر سال شب یلدا مادربزرگم برای همه‌ی ما فال می‌گیرد.

ma'lum ast! har sāl shab-e Yaldā mādarbozorg-am barā-ye hame-ye mā fāl mi-girad.

Of course! Every year on Yalda night my grandmother takes a fāl for all of us.

Spoken معلومه! هر سال شب یلدا مامان‌بزرگم واسه‌ی همه‌مون فال می‌گیره. ma'lum-e! har sāl shab-e Yaldā māmān-bozorg-am vāse-ye hame-mun fāl mi-gire.

Tom

چطوری؟ فقط کتاب را باز می‌کنید؟

chetowri? faghat ketāb rā bāz mi-konid?

How? You just open the book?

Spoken چطوری؟ فقط کتابو باز می‌کنین؟ chetowri? faghat ketāb-o bāz mi-konin?

نه، اول نیت می‌کنی، یعنی آرزویت را در دلت می‌گویی و به کسی نمی‌گویی.

na, avval niyyat mi-koni, ya'ni ārezu-yat rā dar del-at mi-guyi o be kas-i nemi-guyi.

No, first you make your intention – you say your wish in your heart and tell no one.

Spoken نه، اول نیت می‌کنی، یعنی آرزوتو تو دلت می‌گی و به کسی نمی‌گی. na, avval niyyat mi-koni, ya'ni ārezu-t-o tu del-at mi-gi o be kas-i nemi-gi.

بعد؟

And then?

بعد می‌گویی «ای حافظ شیرازی، تو محرم هر رازی» و اولین غزلی را که می‌آید می‌خوانی.

ba'd mi-guyi «ey Hāfez-e Shirāzi, to mahram-e har rāz-i» o avvalin ghazal-i rā ke mi-āyad mi-khāni.

Then you say, 'O Hafez of Shiraz, you who know every secret,' and you read the first ghazal that comes up.

Spoken بعد می‌گی «ای حافظ شیرازی، تو محرم هر رازی» و اولین غزلی رو که میاد می‌خونی. ba'd mi-gi «ey Hāfez-e Shirāzi, to mahram-e har rāz-i» o avvalin ghazal-i ro ke miyād mi-khuni.

باشد، نیت کردم. ای حافظ شیرازی، تو محرم هر رازی…

bāshad, niyyat kardam. ey Hāfez-e Shirāzi, to mahram-e har rāz-i…

All right, I've made my wish. O Hafez of Shiraz, you who know every secret…

Spoken باشه، نیت کردم. ای حافظ شیرازی، تو محرم هر رازی… bāshe, niyyat kardam. ey Hāfez-e Shirāzi, to mahram-e har rāz-i…

bebin che āmad: «Yusof-e gom-gashte bāz-āyad be Kan'ān gham ma-khor»!

What does that mean? I'm not Joseph!

Asking Hafez: the fāl, Yalda and the tomb

The fāl. No other poet is used the way Hafez is. To take a فال حافظ fāl-e Hāfez you hold a question or a wish in your mind (نیت کردن niyyat kardan), often say a little formula – ای حافظ شیرازی، تو محرم هر رازی ey Hāfez-e Shirāzi, to mahram-e har rāz-i O Hafez of Shiraz, confidant of every secret – and some add an oath 'by your Shakh-e Nabat', the beloved of the Hafez legends. Then you open the Divan at random and read the ghazal in front of you. Because his couplets are so open, almost any ghazal can be read as an answer, and the reader – usually the oldest person in the room – explains it with great seriousness and some mischief.

Yalda. On شب یلدا shab-e Yaldā, the longest night of the year (the night before the first of Dey, around 21 December), families sit up late around watermelon, pomegranates and dried fruit and nuts, and the Divan comes out: everyone gets a fāl. For many Iranians a Yalda without Hafez is not Yalda.

In the street. At traffic lights in Tehran children sell little envelopes with a ghazal inside – a فال fāl for a few thousand tomans – and in parks you may still meet a فال‌گیر fālgir with a small bird that picks a card for you.

The tomb. The حافظیه Hāfeziye in Shiraz is a garden of orange trees and cypresses with an open pavilion of eight columns over the poet's tombstone. People come at dusk, touch the stone, recite, sing and – of course – take a fāl. The 20th of Mehr (around 12 October) is Hafez Day in the Iranian calendar, and the garden is full of readings and music.

One old story claims that Hafez borrowed his Arabic first line from a verse attributed to the Umayyad caliph Yazid – a scandal for Shi'i readers, who remember Yazid as the killer of Imam Hossein. Scholars doubt the story, but Iranians still enjoy telling it, together with the joking defence that 'the property of an unbeliever is lawful for a believer'.

Tips

  • Read a couplet aloud before you translate it. The metre often decides an ambiguous word: نبود in couplet 4 must be nabvad 'is not' (two long syllables), not nabud 'was not'.
  • Nine-tenths of the 'difficulty' of classical poetry is contractions. When you see ز، زان، کز، کاخر، وان, read از، از آن، که از، که آخر، و آن.
  • Don't try to decide whether a ghazal is 'really' about wine or about God. Iranian readers keep both meanings in mind at once – that is the pleasure of it.
  • The beloved in Persian poetry has no gender: او is both 'he' and 'she'. Translators have to choose; in Persian you never do.
  • Look up the modern word, not the poetic one: نهان → پنهان، هایل → هولناک، جانان → معشوق، می → شراب. Dictionaries of everyday Persian often leave the poetic words out.
  • Learn two or three couplets by heart. Iranians quote Hafez constantly, and a well-chosen half-line at the dinner table is the best compliment you can pay your hosts.
