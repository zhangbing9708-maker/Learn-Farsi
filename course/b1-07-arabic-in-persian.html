عربی در فارسیArabic in Persian: roots, plurals and tanvin

arabi dar fārsi

Persian and Arabic are not related – one is Indo-European, the other Semitic – but they have lived side by side for fourteen centuries, and a large part of Persian vocabulary, especially in formal and written language, comes from Arabic. These words arrive in families built on three-consonant roots and a set of patterns. Once you know a dozen patterns, words like استخدام or مذاکره stop being a mystery. In this lesson you also learn the Arabic plurals you will meet in writing (کتب، علما، اخبار), the adverbs in -an (لطفاً، مثلاً، تقریباً), and the two Arabic letters ة and ء.

In this lesson you will learn to

  • see the three-consonant root inside Arabic words: علم، عالم، معلم، معلوم، تعلیم
  • recognise the main patterns: فاعل، مفعول، تفعیل، مفاعله، افتعال، استفعال …
  • understand broken plurals such as کتب، اخبار، علما، آثار – and know when to use the Persian plural instead
  • use the tanvin adverbs: لطفاً، مثلاً، تقریباً، حتماً، معمولاً
  • read the letters ة and ء and the hamza seats ئ أ ؤ

Two languages, one dictionary

A short history of Arabic in Persian. Many of the words in it are Arabic themselves – the word list and the grammar below show you how to spot them.

زبان فارسی و زبان عربی از دو خانواده‌ی متفاوت هستند.

zabān-e fārsi va zabān-e arabi az do khānevāde-ye motafāvet hastand.

Persian and Arabic come from two different language families.

فارسی یک زبان هندواروپایی است، اما عربی یک زبان سامی است.

fārsi yek zabān-e hendo-orupāyi ast, ammā arabi yek zabān-e sāmi ast.

Persian is an Indo-European language, while Arabic is a Semitic one.

بعد از ورود اسلام به ایران، کلمه‌های عربی کم‌کم وارد زبان فارسی شدند.

ba'd az vorud-e eslām be Irān, kalame-hā-ye arabi kam-kam vāred-e zabān-e fārsi shodand.

After Islam came to Iran, Arabic words gradually entered the Persian language.

اما دستور زبان فارسی تغییر نکرد و فارسی، فارسی ماند.

ammā dastur-e zabān-e fārsi taghyir nakard o fārsi, fārsi mānd.

But Persian grammar did not change, and Persian stayed Persian.

فردوسی در شاهنامه از کلمه‌های عربی بسیار کم استفاده کرد.

Ferdowsi dar Shāhnāme az kalame-hā-ye arabi besyār kam estefāde kard.

Ferdowsi used very few Arabic words in the Shahnameh.

سعدی و حافظ آزادانه از هر دو زبان استفاده کردند.

Sa'di va Hāfez āzādāne az har do zabān estefāde kardand.

Saadi and Hafez drew freely on both languages.

امروز تقریباً هر جمله‌ی فارسی چند کلمه‌ی عربی دارد.

emruz taghriban har jomle-ye fārsi chand kalame-ye arabi dārad.

Today almost every Persian sentence contains a few Arabic words.

مثلاً در جمله‌ی «معلم کتاب را روی میز گذاشت»، «معلم» و «کتاب» عربی‌اند.

masalan dar jomle-ye «mo'allem ketāb rā ru-ye miz gozāsht», «mo'allem» va «ketāb» arabi-and.

For example, in the sentence 'The teacher put the book on the table', 'teacher' and 'book' are Arabic.

این کلمه‌ها بیشتر از ریشه‌های سه‌حرفی ساخته شده‌اند.

in kalame-hā bishtar az rishe-hā-ye se-harfi sākhte shode-and.

These words are mostly built from three-letter roots.

مثلاً عالم، معلم، معلوم و تعلیم همه از ریشه‌ی «علم» هستند.

masalan ālem, mo'allem, ma'lum va ta'lim hame az rishe-ye «elm» hastand.

For example, ālem (scholar), mo'allem (teacher), ma'lum (known) and ta'lim (teaching) all come from the root of elm (knowledge).

بیشتر این کلمات را با الگوهای مشخصی می‌سازند.

bishtar-e in kalamāt rā bā olgu-hā-ye moshakhkhas-i mi-sāzand.

Most of these words are made with fixed patterns.

اگر این الگوها را بشناسید، معنی بسیاری از کلمه‌های تازه را حدس می‌زنید.

agar in olgu-hā rā beshenāsid, ma'ni-ye besyār-i az kalame-hā-ye tāze rā hads mi-zanid.

If you know these patterns, you can guess the meaning of many new words.

برای ایرانی‌ها این کلمه‌ها دیگر عربی نیستند؛ آن‌ها کاملاً فارسی‌اند.

barā-ye irāni-hā in kalame-hā digar arabi nistand; ān-hā kāmelan fārsi-and.

For Iranians these words are no longer Arabic: they are completely Persian.

Roots: three consonants and a pattern

An Arabic word is built like a mould filled with metal. The root is usually three consonants that carry the meaning – ک ت ب k-t-b writing, ع ل م '-l-m knowing, ف ع ل f-'-l doing. The pattern is a frame of vowels and extra letters that says what kind of word it is: a doer, a thing done, an action, a place.

Pour ک ت ب k-t-b into different patterns and you get کاتب kāteb scribe – the one who writes, مکتوب maktub written, مکتب maktab old-style school – the place of writing and کتاب ketāb book. Pour in ع ل م '-l-m and you get عالم ālem scholar, معلوم ma'lum known, معلم mo'allem teacher – one who makes others know and تعلیم ta'lim teaching.

Grammarians name the patterns with the dummy root ف ع ل f-'-l: the 'doer' pattern is called فاعل fā'el, the 'done' pattern مفعول maf'ul. You don't need Arabic grammar to use them. Persian treats all these words as ordinary nouns and adjectives, and makes verbs from them with کردن kardan and شدن shodan: تعمیر کردن ta'mir kardan to repair, استفاده کردن estefāde kardan to use, معلوم شدن ma'lum shodan to become clear.

Eleven patterns worth knowing

Each pattern below is named after the dummy root ف ع ل. To use the table, look at the letters around the three root consonants: an initial م m often means a doer, a thing done or a place; an initial ت t often means an action; است est- at the start means seeking or using. Then check the meaning in context – patterns are a guide, not a law.

Most of the action nouns (تفعیل، مفاعله، افعال، افتعال، استفعال) become verbs with کردن kardan and شدن shodan: تعمیر کردن ta'mir kardan to repair, مذاکره کردن mozākere kardan to negotiate, اعلام شدن e'lām shodan to be announced, استخدام کردن estekhdām kardan to hire.

Broken plurals

Arabic often makes a plural by changing the inside of the word, like English man – men or mouse – mice: کتاب ketāb → کتب kotob books. These are called broken plurals (جمع مکسر jam'-e mokassar). Arabic also has regular endings: ـات -āt, mostly for things, and ـین -in for people (اطلاعات ettelā'āt information, معلمین mo'allemin teachers).

Persian borrowed many of these plurals, but you can almost always use the Persian plural instead – and in speech you should: کتاب‌ها ketāb-hā, not کتب. Broken plurals live in formal writing, and a few have become ordinary words with a meaning of their own: اخبار akhbār the news, اطلاعات ettelā'āt information, آثار āsār works (of an artist), حقوق hoghugh salary; law; rights, ادبیات adabiyāt literature.

The Arabic ending ـات -āt has even spread to Persian words: ده deh village → دهات dehāt villages, باغ bāgh garden → باغات bāghāt orchards, and, after a vowel, ـجات -jāt: سبزی sabzi → سبزیجات sabzijāt vegetables, میوه mive → میوه‌جات mive-jāt fruit of all kinds.

Tanvin, ة and ء

Tanvin. The ending ـاً -an – an alef with two little strokes on top – turns an Arabic noun or adjective into an adverb, like English -ly: کامل kāmel complete → کاملاً kāmelan completely. You hear these adverbs all day long, in the most informal speech: لطفاً lotfan please, مثلاً masalan for example; like, حتماً hatman definitely, اصلاً aslan (not) at all. Use tanvin only with Arabic words: گاهاً gāhan (from Persian گاه gāh) is common but considered wrong – say گاهی gāhi sometimes.

ة (تای گرد tā-ye gerd round t) ends many Arabic feminine nouns. Persian rewrites it: as ت t where it is pronounced t – رحمت rahmat mercy, دولت dowlat government – and as ه e where it is pronounced e – مدرسه madrese school, جمله jomle sentence. You will see ة itself only in a few fixed phrases, like دایرةالمعارف dāyerat-ol-ma'āref encyclopedia.

ء (همزه hamze hamza) is a glottal stop, the catch in English uh-oh; in Persian it sounds just like ع 'eyn. On its own it appears at the end of a few words (جزء joz' part, شیء shey' object, سوء su' bad, mis-); inside a word it sits on a 'seat': ئ (مسئول mas'ul), أ (تأثیر ta'sir), ؤ (سؤال so'āl). At the end of many words Persian simply drops it: Arabic املاء becomes Persian املا emlā dictation.

Why two plurals?

In his Persian class, Tom asks Ms Ahmadi about the Arabic words he keeps meeting in the newspaper. Ms Ahmadi speaks the careful Persian of the classroom; Tom speaks like his Tehrani friends.

Tom

خانم احمدی، در روزنامه نوشته بود «اخبار». این همان «خبرها» است؟

khānom-e Ahmadi, dar ruznāme neveshte bud «akhbār». in hamān «khabar-hā» ast?

Ms Ahmadi, the paper said 'akhbār'. Is that the same as 'khabar-hā'?

Spoken خانم احمدی، تو روزنامه نوشته بود «اخبار». یعنی همون «خبرها»؟ khānom-e Ahmadi, tu ruznāme neveshte bud «akhbār». ya'ni hamun «khabar-hā»?

Ms Ahmadi

بله. «اخبار» جمع عربی «خبر» است و بیشتر در نوشته‌های رسمی به کار می‌رود.

bale. «akhbār» jam'-e arabi-ye «khabar» ast va bishtar dar neveshte-hā-ye rasmi be kār mi-ravad.

Yes. 'Akhbār' is the Arabic plural of 'khabar', and it's used mostly in formal writing.

Tom

پس «کتب» هم یعنی «کتاب‌ها»؟

pas «kotob» ham ya'ni «ketāb-hā»?

So 'kotob' means 'ketāb-hā' too?

Spoken پس «کتب» هم یعنی «کتابا»؟ pas «kotob» ham ya'ni «ketābā»?

Ms Ahmadi

دقیقاً! اما در گفتگو معمولاً «کتاب‌ها» می‌گوییم.

daghighan! ammā dar goftogu ma'mulan «ketāb-hā» mi-guyim.

Exactly! But in conversation we usually say 'ketāb-hā'.

Tom

چرا «معلم» و «تعلیم» و «علم» این‌قدر شبیه هم هستند؟

cherā «mo'allem» va «ta'lim» va «elm» in-ghadr shabih-e ham hastand?

Why are 'mo'allem', 'ta'lim' and 'elm' so alike?

Spoken چرا «معلم» و «تعلیم» و «علم» این‌قدر شبیه همن؟ cherā «mo'allem» o «ta'lim» o «elm» in-ghadr shabih-e ham-an?

Ms Ahmadi

چون همه از یک ریشه‌اند: ع، ل، م – یعنی دانستن.

chon hame az yek rishe-and: eyn, lām, mim – ya'ni dānestan.

Because they all come from one root – ʿ, l, m – which means 'knowing'.

Tom

و آن حرف عجیب وسط «دایرةالمعارف» چیست؟

va ān harf-e ajib-e vasat-e «dāyerat-ol-ma'āref» chist?

And what's that strange letter in the middle of 'dāyerat-ol-ma'āref'?

Spoken اون حرف عجیب وسط «دایرةالمعارف» چیه؟ un harf-e ajib-e vasat-e «dāyerat-ol-ma'āref» chiye?

Ms Ahmadi

آن «ة» است، یک حرف عربی. در فارسی معمولاً «ت» یا «ه» می‌شود: «رحمت» و «مدرسه».

ān «tā» ast, yek harf-e arabi. dar fārsi ma'mulan «te» yā «he» mi-shavad: «rahmat» va «madrese».

That's ة, an Arabic letter. In Persian it usually becomes ت or ه: rahmat (mercy), madrese (school).

Tom

و «ء» در «سوءتفاهم»؟

va «hamze» dar «su'-e-tafāhom»?

And the ء in 'su'-e tafāhom' (misunderstanding)?

Spoken «ء» تو «سوءتفاهم» چی؟ «hamze» tu «su'-e-tafāhom» chi?

Ms Ahmadi

آن همزه است، یعنی یک مکث کوچک در صدا، مثل «ع».

ān hamze ast, ya'ni yek maks-e kuchak dar sedā, mesl-e «eyn».

That's the hamza – a little catch in the voice, like ع.

Tom

فارسی واقعاً پر از عربی است!

fārsi vāghe'an por az arabi ast!

Persian really is full of Arabic!

Spoken فارسی واقعاً پر از عربیه! fārsi vāghe'an por az arabi-ye!

Ms Ahmadi

اتفاقاً «واقعاً» هم عربی است!

ettefāghan «vāghe'an» ham arabi ast!

As it happens, 'vāghe'an' – 'really' – is Arabic too!

Three ways to say thank you

Ask an Iranian to say thank you and you may get five answers from three languages. مرسی mersi is French, and the most common word in Tehran. ممنون mamnun grateful and متشکرم motashakker-am I am thankful are Arabic – the second built on the pattern تفعّل tafa''ol you met in this lesson. سپاسگزارم sepāsgozār-am I am grateful is pure Persian and sounds solemn, almost literary. And the warmest of all, دستت درد نکند dast-at dard nakonad may your hand not hurt, is a Persian idiom that no dictionary of loanwords can claim.

Not everyone has been happy with the mixture. In the 1930s Iran's first language academy, the فرهنگستان farhangestān, replaced many Arabic and French words with new Persian ones, and some of its inventions are now so ordinary that nobody remembers they were ever new: دانشگاه dāneshgāh university, شهرداری shahrdāri town hall, فرودگاه forudgāh airport. Today's academy mostly fights English: رایانه rāyāne for computer, پیامک payāmak for text message, یارانه yārāne for subsidy. Some caught on – everyone says پیامک and یارانه – and some did not: most Iranians still call a computer کامپیوتر kāmpyuter.

Ferdowsi, who wrote the Shahnameh with very few Arabic words a thousand years ago, is the hero of the purists. But Saadi and Hafez, who mixed the two languages freely, are the poets every Iranian can quote.

Tips

  • Learn Arabic words in families. Once you know علم 'knowledge', you can guess عالم, معلم, معلوم, تعلیم and علوم.
  • In speech, use the Persian plural: کتاب‌ها, خبرها, کلمه‌ها. Keep broken plurals for reading – except the few that are now everyday words: اخبار, اطلاعات, آثار, ادبیات.
  • Don't add tanvin to Persian words. گاهاً, زباناً and تلفناً are heard, but careful speakers say گاهی, زبانی, تلفنی.
  • مثلاً is also a filler, like English 'like': رفتیم، مثلاً، یه رستوران… 'we went to, like, a restaurant…'.
  • In Persian ع and ء are both a glottal stop, and in fast speech often just a pause or nothing at all: مسئول sounds like masul, معلم like moallem.
  • Some letters are a clue: ث، ح، ذ، ص، ض، ط، ظ، ع and ة almost always mean that a word came from Arabic.
