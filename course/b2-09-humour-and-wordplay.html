Course / Upper intermediate (B2) / Lesson 9

طنز و بازی با کلماتHumour and wordplay: Mulla, the cat and the pun

tanz o bāzi bā kalamāt

Iranians are great tellers of jokes, and they have been laughing in Persian for a very long time: at Mulla Nasreddin's crooked logic, at Obeyd Zakani's hypocritical cat, at puns that only work in Persian. In this lesson you read two classic comic tales, learn the words for joking, teasing and satire – and the difference between a friendly tease and a rude remark – see how Persian puns (جناس) work, and practise telling a joke the way Iranians tell it.

In this lesson you will learn to

  • read and retell a Mulla Nasreddin anecdote and Obeyd Zakani's tale of the mouse and the cat
  • tell a friendly tease (سر به سر گذاشتن، شوخی کردن) from mockery and rude remarks (مسخره کردن، متلک انداختن)
  • recognise puns built on Persian homonyms such as شیر، سیر، باز and گور
  • tell a short joke in the storytelling present: یک روز ملا می‌رود…

The pot that had a baby

Mulla Nasreddin – usually just 'the Mulla' – is the wise fool of a thousand stories told from the Balkans to Central Asia. Here is one of the best known, retold in plain written Persian.

روزی ملا از همسایه‌اش دیگی قرض گرفت.

ruz-i Mollā az hamsāye-ash dig-i gharz gereft.

One day the Mulla borrowed a big cooking pot from his neighbour.

چند روز بعد دیگ را پس برد و یک قابلمه‌ی کوچک هم در آن گذاشت.

chand ruz ba'd dig rā pas bord o yek ghāblame-ye kuchak ham dar ān gozāsht.

A few days later he took the pot back, with a little saucepan inside it.

همسایه با تعجب پرسید: «این قابلمه چیست؟»

hamsāye bā ta'ajjob porsid: «in ghāblame chist?»

The neighbour asked in surprise, 'What's this saucepan?'

ملا گفت: «مبارک باشد! دیگ شما زایید.»

Mollā goft: «mobārak bāshad! dig-e shomā zāyid.»

The Mulla said, 'Congratulations! Your pot has had a baby.'

همسایه خندید و با خوشحالی دیگ و قابلمه را گرفت.

hamsāye khandid o bā khoshhāli dig o ghāblame rā gereft.

The neighbour laughed and happily took the pot and the saucepan.

مدتی گذشت و ملا دوباره دیگ را قرض خواست.

moddat-i gozasht o Mollā dobāre dig rā gharz khāst.

Some time passed, and the Mulla asked to borrow the pot again.

همسایه این بار با کمال میل آن را به او داد.

hamsāye in bār bā kamāl-e meyl ān rā be u dād.

This time the neighbour gave it to him most willingly.

اما روزها و هفته‌ها گذشت و از دیگ خبری نشد.

ammā ruz-hā o hafte-hā gozasht o az dig khabar-i na-shod.

But days and weeks went by, and there was no sign of the pot.

آخر همسایه به در خانه‌ی ملا رفت و دیگش را خواست.

ākhar hamsāye be dar-e khāne-ye Mollā raft o dig-ash rā khāst.

In the end the neighbour went to the Mulla's door and asked for his pot.

ملا سرش را پایین انداخت و گفت: «تسلیت می‌گویم؛ دیگ شما مرد.»

Mollā sar-ash rā pāyin andākht o goft: «tasliyat mi-guyam; dig-e shomā mord.»

The Mulla hung his head and said, 'My condolences – your pot has died.'

همسایه فریاد زد: «دیگ که نمی‌میرد!»

hamsāye faryād zad: «dig ke nemi-mirad!»

The neighbour shouted, 'A pot can't die!'

ملا آرام جواب داد: «وقتی باور کردی که دیگ می‌زاید، چرا باور نمی‌کنی که می‌میرد؟»

Mollā ārām javāb dād: «vaghti bāvar kardi ke dig mi-zāyad, cherā bāvar nemi-koni ke mi-mirad?»

The Mulla answered calmly, 'When you believed that a pot could give birth, why won't you believe that it can die?'

از همین حکایت ضرب‌المثلی ساخته‌اند: «دیگی که زایید، سر زا هم می‌رود.»

az hamin hekāyat zarb-ol-masal-i sākhte-and: «dig-i ke zāyid, sar-e zā ham mi-ravad.»

A proverb has been made from this very story: 'A pot that gives birth can also die in childbirth.'

یعنی اگر از چیز عجیبی سود بردی، ضررش را هم باید قبول کنی.

ya'ni agar az chiz-e ajib-i sud bordi, zarar-ash rā ham bāyad ghabul koni.

That is: if you profited from something strange, you must accept its losses too.

How Iranians joke

Persian humour has a few favourite moves:

  • Crooked logic. The Mulla never lies outright; he takes an idea seriously until it turns absurd – a pot that gives birth must be able to die.
  • The quick retort. Iranians admire حاضرجوابی hāzer-javābi quick-wittedness, the answer that turns the tables in one line.
  • Wordplay. Persian is full of words that look or sound alike, and poets have played with them for a thousand years. The rhetorical term is جناس jenās.
  • Satire. From Obeyd Zakani in the fourteenth century to today's newspapers and social media, طنز tanz has been a safe-ish way to criticise the powerful – a cat can say what a person cannot.
  • Teasing among friends. سر به سر گذاشتن sar be sar gozāshtan is a sign of closeness: you tease people you like.

Jokes travel fast in Iran – by text message, in shared taxis, at family gatherings. Like everywhere, some joke cycles target particular towns or peoples. This course leaves them out, and it is wise to do the same: they are the quickest way to stop people laughing with you.

Finally, Iranians laugh a great deal at themselves – at ta'ārof, at traffic, at the Persian love of long titles. Self-mockery is often the friendliest humour of all.

Joking, teasing, mocking: knowing the difference

Persian draws clear lines between kinds of laughter, and the verbs tell you which side of the line you are on. شوخی کردن shukhi kardan and سر به سر کسی گذاشتن sar be sar-e kas-i gozāshtan are friendly: they assume you are close. دست انداختن dast andākhtan is playful but can sting. مسخره کردن maskhare kardan is unkind, and متلک انداختن matalak andākhtan – a snide remark, or a remark shouted at a stranger in the street – is plain rude.

If a tease has gone too far, repair it at once: شوخی کردم! shukhi kardam! I was only joking!, به دل نگیر be del na-gir don't take it to heart.

عمویم همیشه با بچه‌ها سر به سر می‌گذارد.

amu-yam hamishe bā bacche-hā sar be sar mi-gozārad.

My uncle is always teasing the children.

Spoken عموم همیشه با بچه‌ها سر به سر می‌ذاره. amu-m hamishe bā bacche-hā sar be sar mi-zāre.

کسی را به خاطر لهجه‌اش مسخره نکن.

kas-i rā be khāter-e lahje-ash maskhare na-kon.

Don't mock anyone for their accent.

در خیابان به او متلک انداختند و او اعتنا نکرد.

dar khiyābān be u matalak andākhtand o u e'tenā na-kard.

Someone made remarks at her in the street, and she ignored them.

حرفم را به شوخی گرفت و خندید.

harf-am rā be shukhi gereft o khandid.

He took what I said as a joke and laughed.

این موضوع شوخی‌بردار نیست.

in mowzu' shukhi-bardār nist.

This is no joking matter.

شوخی‌شوخی حرفش را زد، ولی منظورش جدی بود.

shukhi-shukhi harf-ash rā zad, vali manzur-ash jeddi bud.

He said it half in jest, but he meant it.

Puns: جناس

A Persian pun usually rests on one of two things:

  1. One word, two meanings – the same spelling and sound: شیر shir is a lion, milk and a tap; سیر sir is garlic and 'full after eating'. Rhetoric calls this jenās-e tām, the complete pun.
  2. Two words that differ by one vowel or letter – since short vowels are not written, they even look the same: کشتی kashti ship and کشتی koshti wrestling, نه na no and نه noh nine.

Poets used both constantly. A famous quatrain attributed to Khayyam ends on the two meanings of گور gur – the wild ass that King Bahram loved to hunt (he was nicknamed Bahram-e Gur), and the grave:

بهرام که گور می‌گرفتی همه عمر / دیدی که چگونه گور بهرام گرفت؟ bahrām ke gur mi-gerefti hame 'omr / didi ke chegune gur bahrām gereft? Bahram, you who caught wild asses all your life – did you see how the grave caught Bahram?

Puns are the jokes that translate worst. When an Iranian groans بی‌مزه! bi-maze! lame! at your first Persian pun, take it as a compliment.

I ate garlic and got full.

باز در را باز گذاشتی؟

bāz dar rā bāz gozāshti?

Did you leave the door open again?

ماه من، این ماه به دیدنم بیا.

māh-e man, in māh be didan-am biyā.

My moon (my beauty), come and see me this month.

کشتی‌گیر با کشتی به جزیره رفت.

koshti-gir bā kashti be jazire raft.

The wrestler went to the island by ship.

گفتم نه، نه بار گفتم نه!

goftam na, noh bār goftam na!

I said no – I said no nine times!

دیوار موش دارد، موش هم گوش دارد.

divār mush dārad, mush ham gush dārad.

Walls have mice, and mice have ears.

A rhyme rather than a pun – the Persian 'walls have ears'.

خرگوش گوش‌های بلندی دارد، ولی خر نیست!

khar-gush gush-hā-ye boland-i dārad, vali khar nist!

The hare has long ears, but it's no donkey!

خرگوش 'hare' is literally 'donkey-ear' – a children's joke built on a compound.

Telling a joke the Iranian way

When Iranians tell a joke or an anecdote aloud, they switch into the storytelling present: یک روز ملا می‌رود بازار yek ruz Mollā mi-ravad bāzār one day the Mulla goes to the market – just as English says so this guy walks into a bar. Speech is quoted with می‌گوید mi-guyad (spoken mi-ge) and no 'that'. Characters are often just طرف taraf the guy or یارو yāru this fellow – but یارو sounds rude about a real person, so keep it for jokes.

The punchline comes last and stands alone, often after a pause or a خلاصه kholāse anyway. Here is another famous Mulla story told that way – the written form first, then the way you would hear it at a dinner table.

yek ruz Mollā se kilu gusht mi-kharad o be khāne mi-āvarad.

One day the Mulla buys three kilos of meat and brings it home.

Spoken یه روز ملا سه کیلو گوشت می‌خره، میاره خونه. ye ruz Mollā se kilu gusht mi-khare, miyāre khune.

زنش گوشت را برای مهمان‌هایش کباب می‌کند و همه را می‌خورند.

zan-ash gusht rā barā-ye mehmān-hā-yash kabāb mi-konad o hame rā mi-khorand.

His wife grills the meat for her guests, and they eat the lot.

Spoken زنش گوشتو واسه مهموناش کباب می‌کنه، همه‌شو می‌خورن. zan-esh gusht-o vāse mehmun-āsh kabāb mi-kone, hame-sho mi-khoran.

شب ملا می‌پرسد: «گوشت کو؟»

shab Mollā mi-porsad: «gusht ku?»

In the evening the Mulla asks, 'Where's the meat?'

Spoken شب ملا می‌پرسه: «گوشت کو؟» shab Mollā mi-porse: «gusht ku?»

زن می‌گوید: «گربه خورد!»

zan mi-guyad: «gorbe khord!»

His wife says, 'The cat ate it!'

Spoken زنه می‌گه: «گربه خورد!» zane mi-ge: «gorbe khord!»

ملا گربه را برمی‌دارد و روی ترازو می‌گذارد: درست سه کیلو است.

Mollā gorbe rā bar-mi-dārad o ru-ye tarāzu mi-gozārad: dorost se kilu ast.

The Mulla picks up the cat and puts it on the scales: exactly three kilos.

Spoken ملا گربه رو برمی‌داره، می‌ذاره رو ترازو: درست سه کیلو! Mollā gorbe ro bar-mi-dāre, mi-zāre ru tarāzu: dorost se kilu!

می‌گوید: «اگر این گربه است، پس گوشت کو؟ اگر این گوشت است، پس گربه کو؟»

mi-guyad: «agar in gorbe ast, pas gusht ku? agar in gusht ast, pas gorbe ku?»

He says, 'If this is the cat, where's the meat? If this is the meat, where's the cat?'

Spoken می‌گه: «اگه این گربه‌ست، پس گوشت کو؟ اگه این گوشته، پس گربه کو؟» mi-ge: «age in gorbe-st, pas gusht ku? age in gushte, pas gorbe ku?»

خلاصه، از آن روز گربه‌ی ملا معروف شد.

kholāse, az ān ruz gorbe-ye Mollā ma'ruf shod.

Anyway, from that day on the Mulla's cat was famous.

Spoken خلاصه، از اون روز گربه‌ی ملا معروف شد. kholāse, az un ruz gorbe-ye Mollā ma'ruf shod.

Obeyd Zakani: the mouse and the cat

Obeyd Zakani (died about 1370) lived in Shiraz in the age of Hafez and was the sharpest satirist of classical Persian. His Mouse and Cat is a short comic epic in verse; it starts its story with the line از قضای فلک یکی گربه / بود چون اژدها به کرمانا az ghazā-ye falak yek-i gorbe / bud chon ezhdehā be Kermānā by the will of heaven there was a cat in Kerman, like a dragon. Here it is retold in modern prose.

عبید زاکانی، شاعر و طنزنویس قرن هشتم هجری، داستان موش و گربه را به شعر سروده است.

Obeyd Zākāni, shā'er o tanz-nevis-e gharn-e hashtom-e hejri, dāstān-e mush o gorbe rā be she'r sorude ast.

Obeyd Zakani, poet and satirist of the eighth century of the Hijra – the fourteenth century AD – told the story of the mouse and the cat in verse.

در کرمان گربه‌ای بود به بزرگی اژدها که همه از او می‌ترسیدند.

dar Kermān gorbe-i bud be bozorgi-ye ezhdehā ke hame az u mi-tarsidand.

In Kerman there was a cat as big as a dragon, and everyone was afraid of him.

روزی گربه به شرابخانه رفت تا موش شکار کند.

ruz-i gorbe be sharāb-khāne raft tā mush shekār konad.

One day the cat went into a wine cellar to hunt mice.

موشی از خم شراب خورده بود و مست شده بود.

mush-i az khom-e sharāb khorde bud o mast shode bud.

A mouse had drunk from a wine jar and was drunk.

موش مست فریاد می‌زد: «گربه کجاست تا سرش را بکنم؟»

mush-e mast faryād mi-zad: «gorbe kojā-st tā sar-ash rā bekanam?»

The drunken mouse was shouting, 'Where's that cat? I'll tear his head off!'

گربه از پشت خم بیرون پرید و موش را گرفت و خورد.

gorbe az posht-e khom birun parid o mush rā gereft o khord.

The cat leapt out from behind the jar, caught the mouse and ate him.

بعد به مسجد رفت، دست و رویش را شست و توبه کرد.

ba'd be masjed raft, dast o ru-yash rā shost o towbe kard.

Then he went to the mosque, washed his hands and face, and repented.

موشی که پشت منبر پنهان شده بود، این خبر را برای موش‌ها برد.

mush-i ke posht-e menbar penhān shode bud, in khabar rā barā-ye mush-hā bord.

A mouse who had been hiding behind the pulpit took the news to the other mice.

موش‌ها خوشحال شدند و هفت موش را با هدیه‌های فراوان پیش گربه فرستادند.

mush-hā khoshhāl shodand o haft mush rā bā hediye-hā-ye farāvān pish-e gorbe ferestādand.

The mice were overjoyed and sent seven mice to the cat with a mountain of presents.

هدیه‌ها شراب و کباب و کشمش و خرما و پنیر و برنج بود.

hediye-hā sharāb o kabāb o keshmesh o khormā o panir o berenj bud.

The presents were wine, kebab, raisins, dates, cheese and rice.

گربه هدیه‌ها را دید و ناگهان پنج موش را با هم گرفت.

gorbe hediye-hā rā did o nāgahān panj mush rā bā ham gereft.

The cat looked at the presents – and suddenly seized five of the mice at once.

دو موش فرار کردند و خبر را به پادشاه موش‌ها رساندند.

do mush farār kardand o khabar rā be pādshāh-e mush-hā resāndand.

Two mice escaped and took the news to the king of the mice.

پادشاه لشکری بزرگ جمع کرد و گربه در جنگ اسیر شد.

pādshāh lashkar-i bozorg jam' kard o gorbe dar jang asir shod.

The king gathered a great army, and in the battle the cat was taken prisoner.

اما وقتی می‌خواستند او را به دار بزنند، بندها را پاره کرد و لشکر موش‌ها را پراکند.

ammā vaghti mi-khāstand u rā be dār bezanand, band-hā rā pāre kard o lashkar-e mush-hā rā parākand.

But just as they were about to hang him, he broke his bonds and scattered the army of mice.

قرن‌هاست که خوانندگان در این گربه‌ی ریاکار چهره‌ی حاکمان دروغگو را می‌بینند.

gharn-hā-st ke khānandegān dar in gorbe-ye riyākār chehre-ye hākemān-e dorughgu rā mi-binand.

For centuries readers have seen in this hypocritical cat the face of lying rulers.

به قول مردم: «توبه‌ی گرگ مرگ است.»

be ghowl-e mardom: «towbe-ye gorg marg ast.»

As people say: 'A wolf's repentance is death' – it only ends when the wolf does.

From Obeyd to the thirteenth of Farvardin

Obeyd Zakani wrote in fourteenth-century Shiraz, in the same streets as Hafez, under rulers who alternated between pleasure and harsh piety. Besides Mouse and Cat he wrote mock treatises: The Ethics of the Aristocrats, which 'proves' that the old virtues are out of date, and a satirical dictionary of Definitions in which every respectable title is given a wicked meaning. Many readers see in his pious, murderous cat a portrait of a ruler of his own day; the poem has been read since as a satire on any power that wraps itself in religion.

Mulla Nasreddin belongs to everyone. Turks call him Nasreddin Hoca, Central Asians often Afandi, and Arabs tell similar stories of Juha. In Iran his stories are children's books, proverbs and television sketches, and you will hear Iranians quote him without telling the whole story: دیگ زایید dig zāyid, 'the pot had a baby', is enough.

The lie of the thirteenth. On سیزده‌بدر sizdah-bedar, the thirteenth day of the new year, families picnic outdoors and play tricks on each other. A prank on that day is a دروغ سیزده dorugh-e sizdah the lie of the thirteenth – Iran's April Fool, which some say is older than the European one.

Why jokes don't translate. A Persian joke may rest on a pun (شیر، سیر), on an unwritten vowel (کشتی), on the ritual politeness of ta'ārof, on a line of Hafez everybody knows, or on the switch between bookish and street Persian. Explaining it kills it. The surest sign that your Persian has arrived is the day you laugh before the explanation.

Tips

  • Laugh at the Mulla, not at people. Jokes about a town, a people or an accent may get a laugh in some company, but they cost friends – leave them alone.
  • A tease needs a safety net. If your سر به سر گذاشتن lands badly, add at once: شوخی کردم، به دل نگیر shukhi kardam, be del na-gir I was joking, don't take it to heart.
  • Learn a few Mulla stories by heart and tell them in the storytelling present with spoken forms: ye ruz Mollā mi-re… It is one of the best fluency exercises there is.
  • Collect homonyms as you meet them – شیر، سیر، باز، ماه، مهر، شانه. Each one is a pun waiting for you.
  • In writing, satire is طنز tanz; a personal attack in verse is هجو hajv. Iranian critics are proud of the first and wary of the second.
  • If you don't get a joke, say so with a smile: نگرفتم! na-gereftam! I didn't get it! – گرفتن 'to get' works exactly as in English.
