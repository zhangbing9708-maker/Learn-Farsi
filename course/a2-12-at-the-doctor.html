در مطب دکترAt the doctor's: symptoms, advice and the pharmacy

dar matab-e doktor

In A1 you learned to say what hurts and to buy something at the pharmacy. Now you go further: you make an appointment, describe your symptoms to a doctor, understand the advice you are given – باید, نباید and the gentler بهتر است – and follow the instructions on a box of tablets or a bottle of syrup. You also learn what Iranians say to someone who is ill.

In this lesson you will learn to

  • make an appointment and describe your symptoms
  • say how long you have been ill with از … and … است که
  • understand and give advice with باید، نباید and بهتر است
  • follow the instructions for tablets, syrup and injections
  • say the right thing to someone who is ill

Three things a doctor's visit adds

In A1 you said what hurts: سرم درد می‌کند sar-am dard mi-konad my head hurts. A visit to the doctor adds three things.

How long? Persian uses the present with از az since where English uses have been: از سه روز پیش مریضم az se ruz-e pish mariz-am I've been ill for three days – literally I am ill since three days ago. A second pattern puts the time first: سه روز است که سرفه می‌کنم se ruz ast ke sorfe mi-konam I've been coughing for three days – it is three days that I cough.

Eating your medicine. You catch a cold by eating it – سرما خوردن sarmā khordan to catch a cold, literally to eat cold – and you eat the cure too: قرص بخورید ghors bokhorid take the tablets, شربت بخورید sharbat bokhorid take the syrup.

Advice. Doctors use باید bāyad must, should and the softer بهتر است behtar ast it's better, you'd better, both followed by the subjunctive: بهتر است به کلاس نروید behtar ast be kelās na-ravid you'd better not go to class.

Saying what's wrong – and for how long

Symptoms come in three patterns. Some you have: تب دارم tab dāram I have a temperature, سرفه دارم sorfe dāram I have a cough. Some are a body part that does pain, with the possessive ending: سرم درد می‌کند sar-am dard mi-konad my head hurts. And some are compound verbs: سرفه می‌کنم sorfe mi-konam I'm coughing, سرما خورده‌ام sarmā khorde-am I've caught a cold – the present perfect from lesson 2, because the cold began in the past and is still with you.

For how long, Persian keeps the present tense: از دیروز تب دارم az diruz tab dāram I've had a temperature since yesterday. Or turn it round with است که ast ke: دو روز است که سرفه می‌کنم do ruz ast ke sorfe mi-konam I've been coughing for two days. The doctor asks از کی؟ az key? since when? or چند وقت است؟ chand vaght ast? how long has it been?

Advice: باید، نباید and بهتر است

باید bāyad must, should never changes; the verb after it is in the subjunctive and carries the person: باید استراحت کنید bāyad esterāhat konid you should rest, باید بخوابم bāyad bekhābam I must sleep. Its negative نباید na-bāyad means must not: نباید سیگار بکشید na-bāyad sigār bekeshid you mustn't smoke.

Doctors, parents and friends often prefer the gentler بهتر است behtar ast it's better – again with the subjunctive. To advise someone not to do something, make the subjunctive negative: بهتر است بیرون نروید behtar ast birun na-ravid you'd better not go out. In speech it shrinks to بهتره behtare.

There's no need is لازم نیست lāzem nist + subjunctive – useful for calming a nervous patient.

At the pharmacy: how much and how often

Medicine comes as قرص ghors tablets, کپسول kapsul capsules, شربت sharbat syrup, قطره ghatre drops, پماد pomād ointment or آمپول āmpul an injection – and you eat most of them: قرص بخورید ghors bokhorid take the tablets. Ointment and injections are struck instead: پماد بزنید pomād bezanid put on the ointment, آمپول بزنید āmpul bezanid have the injection.

The instructions use one neat trick: the indefinite ـی -i on a time word means per: روزی سه بار ruz-i se bār three times a day, هفته‌ای یک بار hafte-i yek bār once a week. هر har every works too: هر هشت ساعت har hasht sā'at every eight hours.

Doctors, pharmacies and a cure called nabat

Many Iranian doctors lead double lives: in the morning they work in a hospital, and in the late afternoon and evening they see patients in their own مطب matab surgery. You book a نوبت nowbat appointment by phone or online, give your name to the منشی monshi receptionist when you arrive – and then wait, often for a long time. Doctors are addressed as خانم دکتر khānom doktor or آقای دکتر āghā-ye doktor, and the title sticks: friends call a doctor doktor at parties too.

Pharmacies are everywhere, and there is always a داروخانه‌ی شبانه‌روزی dāru-khāne-ye shabāne-ruzi 24-hour pharmacy not far away. Many have a small تزریقات tazrighāt injection room at the back, because injections have long been popular in Iran. Iran's Pharmacists' Day falls on the birthday of the great physician رازی Rāzi (Rhazes), who worked in Rey, just south of today's Tehran, more than a thousand years ago.

At home, the first remedies are older. For a cold there is boiled turnip, شلغم shalgham, sold steaming from carts on winter evenings; for an upset stomach or a shock, hot water with نبات nabāt, golden rock sugar flavoured with saffron. And when someone tells you they are ill, say بلا دور باشد balā dur bāshad may harm stay far away – or the everyday wish زودتر خوب شوید zud-tar khub shavid get well soon.

Tips

  • You eat your medicine. Tablets, syrup, water and tea all take خوردن: قرص بخورید، آب بخورید. نوشیدن (to drink) is bookish.
  • Since and for: Persian says I am ill since three days ago – از سه روز پیش مریضم – with the present tense, not the present perfect.
  • بهتر است is the polite way to give advice to anyone – a patient, a guest, your boss. باید said to a stranger can sound bossy.
  • Address a doctor as خانم دکتر or آقای دکتر, with or without the surname – the same goes for anyone with a doctorate.
  • When someone says they're ill: بلا دور باشد! or ان‌شاءالله زود خوب می‌شوید (God willing, you'll soon be better). In speech بهتر است is بهتره and لازم است is لازمه.
