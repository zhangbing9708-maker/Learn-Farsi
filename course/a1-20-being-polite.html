تعارف نکنید!Being polite: ta'arof, invitations and phone calls

ta'ārof na-konid!

Iranians are famous for their courtesy, and it comes with a whole set of phrases: what you say when you arrive, when you are offered tea, when someone has cooked for you, when someone is working, when you leave. Behind them lies تعارف – ta'arof, the elaborate politeness of offering and refusing. In this last A1 lesson you learn the formulas, how to accept an invitation, and how to get through a simple phone call.

In this lesson you will learn to

  • use the everyday formulas: بفرمایید، خواهش می‌کنم، ببخشید، چشم
  • thank a host and answer thanks: دست شما درد نکند، نوش جان، قابل ندارد
  • greet someone at work with خسته نباشید
  • understand ta'arof: when 'no' means 'ask me again'
  • invite someone, accept an invitation and make a simple phone call

Tom

خواهش می‌کنم. باز هم به ما سر بزنید.

Spoken خواهش می‌کنم. باز هم به ما سر بزنین. khāhesh mi-konam. bāz ham be mā sar bezanin.

Tom

Of course. Thank you very much, goodbye!

Ta'arof in one minute

Look at what happened in that dialogue. Tom brought flowers and Mr Karimi said چرا زحمت کشیدی؟ cherā zahmat keshidi? why did you go to the trouble? – not a complaint, just good manners. Tom answered with the classic قابل شما را ندارد ghābel-e shomā rā na-dārad it is not worthy of you. Mrs Karimi offered tea, Tom said no once, she insisted – تعارف نکنید! ta'ārof na-konid! don't stand on ceremony! – and then he said yes.

This polite dance of offering, refusing and insisting is تعارف ta'ārof. It is part of every visit, every meal and even every taxi ride. You don't need to master it now, but you do need the formulas: Iranians use them dozens of times a day, and they are delighted when a foreigner uses them too.

Almost all of them are wishes or images: may your hand not hurt, may you not be tired, sweet to your soul. Learn them as whole phrases, like bless you in English.

The everyday formulas – and their answers

Each formula has its moment, and many have a fixed answer. Don't translate them word for word: دست شما درد نکند dast-e shomā dard na-konad is simply thank you for something someone has made or done for you, and خسته نباشید khaste na-bāshid is what you say to a shopkeeper, a teacher at the end of class or a friend who has just finished a job.

The formulas with a verb use the be- / na- forms you know from the imperative and from باید: نکند na-konad may it not do, نباشید na-bāshid may you not be. To a friend, change shomā to to and the endings to the singular: دستت درد نکند dast-at dard na-konad, خسته نباشی khaste na-bāshi.

The ta'arof dance: offering, refusing, insisting

When you are offered something – tea, fruit, a second helping, the front seat – the polite pattern is: refuse once, let the host insist, then accept. Hosts expect to offer at least twice, so a first no is never the end of the matter.

Most of the phrases are polite imperatives, and the refusals are negative imperatives with na- (lesson 16): زحمت نکشید zahmat na-keshid don't trouble yourself, تعارف نکنید ta'ārof na-konid don't stand on ceremony. A very polite word for eat or have is میل کردن meyl kardan: میل کنید meyl konid please have some.

If you really mean no, say it with a smile and a reason: نه، واقعاً ممنون. سیر شدم. na, vāghe'an mamnun. sir shodam. No, really, thank you. I'm full.

On the phone, and invitations

Iranians answer the phone with الو؟ alo? – or, at work, with بفرمایید. befarmāyid. Two things are new. First, when you ask for a respected person, Persian uses the plural verb out of respect, even for one person: آقای کریمی هستند؟ āghā-ye Karimi hastand? Is Mr Karimi there? Second, hold the line is an image: گوشی دستتان باشد gushi dast-etān bāshad, literally let the receiver stay in your hand.

To invite someone, the simplest way is مهمان ما باشید mehmān-e mā bāshid be our guest(s) – باشید bāshid is the be- form of to be. Accept with با کمال میل bā kamāl-e meyl with the greatest pleasure or حتماً hatman definitely. And on the phone, goodbye comes in a long string: قربان شما، خدا حافظ! ghorbān-e shomā, khodā hāfez!

Ta'arof: politeness as an art

تعارف ta'ārof is the Iranian art of putting the other person first. At a door, two people may stand for a while saying بفرمایید befarmāyid after you – نه، شما بفرمایید na, shomā befarmāyid no, after you. A host keeps filling your plate and your tea glass; a guest praises the food and leaves a little on the plate to show there was more than enough. A shopkeeper or a taxi driver who says قابل ندارد ghābel na-dārad is being gracious, not generous – you insist, and you pay.

The language is full of exaggerated humility: قربان شما ghorbān-e shomā I am your sacrifice means thanks, bye, and چاکرم chāker-am I'm your servant is a cheerful thanks among Tehran men. It isn't insincere; it is a way of showing respect, احترام ehterām, and of keeping social life smooth.

Foreigners are forgiven almost everything. A safe rule: refuse an offer once, accept the second time, thank the cook with dast-etān dard na-konad, and when you leave, say bebakhshid, zahmat dādim.

Tips

  • befarmāyid is the Swiss army knife of Persian politeness: come in, sit down, help yourself, here you are, after you, go ahead, I'm listening. The situation tells you which.
  • A first 'no' is often just politeness. If you are the host, offer again; if you are the guest and really want something, accept the second offer.
  • chashm (eye) is the polite yes to a request – from children to parents, from staff to customers. The kind answer is chashm-at bi-balā, 'may your eye be safe from harm'.
  • Spoken and friendly forms: dast-et dard nakone (to a friend), dast-etun dard nakone (polite); khaste nabāshi / khaste nabāshin.
  • In shops and taxis ghābel na-dārad never means it's free. Say khāhesh mi-konam, befarmāyid and hand over the money.
