فارسی بلدید؟Do you speak Persian? Countries and languages

fārsi balad-id?

'You speak Persian!' is something you will hear again and again in Iran – usually with delight. In this lesson you learn to say which languages you speak, where you live and where people come from, and you meet the most important pattern in Persian grammar: the present tense, mi- + stem + ending.

In this lesson you will learn to

  • say which languages you speak, and ask others
  • say where you live: dar Tehrān zendegi mi-konam
  • make the present tense with mi- + stem + ending, and the negative with nemi-
  • use balad-am 'I know (how to)'
  • name countries, nationalities and languages

In a taxi

Tom takes a taxi home from the university. The driver, Mr Rahimi, is curious about his passenger.

Mr Rahimi

ببخشید، شما اهل کجا هستید؟

bebakhshid, shomā ahl-e kojā hastid?

Excuse me, where are you from?

Spoken ببخشید، شما اهل کجایید؟ bebakhshid, shomā ahl-e kojā-yid?

Tom

من اهل استرالیا هستم.

man ahl-e Ostorāliyā hastam.

I'm from Australia.

Mr Rahimi

به‌به! شما خیلی خوب فارسی صحبت می‌کنید!

bah-bah! shomā kheyli khub fārsi sohbat mi-konid!

Wow! You speak Persian very well!

Spoken به‌به! شما خیلی خوب فارسی حرف می‌زنین! bah-bah! shomā kheyli khub fārsi harf mi-zanin!

Tom

نه، فقط کمی بلدم. در تهران فارسی یاد می‌گیرم.

na, faghat kami balad-am. dar Tehrān fārsi yād mi-giram.

No, I only know a little. I'm learning Persian in Tehran.

Spoken نه، فقط یه کم بلدم. تو تهران فارسی یاد می‌گیرم. na, faghat ye kam balad-am. tu Tehrān fārsi yād mi-giram.

Mr Rahimi

در استرالیا مردم چه زبانی صحبت می‌کنند؟

dar Ostorāliyā mardom che zabān-i sohbat mi-konand?

What language do people speak in Australia?

Tom

انگلیسی. من انگلیسی و کمی فرانسوی صحبت می‌کنم.

engelisi. man engelisi o kami farānsavi sohbat mi-konam.

English. I speak English and a little French.

Mr Rahimi

من هم کمی انگلیسی بلدم! برادرم در کانادا زندگی می‌کند.

man ham kami engelisi balad-am! barādar-am dar Kānādā zendegi mi-konad.

I know a little English too! My brother lives in Canada.

Spoken من هم یه کم انگلیسی بلدم! داداشم کانادا زندگی می‌کنه. man ham ye kam engelisi balad-am! dādāsh-am Kānādā zendegi mi-kone.

Tom

شما اهل تهران هستید؟

shomā ahl-e Tehrān hastid?

Are you from Tehran?

Mr Rahimi

نه، من اهل تبریز هستم. زبان مادری من ترکی است.

na, man ahl-e Tabriz hastam. zabān-e mādari-ye man torki ast.

No, I'm from Tabriz. My mother tongue is Turkish (Azerbaijani).

Tom

پس شما دو زبان صحبت می‌کنید، ترکی و فارسی!

pas shomā do zabān sohbat mi-konid, torki o fārsi!

So you speak two languages, Turkish and Persian!

Mr Rahimi

بله! و شما کجا زندگی می‌کنید؟

bale! va shomā kojā zendegi mi-konid?

Yes! And where do you live?

Spoken آره! شما کجا زندگی می‌کنین؟ āre! shomā kojā zendegi mi-konin?

Tom

نزدیک دانشگاه زندگی می‌کنم. همین‌جا، لطفاً!

nazdik-e dāneshgāh zendegi mi-konam. hamin-jā, lotfan!

I live near the university. Right here, please!

Mr Rahimi

بفرمایید. به ایران خوش آمدید!

befarmāyid. be Irān khosh āmadid!

Here we are. Welcome to Iran!

Speaking a language: balad-am and sohbat mi-konam

Persian has two everyday ways to say that you speak a language. The first is بلد balad knowing how with the endings of to be: فارسی بلدم fārsi balad-am I know Persian, انگلیسی بلدید؟ engelisi balad-id? do you speak English? The second is a real verb, صحبت کردن sohbat kardan to speak, to talk: فارسی صحبت می‌کنم fārsi sohbat mi-konam I speak Persian. In the street you will mostly hear حرف زدن harf zadan to talk: fārsi harf mi-zanam.

Languages are named like nationalities, with -i: فارسی fārsi, انگلیسی engelisi, عربی arabi. The word for language, زبان zabān, also means tongue – so your mother tongue is زبان مادری zabān-e mādari, just as in English.

And be ready: say three words of Persian and someone will answer به‌به! چه خوب فارسی صحبت می‌کنید! bah-bah! che khub fārsi sohbat mi-konid! wow, how well you speak Persian!

The present tense: mi- + stem + ending

Almost every Persian verb makes its present tense the same way: mi- + the present stem + a personal ending. The endings are the ones you know from to be and dāshtan: -am, -i, -ad, -im, -id, -and.

The present stem has to be learned with each verb, like the forms of an English irregular verb: کردن kardan to do → kon-, زدن zadan to hit → zan-, گرفتن gereftan to take → gir-, دانستن dānestan to know → dān-. So می‌کنم mi-konam I do, می‌زنم mi-zanam I hit, می‌گیرم mi-giram I take, می‌دانم mi-dānam I know.

Many everyday verbs are compounds: a noun plus a verb such as kardan. Only the verb part changes: زندگی می‌کنم zendegi mi-konam I live (literally I do life), صحبت می‌کنید sohbat mi-konid you speak, فارسی یاد می‌گیرم fārsi yād mi-giram I am learning Persian.

One present covers both I live and I am living. In writing, mi- is always joined to the verb with a ZWNJ: می‌کنم.

Saying 'not': nemi- and balad nistam

To make a present-tense verb negative, mi- becomes nemi-: صحبت می‌کنم sohbat mi-konam I speak → صحبت نمی‌کنم sohbat nemi-konam I don't speak. The stress moves to the front: NE-mi-konam. A word you will use every day is نمی‌دانم nemi-dānam I don't know – spoken nemi-dunam.

بلد balad is not a verb but a describing word like khub, so it takes the endings of to be and needs no mi-: بلدم balad-am I know (how), بلد است balad ast he / she knows. Its negative is simply the negative of to be: بلد نیستم balad nistam I don't know (how).

Where? Which language? Countries and peoples

کجا kojā where stands where the answer will go: شما کجا زندگی می‌کنید؟ shomā kojā zendegi mi-konid? where do you live? → من در تهران زندگی می‌کنم man dar Tehrān zendegi mi-konam I live in Tehran. With a city or a country, in is در dar – in speech often تو tu, or nothing at all: Tehrān zendegi mi-konam.

For which or what kind of, put چه che before a noun with the -i ending: چه زبانی؟ che zabān-i? what language?, چه کشوری؟ che keshvar-i? which country? For more than one, add -hā-yi: چه زبان‌هایی بلدید؟ che zabān-hā-yi balad-id? what languages do you know?

As you saw in lesson 2, the nationality is the country + -i, and the language is usually the same word. A few peoples have a short name of their own – tork, tājik, rus – but their language still ends in -i.

One language, three countries – and many languages in one

Persian is not only the language of Iran. In Afghanistan it is called دری dari and is one of the country's two official languages; in Tajikistan it is تاجیکی tājiki (Tajik) and is written in the Cyrillic alphabet. An Iranian, an Afghan and a Tajik can talk to each other with a little effort – rather like an American, a Scot and an Australian. Altogether well over a hundred million people speak Persian, and its great poets – Rumi, Hafez, Saadi – belong to all of them.

Iran, for its part, is home to many languages. In the north-west, around Tabriz, millions grow up speaking Azerbaijani Turkish, like Mr Rahimi the taxi driver. There are also Kurdish, Luri, Gilaki and Mazandarani, Arabic in the south-west, Baluchi in the south-east, Turkmen in the north-east, and Armenian and Assyrian communities in the cities. Persian is the language everyone shares – at school, at work and on television – so the question زبان مادری شما چیست؟ zabān-e mādari-ye shomā chist? what is your mother tongue? can have a surprising answer.

Tips

  • The mi- of the present is always joined to the verb with a ZWNJ: می‌کنم – never میکنم or می کنم.
  • In compound verbs only the verb part changes: zendegi mi-konam, zendegi mi-koni, zendegi mi-konad… The first word stays as it is.
  • Spoken Tehrani shortens the endings: mi-konad → mi-kone, mi-konid → mi-konin, mi-konand → mi-konan, and mi-dānam → mi-dunam.
  • Stress the mi-: MI-konam. In the negative, stress the ne-: NE-mi-konam.
  • When someone praises your Persian, the polite answer is a modest one: na, faghat kami balad-am – 'no, I only know a little'.
