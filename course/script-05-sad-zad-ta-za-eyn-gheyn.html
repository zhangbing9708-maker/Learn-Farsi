ص، ض، ط، ظ، ع، غSād, zād, tā, zā, eyn, gheyn – the Arabic letters

sād, zād, tā, zā, eyn, gheyn

Six letters that came into Persian with Arabic words. Four of them bring no new sound at all – sād sounds like sin, zād and zā like ze, tā like te – so all you learn is which words use them. The other two are eyn, a small catch in the throat, and gheyn, a gargled gh. With them you can read clock, food, poem and garden.

In this lesson you will learn to

  • read and write ص ض ط ظ and know which everyday letter each one sounds like
  • read and write ع غ, the letters that change shape most
  • pronounce ع as a glottal catch and غ as gh
  • read words such as sedā, sā'at, ghazā and bāgh

Why Persian keeps Arabic letters

After the Arab conquest of Iran in the 7th century, Persian came to be written in the Arabic alphabet, and it borrowed thousands of Arabic words – a large share of its vocabulary today. The words kept their Arabic spelling, but Persian speakers never took over some of the Arabic sounds behind the letters. Arabic has 'heavy' versions of s, z and t, said with the back of the tongue raised; in Persian they became the ordinary s, z and t.

So one sound can now be written in several ways:

  • s: س sin (everyday), ث se, ص sād
  • z: ز ze (everyday), ذ zāl, ض zād, ظ zā
  • t: ت te (everyday), ط tā

It is like f and ph in English: the spelling tells you where a word comes from, not how it sounds. A word with ص ض ط or ظ is almost always from Arabic. You learn the spelling together with the word – and Iranian schoolchildren practise exactly this for years, in dictation tests called دیکته dikte.

Sād and zād, tā and zā

ص sād is a long flat loop followed by a small tooth. Alone or at the end of a word it finishes in a wide bowl, like sin: ص, ـص. At the start or in the middle the bowl drops away: صـ, ـصـ. ض zād is the same shape with one dot above.

ط tā is a flat loop with a tall upright stroke, like a flag on a pole, and ظ zā adds one dot. These two hardly change at all: ط, طـ, ـطـ, ـط.

All four join on both sides, and they sound like the everyday letters: ص is s, ض and ظ are z, ط is t. So صدا sedā sound, voice sounds exactly as if it were written with sin, رضا Rezā Reza as if it had ze, and خط khatt line; handwriting as if it ended in te.

Eyn and gheyn – the shape-shifters

ع eyn changes shape more than any other letter. Alone it is a small open hook on top of a big curving tail: ع. At the start of a word only the open hook is left: عـ. In the middle it closes into a small filled triangle: ـعـ. At the end it is that triangle with the tail: ـع. غ gheyn is exactly the same with one dot above: غ, غـ, ـغـ, ـغ.

Eyn has no sound of its own that you can clearly hear. At the start of a word it simply carries the vowel, just like alef: عدد 'adad number begins with a plain a. Between vowels it is a small catch in the throat – the break in uh-oh: ساعت sā'at hour; clock is sā – at. After a consonant the catch is often dropped and the vowel before it is simply held longer: in شعر she'r poem the e is drawn out a little. This course writes eyn with an apostrophe: '.

Gheyn is gh, a gargled sound at the back of the mouth, close to the French r in Paris. Say kh from lesson 3, then switch your voice on: غذا ghazā food, باغ bāgh garden. In the next lesson you will meet ghāf, a second letter that most Iranians say exactly the same way.

Reading tips

  • Learn these spellings word by word. Hearing s tells you nothing about whether it is written س, ث or ص.
  • A word with ص ض ط ظ is almost always from Arabic – but not always: صد sad hundred and شصت shast sixty are good old Persian words written with sād.
  • Inside a word, a small filled triangle is ع – or غ, if it has a dot. Read it as a catch, or as gh.
  • For gh, start from kh and add your voice. Don't let it harden into a g.
  • Don't worry about how Arabic pronounces these letters. In Persian ص is simply s, ط is t, and ض ظ are z.
