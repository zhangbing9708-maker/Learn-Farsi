نخستین گام‌ها در شعرFirst steps in poetry: reading a classical poem

nakhostin gām-hā dar she'r

Iranians quote poetry the way other people quote proverbs, and almost all of it is classical. In this lesson you learn how a classical poem is put together – the بیت and its two halves, the rhyme and the refrain, the poet's pen name – how to hear the rhythm by counting long and short syllables, and the old short forms that poets use. Then you read Saadi's بنی‌آدم line by line and find out how Iranians ask Hafez about their future on Yalda night.

In this lesson you will learn to

  • name the parts of a poem: بیت، مصراع، قافیه، ردیف، تخلص
  • tell a غزل from a رباعی، a مثنوی and a قصیده
  • hear the metre by counting long and short syllables
  • recognise the poetic forms ز، چو، همی، -ا، دگر، کز and give their modern equivalents
  • read Saadi's بنی‌آدم line by line and explain fāl-e Hāfez

Children of Adam

Saadi of Shiraz finished his Golestan ('The Rose Garden') in 1258. Here is the story behind its most famous lines, and the three bayts themselves, each followed by a plain modern version that begins with یعنی 'that is'. Watch for the short forms ز، چو، دگر and کز.

سعدی، شاعر بزرگ شیراز، حدود هشتصد سال پیش زندگی می‌کرد.

Sa'di, shā'er-e bozorg-e Shirāz, hodud-e hashtsad sāl pish zendegi mi-kard.

Saadi, the great poet of Shiraz, lived about eight hundred years ago.

در کتاب گلستان، داستانی هست که در مسجد بزرگ دمشق اتفاق می‌افتد.

dar ketāb-e Golestān, dāstān-i hast ke dar masjed-e bozorg-e Dameshgh ettefāgh mi-oftad.

In his book the Golestan there is a story that takes place in the Great Mosque of Damascus.

سعدی کنار آرامگاه حضرت یحیی نشسته بود که پادشاهی ستمگر نزد او آمد.

Sa'di kenār-e ārāmgāh-e hazrat-e Yahyā neshaste bud ke pādeshāh-i setamgar nazd-e u āmad.

Saadi was sitting beside the tomb of the prophet John (Yahya) when an unjust king came to him.

پادشاه از سعدی خواست که برایش دعا کند.

pādeshāh az Sa'di khāst ke barā-yash do'ā konad.

The king asked Saadi to pray for him.

سعدی به او گفت که به مردم ضعیف رحم کند و این سه بیت را خواند.

Sa'di be u goft ke be mardom-e za'if rahm konad va in se beyt rā khānd.

Saadi told him to have mercy on the weak, and recited these three bayts.

بنی‌آدم اعضای یکدیگرند / که در آفرینش ز یک گوهرند

bani-ādam a'zā-ye yekdigar-and / ke dar āfarinesh ze yek gowhar-and

The children of Adam are limbs of one another, / for in creation they are made of one essence.

ز ze = از az; یکدیگرند yekdigar-and = یکدیگر هستند yekdigar hastand. Many people quote the first half as «بنی‌آدم اعضای یک پیکرند» – 'limbs of one body'.

یعنی همه‌ی انسان‌ها اعضای یک بدن هستند، چون از یک اصل آفریده شده‌اند.

ya'ni hame-ye ensān-hā a'zā-ye yek badan hastand, chon az yek asl āfaride shode-and.

In other words, all human beings are limbs of one body, because they were created from one origin.

چو عضوی به درد آورد روزگار / دگر عضوها را نماند قرار

cho 'ozvi be dard āvarad ruzgār / degar 'ozv-hā rā namānad gharār

When the world brings pain to one limb, / the other limbs can find no rest.

چو cho = وقتی که vaghti ke; دگر degar = دیگر digar; عضوها را 'ozv-hā rā = 'for the limbs'. The verbs آورد āvarad and نماند namānad have no می: this is always true.

یعنی وقتی یک عضو بدن درد می‌کشد، عضوهای دیگر هم آرام نمی‌گیرند.

ya'ni vaghti yek 'ozv-e badan dard mi-keshad, 'ozv-hā-ye digar ham ārām nemi-girand.

In other words, when one limb of the body hurts, the other limbs cannot rest either.

تو کز محنت دیگران بی‌غمی / نشاید که نامت نهند آدمی

to kaz mehnat-e digarān bi-ghami / nashāyad ke nām-at nahand ādami

You who feel no grief at the suffering of others / do not deserve to be called human.

Literally: you who from the suffering of others are without-sorrow, it is not fitting that they put your name 'human'

کز kaz = که از ke az; نشاید nashāyad = 'it is not fitting' (from the old verb شایستن); نهند nahand = 'they put' (نهادن = گذاشتن).

یعنی اگر از رنج دیگران غمگین نمی‌شوی، شایسته نیست که تو را انسان بنامند.

ya'ni agar az ranj-e digarān ghamgin nemi-shavi, shāyeste nist ke to rā ensān benāmand.

In other words, if the suffering of others does not sadden you, you do not deserve to be called human.

امروز بچه‌های ایرانی این شعر را در مدرسه از بر می‌کنند.

emruz bacche-hā-ye irāni in she'r rā dar madrese az bar mi-konand.

Today Iranian children learn this poem by heart at school.

بعد از هر زلزله یا سیل، مردم بیت اول آن را در پیام‌هایشان می‌نویسند.

ba'd az har zelzele yā seyl, mardom beyt-e avval-e ān rā dar payām-hā-yeshān mi-nevisand.

After every earthquake or flood, people write its first bayt in their messages.

این شعر روی فرشی در ساختمان سازمان ملل در نیویورک هم بافته شده است.

in she'r ru-ye farsh-i dar sākhtemān-e sāzmān-e melal dar Niyuyork ham bāfte shode ast.

This poem has even been woven into a carpet in the United Nations building in New York.

How to read a bayt

Iranians quote poetry the way English speakers quote proverbs – at weddings and funerals, in arguments, in text messages – and almost all of it is classical, written between the 10th and the 15th centuries. Persian has changed so little since then that a schoolchild in Tehran can read Saadi. What makes old poems hard is not so much the words as the way they are put together.

A classical poem is a chain of بیت beyt bayts: lines in two halves. Each half is a مصراع mesrā'. Books print the two halves side by side – read the right-hand half first, then the left – and in this course they are separated by / .

Four things will surprise you after modern prose:

  1. Free word order. To fit the rhythm, a verb can come before its subject: چو عضوی به درد آورد روزگار cho 'ozvi be dard āvarad ruzgār is literally when a limb to pain brings – the world.
  2. Short forms. ز ze for از az, چو cho for چون chon, دگر degar for دیگر digar: a syllable shorter, to fit the metre.
  3. No می. The bare present آورد āvarad means brings – always brings: a general truth.
  4. Arabic. Poets mixed in Arabic words and sometimes whole Arabic half-lines; Hafez's very first line begins in Arabic.

The good news: the world of the poems is small. The same cupbearer, wine, garden, nightingale and cruel beloved come back again and again, so every poem you read makes the next one easier.

The anatomy of a poem: bayt, rhyme, refrain – and the four forms

Every classical poem is built from the same parts.

The rhyme, قافیه ghāfiye, is the sound that comes back at the end of the lines. After it there may be a refrain, ردیف radif: one or more words repeated, unchanged, at the end of every rhyming line. A rhyme is compulsory; a radif is a choice – and the great poets love it, because it makes every bayt land on the same words.

  • In Hafez's first ghazal the rhyme is -el and the radif is only ها -hā: nāvel-hā, moshkel-hā, del-hā, manzel-hā, sāhel-hā, mahfel-hā…
  • In his ghazal of Joseph the radif is a whole phrase, غم مخور gham ma-khor do not grieve, and the rhyme is the word just before it: Kan'ān, golestān…
  • Saadi's بنی‌آدم has rhymes but no radif – and each of its bayts rhymes on its own, like a small masnavi.

In a ghazal both halves of the first bayt rhyme; after that only the second half of each bayt does: aa ba ca da… The last bayt usually names the poet by his pen name, the تخلص takhallos: Hafez and Saadi often speak to themselves in their last lines. Ho, cupbearer! Pass round the cup and hand it on – / for love seemed easy at first, but then came the troubles.

Literally: ho, O cupbearer, turn a cup and hand it – for love showed easy at first, but difficulties fell

The first bayt of the Divan of Hafez – a ghazal, so both halves rhyme. The first half is Arabic. Rhyme: ناول nāvel / مشکل moshkel (-el); radif: ها -hā.

حضوری گر همی‌خواهی از او غایب مشو حافظ

hozur-i gar hami-khāhi az u ghāyeb ma-show Hāfez

If you want to be in His presence, do not be absent from Him, Hafez.

The first half of the last bayt of the same ghazal: the poet speaks to himself by his takhallos, حافظ Hāfez.

یوسف گم‌گشته باز آید به کنعان غم مخور / کلبه‌ی احزان شود روزی گلستان غم مخور

Yusef-e gom-gashte bāz āyad be Kan'ān gham ma-khor / kolbe-ye ahzān shavad ruz-i golestān gham ma-khor

Lost Joseph will come back to Canaan – do not grieve; / the hut of sorrows will one day be a rose garden – do not grieve.

Hafez again. Radif: غم مخور gham ma-khor; rhyme: کنعان Kan'ān / گلستان golestān. The 'hut of sorrows' is where Jacob mourned his lost son Joseph until he came home.

این قافله‌ی عمر عجب می‌گذرد / دریاب دمی که با طرب می‌گذرد

in ghāfele-ye 'omr 'ajab mi-gozarad / daryāb dam-i ke bā tarab mi-gozarad

How strangely this caravan of life goes by! / Seize the moment that passes in joy.

A robā'i attributed to Khayyam, first bayt. Rhyme: عجب 'ajab / طرب tarab; radif: می‌گذرد mi-gozarad.

ساقی غم فردای حریفان چه خوری / پیش آر پیاله را که شب می‌گذرد

sāghi gham-e fardā-ye harifān che khori / pish ār piyāle rā ke shab mi-gozarad

Cupbearer, why grieve over your companions' tomorrow? / Bring the cup, for the night is passing.

The second bayt: the third line does not rhyme, the fourth comes back to شب shab + می‌گذرد mi-gozarad – the pattern aa ba.

بشنو این نی چون شکایت می‌کند / از جدایی‌ها حکایت می‌کند

beshnow in ney chon shekāyat mi-konad / az jodāyi-hā hekāyat mi-konad

Listen to this reed, how it complains, / telling the tale of separations.

The first bayt of Rumi's Masnavi, which runs to over 25,000 bayts, each rhyming on its own: here شکایت shekāyat / حکایت hekāyat with the radif می‌کند mi-konad. Often quoted as «بشنو از نی چون حکایت می‌کند / از جدایی‌ها شکایت می‌کند».

بوی جوی مولیان آید همی / یاد یار مهربان آید همی

bu-ye ju-ye Muliyān āyad hami / yād-e yār-e mehrabān āyad hami

The scent of the Muliyan stream comes to me, / the memory of a dear friend comes to me.

The opening of a qasida by Rudaki (died 941), the first great poet of Persian. The Samanid emir had stayed too long in Herat; when Rudaki sang this, the story goes, he jumped on his horse without his boots and rode home to Bukhara. Radif: آید همی āyad hami; rhyme: مولیان Muliyān / مهربان mehrabān.

Long and short: hearing the metre

Classical verse has a strict rhythm, its وزن vazn metre: a fixed pattern of long and short syllables, هجا hejā. You don't need the technical names to hear it – just two rules:

  • Short (∪): a consonant + a short vowel a, e, o that ends the syllable: ba, ke, ze, ye.
  • Long (–): a long vowel ā, i, u or ow, ey – ni, ā, gow – or any vowel closed by a consonant: dar, yek, nesh.

Two more facts make it work. Words run together across the space, so ādam a'zā is read ā-da-ma'-zā. And the last syllable of a mesrā' always counts as long.

Saadi's lines are in the metre called متقارب motaghāreb, the metre of Ferdowsi's Shahnameh: three groups of ∪ – – and a short ∪ –, da-DUM-DUM da-DUM-DUM da-DUM-DUM da-DUM. Hafez's first ghazal is in هزج hazaj, four groups of ∪ – – –. Rumi's Masnavi is in رمل ramal: – ∪ – –, – ∪ – –, – ∪ –. Tap the long syllables as you read aloud and you will hear the difference.

The poet's short forms: ز، چو، همی، -ا، دگر، کز

Poets needed words that were a syllable shorter or longer, so poetry kept old and short forms that modern prose has dropped. Learn them once and you can read a thousand years of verse – and many proverbs, which are often lines of poetry.

Most are simply short forms: ز ze is از az, دگر degar is دیگر digar, گر gar is اگر agar. Some are two words run together: کز kaz = که از ke az, کاین kin = که این ke in, زان zān = از آن az ān.

The -ā at the end of a word has two jobs. On a person it calls them: ساقیا sāghiyā O cupbearer!, حافظا Hāfezā O Hafez!, خدایا khodāyā O God! – the last one is still everyday Persian. On a few words it means how much!: بسا basā many a… (from بس bas much), which lives on in چه بسا che basā quite possibly.

همی hami is the old form of می‌ mi-, and in early poems it can even stand after the verb: آید همی āyad hami = می‌آید mi-āyad keeps coming. Very often there is no prefix at all: the bare present آورد āvarad = می‌آورد mi-āvarad, used for what is always true.

بوی جوی مولیان آید همی

bu-ye ju-ye Muliyān āyad hami

The scent of the Muliyan stream keeps coming.

Rudaki, 10th century: همی hami after the verb.

بوی جوی مولیان می‌آید.

bu-ye ju-ye Muliyān mi-āyad.

The scent of the Muliyan stream is coming.

The same in modern prose.

حضوری گر همی‌خواهی از او غایب مشو حافظ

hozur-i gar hami-khāhi az u ghāyeb ma-show Hāfez

If you want to be in His presence, do not be absent from Him, Hafez.

گر gar = اگر agar; همی‌خواهی hami-khāhi = می‌خواهی mi-khāhi; مشو ma-show = نشو na-show.

حافظ، اگر حضور می‌خواهی، از او غایب نشو.

Hāfez, agar hozur mi-khāhi, az u ghāyeb na-show.

Hafez, if you want to be with Him, don't be absent from Him.

Modern prose: the name comes first and the verb goes last.

ساقیا برخیز و درده جام را / خاک بر سر کن غم ایام را

sāghiyā bar-khiz o dar-deh jām rā / khāk bar sar kon gham-e ayyām rā

Cupbearer, rise and pour the cup, / and throw dust on the sorrows of the days.

Hafez: ساقیا sāghiyā = ای ساقی ey sāghi; درده dar-deh = 'pour out, give'.

ای ساقی، بلند شو و جام را بده؛ غم روزگار را فراموش کن.

ey sāghi, boland show o jām rā bedeh; gham-e ruzgār rā farāmush kon.

O cupbearer, get up and give me the cup; forget the sorrows of the world.

ز دانش دل پیر برنا بود

ze dānesh del-e pir bornā bovad

Through knowledge an old heart grows young.

Ferdowsi: ز ze = از az; بود bovad = 'is, always is'; برنا bornā = 'young'.

با دانش، دل آدم پیر هم جوان می‌ماند.

bā dānesh, del-e ādam-e pir ham javān mi-mānad.

With knowledge, even an old person's heart stays young.

A fortune from Hafez

It is Yalda night at Leila's family home. Tom saw a Hafez fortune taken last year but did not understand a word, so Leila explains – and then takes one for him.

Tom

لیلا، پارسال در خانه‌ی پدربزرگ مینا فال حافظ گرفتیم، ولی من چیزی از شعرش نفهمیدم.

Leylā, pārsāl dar khāne-ye pedar-bozorg-e Minā fāl-e Hāfez gereftim, vali man chiz-i az she'r-ash na-fahmidam.

Leila, last year we took a Hafez fortune at Mina's grandfather's house, but I didn't understand anything of the poem.

Spoken لیلا، پارسال خونه‌ی پدربزرگ مینا فال حافظ گرفتیم، ولی من هیچی از شعرش نفهمیدم. Leylā, pārsāl khune-ye pedar-bozorg-e Minā fāl-e Hāfez gereftim, vali man hichi az she'r-esh na-fahmidam.

Leila

طبیعی است! غزل‌های حافظ برای خود ایرانی‌ها هم آسان نیست.

tabi'i ast! ghazal-hā-ye Hāfez barā-ye khod-e irāni-hā ham āsān nist.

That's natural! Hafez's ghazals aren't easy even for Iranians.

Spoken طبیعیه! غزلای حافظ واسه خود ایرانیا هم آسون نیست. tabi'i-ye! ghazal-ā-ye Hāfez vāse khod-e irāni-yā ham āsun nist.

Tom

پس چرا همه آن‌قدر جدی گوش می‌دادند؟

pas cherā hame ān-ghadr jeddi gush mi-dādand?

Then why was everyone listening so seriously?

Spoken پس چرا همه اون‌قدر جدی گوش می‌دادن؟ pas cherā hame un-ghadr jeddi gush mi-dādan?

Leila

چون حافظ را «لسان‌الغیب» می‌دانند؛ یعنی کسی که از دنیای پنهان خبر دارد.

chon Hāfez rā «lesān-ol-gheyb» mi-dānand; ya'ni kas-i ke az donyā-ye penhān khabar dārad.

Because they consider Hafez 'the Tongue of the Unseen' – someone who knows about the hidden world.

Spoken چون حافظو «لسان‌الغیب» می‌دونن؛ یعنی کسی که از دنیای پنهان خبر داره. chon Hāfez-o «lesān-ol-gheyb» mi-dunan; ya'ni kas-i ke az donyā-ye penhān khabar dāre.

Tom

خب، فال حافظ را چطور می‌گیرند؟

khob, fāl-e Hāfez rā chetor mi-girand?

So how do you take a Hafez fortune?

Spoken خب، فال حافظو چطوری می‌گیرن؟ khob, fāl-e Hāfez-o chetori mi-giran?

Leila

اول نیت می‌کنی؛ یعنی در دلت آرزویی می‌کنی یا سؤالی می‌پرسی، ولی به کسی نمی‌گویی.

avval niyyat mi-koni; ya'ni dar del-at ārezu-yi mi-koni yā so'āl-i mi-porsi, vali be kas-i nemi-guyi.

First you make a niyyat: you make a wish or ask a question in your heart, but you don't tell anyone.

Spoken اول نیت می‌کنی؛ یعنی تو دلت یه آرزویی می‌کنی یا یه سؤالی می‌پرسی، ولی به کسی نمی‌گی. avval niyyat mi-koni; ya'ni tu del-et ye ārezu-yi mi-koni yā ye so'āl-i mi-porsi, vali be kas-i nemi-gi.

Tom

بعد چه؟ کتاب را همین‌طوری باز می‌کنی؟

ba'd che? ketāb rā hamin-towri bāz mi-koni?

Then what? You just open the book anywhere?

Spoken بعد چی؟ کتابو همین‌جوری باز می‌کنی؟ ba'd chi? ketāb-o hamin-juri bāz mi-koni?

Leila

نه، قبلش می‌گویی: «ای حافظ شیرازی، تو محرم هر رازی؛ تو را به شاخ نباتت قسم!»

na, ghabl-ash mi-guyi: «ey Hāfez-e Shirāzi, to mahram-e har rāz-i; to rā be shākh-e nabāt-at ghasam!»

No – before that you say: 'O Hafez of Shiraz, you who keep every secret – I beg you by your Shakh-e Nabat!'

Spoken نه، قبلش می‌گی: «ای حافظ شیرازی، تو محرم هر رازی؛ تو رو به شاخ نباتت قسم!» na, ghabl-esh mi-gi: «ey Hāfez-e Shirāzi, to mahram-e har rāz-i; to ro be shākh-e nabāt-et ghasam!»

Tom

شاخ نبات دیگر کیست؟

shākh-e nabāt digar kist?

And who on earth is Shakh-e Nabat?

Spoken شاخ نبات دیگه کیه؟ shākh-e nabāt dige kiye?

Leila

می‌گویند معشوق حافظ بوده است. حالا ساکت باش، می‌خواهم فالت را بخوانم!

They say she was Hafez's beloved. Now be quiet – I want to read your fortune!

Spoken می‌گن معشوق حافظ بوده. حالا ساکت باش، می‌خوام فالتو بخونم! mi-gan ma'shugh-e Hāfez bude. hālā sāket bāsh, mi-khām fāl-et-o bekhunam!

Leila

یوسف گم‌گشته باز آید به کنعان غم مخور / کلبه‌ی احزان شود روزی گلستان غم مخور

Yusef-e gom-gashte bāz āyad be Kan'ān gham ma-khor / kolbe-ye ahzān shavad ruz-i golestān gham ma-khor

'Lost Joseph will come back to Canaan – do not grieve; / the hut of sorrows will one day be a rose garden – do not grieve.'

Tom

یعنی حافظ به من می‌گوید غصه نخور؟

ya'ni Hāfez be man mi-guyad ghosse na-khor?

So Hafez is telling me not to worry?

Spoken یعنی حافظ بهم می‌گه غصه نخور؟ ya'ni Hāfez behem mi-ge ghosse na-khor?

Leila

دقیقاً! ببین، «غم مخور» در آخر هر بیت تکرار می‌شود؛ این ردیف است. «کنعان» و «گلستان» هم قافیه‌اند.

daghighan! bebin, «gham ma-khor» dar ākhar-e har beyt tekrār mi-shavad; in radif ast. «Kan'ān» va «golestān» ham ghāfiye-and.

Exactly! Look: 'gham ma-khor' comes back at the end of every bayt – that's the radif. And 'Kan'ān' and 'golestān' are the rhyme.

Spoken دقیقاً! ببین، «غم مخور» آخر هر بیت تکرار می‌شه؛ این ردیفه. «کنعان» و «گلستان» هم قافیه‌ان. daghighan! bebin, «gham ma-khor» ākhar-e har beyt tekrār mi-she; in radif-e. «Kan'ān» o «golestān» ham ghāfiye-an.

Tom

So tonight I've learnt some poetry and got a good fortune too. Happy Yalda!

Spoken پس امشب هم شعر یاد گرفتم، هم فالم خوب اومد. یلدا مبارک! pas emshab ham she'r yād gereftam, ham fāl-am khub umad. yaldā mobārak!

Hafez in the garden, Hafez in song

The Hāfeziye. Hafez died in Shiraz in 1390 and was buried in a garden outside the city. His tomb, the حافظیه Hāfeziye, is an open pavilion of eight columns under a dome tiled on the inside, among orange trees – less a monument than a meeting place. Visitors lay a hand on the carved stone and murmur a ghazal, students come at sunset, couples come on dates, and fortune-sellers at the gate offer a folded verse, sometimes picked out of a box by a canary. On ۲۰ مهر bistom-e Mehr (12 October), the Day of Hafez, the garden is full of readers; Saadi has his own day on ۱ اردیبهشت avval-e Ordibehesht (21 April), at his own tomb a few kilometres away.

Poetry you can hear. The word ردیف radif has a second life: it is also the name of the traditional repertoire of Persian classical music, the melodies that masters pass on to their students by ear. Singers of آواز āvāz choose a ghazal by Hafez, Saadi or Rumi and stretch it over the melody, long syllables long and short ones short, exactly where the metre puts them. Many Iranians first learn the great ghazals from songs – one reason they feel the metre without ever studying it.

By heart. Children learn poems از بر az bar by heart at school, and many adults can recite dozens of ghazals. Quote a well-chosen bayt at the right moment and an Iranian friend will often answer with another.

Tips

  • Read the right-hand mesrā' first, then the left. In books the two halves stand side by side; online they are often on two lines or separated by a slash.
  • Find the verb first – it may be anywhere in the line – then the subject, then put the line into modern prose order in your head: چو عضوی به درد آورد روزگار → وقتی روزگار عضوی را به درد می‌آورد.
  • When you meet ز، چو، کز، دگر، گر، همی, say the modern form silently: از، چون، که از، دیگر، اگر، می. After a few poems you will stop noticing them.
  • Read aloud. If the rhythm limps, you have probably misread a vowel: the metre tells you where an ezafe goes and where it does not.
  • A long vowel + a consonant (tād, ruz) or a short vowel + two consonants (dard, 'ozv) counts as long + short – a beat and a half. That is how lines that look too short still fill the pattern.
  • Learn one bayt a week by heart. Quoting Saadi or Hafez at the right moment is the warmest compliment you can pay the language – and Iranians will notice.
