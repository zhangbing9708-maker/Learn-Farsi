خرید و پولShopping and money: prices and bargaining

kharid o pul

Iran is a wonderful place to shop – from the great covered bazaars to the fruit stalls on every corner – but the money takes some getting used to. In this lesson you ask prices with chand and cheghadr, learn the difference between rials and tomans (and why nobody says all the zeros), say that something is expensive or cheap, compare with -tar, and ask for a discount like a local.

In this lesson you will learn to

  • ask the price with chand and cheghadr
  • understand prices in tomans and rials
  • say gerān 'expensive' and arzān 'cheap', and compare with -tar
  • bargain and ask for a discount (takhfif)
  • use mi-shavad: 'how much does it come to?', 'is it possible?'

Bargaining at Tajrish Bazaar

Sara takes Tom to Tajrish Bazaar in the north of Tehran. Tom wants to buy a termeh – a woven cloth from Yazd – for his mother in Australia.

Tom

ببخشید، آقا، این ترمه چند است؟

bebakhshid, āghā, in terme chand ast?

Excuse me, sir, how much is this termeh?

Spoken ببخشید، آقا، این ترمه چنده؟ bebakhshid, āghā, in terme chande?

Seller

این ترمه‌ی یزد است، کار دست. دو میلیون و پانصد هزار تومان.

in terme-ye Yazd ast, kār-e dast. do milyun o pānsad hezār tumān.

This is a termeh from Yazd, handmade. Two million five hundred thousand tomans.

Tom

دو میلیون و پانصد؟ خیلی گران است!

do milyun o pānsad? kheyli gerān ast!

Two and a half million? That's very expensive!

Spoken دو میلیون و پونصد؟ خیلی گرونه! do milyun o punsad? kheyli gerune!

Seller

گران نیست، آقا. جنسش خیلی خوب است.

gerān nist, āghā. jens-ash kheyli khub ast.

It's not expensive, sir. The quality is very good.

Sara

آن یکی کوچک‌تر است. آن چقدر است؟

ān yeki kuchak-tar ast. ān cheghadr ast?

That one is smaller. How much is that one?

Spoken اون یکی کوچیک‌تره. اون چقدره؟ un yeki kuchik-tare. un cheghadre?

Seller

آن یکی ارزان‌تر است، یک میلیون و هشتصد هزار تومان.

ān yeki arzān-tar ast, yek milyun o hashtsad hezār tumān.

That one is cheaper: one million eight hundred thousand tomans.

Tom

تخفیف می‌دهید؟ یک میلیون و پانصد خوب است؟

takhfif mi-dahid? yek milyun o pānsad khub ast?

Will you give me a discount? Is one and a half million OK?

Spoken تخفیف می‌دین؟ یه میلیون و پونصد خوبه؟ takhfif mi-din? ye milyun o punsad khube?

Seller

نه، آقا، نمی‌شود. آخرش یک میلیون و هفتصد.

na, āghā, nemi-shavad. ākhar-ash yek milyun o haftsad.

No, sir, I can't. My last price is one million seven hundred.

Spoken نه، آقا، نمی‌شه. آخرش یه میلیون و هفتصد. na, āghā, nemi-she. ākhar-esh ye milyun o haftsad.

Sara

یک میلیون و ششصد، لطفاً. او مهمان است!

yek milyun o sheshsad, lotfan. u mehmān ast!

One million six hundred, please. He's a guest in our country!

Seller

باشد، به خاطر مهمان. یک میلیون و ششصد.

bāshad, be khāter-e mehmān. yek milyun o sheshsad.

All right, for the guest's sake. One million six hundred.

Spoken باشه، به خاطر مهمون. یه میلیون و ششصد. bāshe, be khāter-e mehmun. ye milyun o sheshsad.

Tom

با کارت می‌شود؟

bā kārt mi-shavad?

Can I pay by card?

Spoken با کارت می‌شه؟ bā kārt mi-she?

Seller

بله، حتماً. بفرمایید، این هم کیسه.

bale, hatman. befarmāyid, in ham kise.

Yes, of course. Here you are, and here's a bag.

Tom

خیلی ممنون. خدا حافظ!

kheyli mamnun. khodā hāfez!

Thank you very much. Goodbye!

Seller

خدا حافظ. مبارک باشد!

khodā hāfez. mobārak bāshad!

Goodbye. Enjoy it!

Spoken خدافظ. مبارک باشه! khodāfez. mobārak bāshe!

Big numbers, short words

Prices in Iran are big numbers, so two words are essential: هزار hezār thousand and میلیون milyun million. Join the parts with و o and, as you learned in lesson 4: دو میلیون و پانصد هزار do milyun o pānsad hezār 2,500,000.

In conversation Iranians shorten prices. Once everyone knows the size of the number, they drop it: the seller's do milyun o pānsad hezār becomes simply do milyun o pānsad, or even دو و پانصد do o pānsad two and five hundred, and Tom's offer could just as well be yek o nim, 'one and a half'. When you are not sure, ask: هزار تومان؟ hezār tumān? thousand tomans? or میلیون؟ milyun? million?

The seller's last words, مبارک باشد! mobārak bāshad! may it be blessed!, are what you say to anyone who has just bought something – a coat, a car, a house. The answer is ممنون mamnun thanks.

How much? chand and cheghadr

There are two ways to ask the price, and you will hear both all the time. چند chand how many, how much is the shopkeeper's word: این چند است؟ in chand ast? How much is this? (spoken in chande?). چقدر cheghadr how much means the same: این چقدر است؟ in cheghadr ast? (spoken in cheghadre?). Use either.

For things sold by weight, ask کیلویی چند است؟ kiluyi chand ast? how much is it a kilo? – the ending -i turns kilu into per kilo. In the same way تایی tāyi each, apiece asks the price of one: تایی چند است؟ tāyi chand ast? how much is it each?

When you have chosen everything, ask for the total with روی هم چقدر می‌شود؟ ru-ye ham cheghadr mi-shavad? how much does it all come to? – mi-shavad means it becomes. After paying, the question goes into the past: چقدر شد؟ cheghadr shod? how much was it?, as you saw in lesson 10.

Rials and tomans

Iran has one currency with two names. The official unit is the ریال riyāl rial: banknotes, receipts, bank cards and supermarket price stickers show rials. But almost nobody talks in rials. In shops, in taxis and at home people count in تومان tumān toman, and one toman = ten rials. To turn rials into tomans, simply knock off the last zero.

So a price tag that says ۵۰۰٬۰۰۰ ریال pānsad hezār riyāl 500,000 rials is pānsad hezār riyāl on paper, but the shopkeeper will tell you پنجاه هزار تومان panjāh hezār tumān fifty thousand tomans – and in everyday speech often just پنجاه تومن panjāh toman, leaving out the hezār. With bigger sums the milyun can disappear too: buying a coat, do toman may well mean two million tomans.

This sounds alarming, but in practice the size of the thing tells you the size of the number. If in doubt, ask: تومان یا ریال؟ tumān yā riyāl? tomans or rials?

Cheaper, and 'is it possible?': -tar and mi-shavad

To compare, add -tar to the adjective. It works like English -er, but for every adjective, long or short: ارزان arzān cheap → ارزان‌تر arzān-tar cheaper, گران gerān expensive → گران‌تر gerān-tar more expensive. One common adjective is irregular: خوب khub good → بهتر behtar better. Than is از az from, and it comes before the adjective: این از آن ارزان‌تر است. in az ān arzān-tar ast. This is cheaper than that. You will practise comparisons fully in A2; for shopping, -tar is enough.

می‌شود mi-shavad (spoken mi-she), from shodan, is the shopper's best friend. It means it becomes, it comes to – چقدر می‌شود؟ cheghadr mi-shavad? how much does it come to? – and also it's possible, it's all right: با کارت می‌شود؟ bā kārt mi-shavad? is it all right by card? No is نمی‌شود nemi-shavad it's not possible. And the bargainer's question is ارزان‌تر نمی‌شود؟ arzān-tar nemi-shavad? can't it get any cheaper?

On Thursdays Leila goes to the fruit and vegetable market.

Bazaars, bargaining and a lot of zeros

The heart of every old Iranian city is its بازار bāzār – a covered maze of brick vaults, domes and courtyards where each trade has its own lane: carpets here, copper there, then spices, gold, fabrics and tea. Tehran's Grand Bazaar, Isfahan's bazaar beside Naqsh-e Jahan Square, the bazaar of Tabriz (a UNESCO World Heritage site) and Shiraz's Vakil Bazaar are worth a visit even if you buy nothing. In the north of Tehran, بازار تجریش bāzār-e Tajrish is smaller and friendlier, full of fruit, sweets, flowers and household things.

In the bazaar, a price is the start of a conversation. Bargaining is normal for carpets, handicrafts and souvenirs, and it is done with smiles, tea and plenty of تعارف ta'ārof: the seller may wave your money away with قابل ندارد ghābel na-dārad it's not worthy of you, but you are still expected to pay. In supermarkets, chain stores and pharmacies, prices are fixed.

The zeros come from years of inflation. In 2025 Iran passed a law to remove four of them: a new toman, worth 10,000 old rials, is to become the official unit, with old and new money used side by side for a few years. Until the change is complete you may meet three ways of writing the same price – so the question تومان یا ریال؟ tumān yā riyāl? is never a silly one.

Tips

  • Before n, Tehranis often turn ā into u: gerān → gerun, arzān → arzun, pānsad → punsad, nān → nun. You will hear kheyli gerune! far more often than kheyli gerān ast!
  • Bargain only where it is normal – carpets, souvenirs, the bazaar – and start at about two-thirds of the asking price. A smile and ākhar-ash chand? 'what's your last price?' work better than walking away.
  • chand before a noun means how many: chand kilu? 'how many kilos?'. On its own at the end it means how much: in chand ast?
  • gh in gheymat and naghd is the deep throat sound of ghazā; kh in kharid and takhfif is the loch sound. Keep them apart: kharid 'shopping', not gharid.
  • When someone has bought something, say mobārak bāshad! (spoken mobārak bāshe!). The buyer answers mamnun or mersi.
