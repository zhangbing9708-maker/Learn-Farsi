دستور زبانGrammar

dastur-e zabān

How Persian sentences are built, and an index of every grammar point in the course. Persian grammar is famously regular: no gender, no articles to agree, verbs with one set of endings. The colours show each part's role.

  • Sentence patterns
  • Grammar in the course

Sentence patterns

Yes. Persian grammar books sort simple sentences by how many parts the verb needs: two, three or four. That gives seven basic patterns (one with two parts, three with three, three with four), close to the five to seven that English grammars list. The parts are the same as in English. The big difference is the order: Farsi puts the verb last.

  • subject نهاد nahād – who or what the sentence is about. Often left out, because the verb's ending already says who.
  • object مفعول maf'ul – what the action is done to. It takes را when it is something specific.
  • complement مسند mosnad – what the subject or object is or becomes: cold, a teacher, happy.
  • preposition phrase متمم motammam – a phrase with به، از، با، برای… that the verb needs, like English 'listen to'.
  • adverbial قید gheyd – extra detail: when, where, how. You can leave it out.
  • verb فعل fe'l – usually last (in everyday speech a destination can follow it: رفتم خونه 'I went home'). Its ending shows the person: رفتم I went, رفتیم we went.

علی کتاب را می‌خواند.

ali ketāb rā mi-khānad.

Ali is reading the book

می‌خواند

English goes subject, verb, object. Farsi goes subject, object, verb, and the object takes را because it is one particular book.

English puts when and where at the end; Farsi puts them before the object and the verb. A general object like کتاب 'a book' stays right next to the verb.

The subject can stay in the verb

Every verb ends with a person ending, so the subject is often left out. A pronoun like من 'I' or ما 'we' is added mainly for emphasis or contrast.

Saying 'not'

Put ن‑ in front of the verb (نمی‑ for verbs with می‑). In a compound verb it goes on the verb part: کار نمی‌کنم 'I don't work'. است 'is' becomes نیست 'is not'.

The hidden ‑e that links words

A noun is joined to a word that describes it, or to its owner, by an ‑e sound called ezafe (‑ye after a vowel). After a consonant it is said but usually not written (shown here with a small mark: کتابِ خوب); after a vowel it is written ی: خانه‌ی علی.

کتابِ خوب

ketāb-e khub

a good book

خانه‌ی علی

khāne-ye ali

Ali's house

Compound verbs stay together

Many verbs are a noun or adjective plus a light verb such as کردن 'do', دادن 'give', گرفتن 'take' or زدن 'hit'. The pair works as one verb and stays together at the end.

من هر روز کار می‌کنم.

man har ruz kār mi-konam.

I work every day
