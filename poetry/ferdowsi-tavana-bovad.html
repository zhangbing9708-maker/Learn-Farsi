Poetry / Ferdowsi

توانا بود هر که دانا بودWhoever knows is strong

tavānā bovad har ke dānā bovad

Abolqasem Ferdowsi of Tus (940–about 1020) spent some thirty years putting the legends and history of ancient Iran into Persian verse. He finished the Shahnameh, 'The Book of Kings', in 1010: some 50,000 couplets, from the first king, Kayumars, to the Arab conquest in the seventh century. He used remarkably few Arabic words, and Iranians credit him with saving their language. These are the book's opening lines, a hymn to God as the lord of soul and wisdom, jān o kherad. They describe a God that eyes cannot see and thought cannot reach, and they end in the couplet every Iranian knows: 'Whoever knows is strong.'

Ferdowsi, Shahnameh, 10th century, masnavi (rhyming couplets) فردوسی

In the name of the Lord of soul and wisdom, / beyond whom no thought can rise.

You will never see the Creator with your eyes, / so do not tire your two eyes trying.

نیابد بدو نیز اندیشه راهکه او برتر از نام و از جایگاه

nayābad bedu niz andishe rāh / ke u bartar az nām o az jāygāh

Thought, too, can find no road to him, / for he is above all name and place.

The second half-line has no verb: است 'is' is understood – a common shortcut in the Shahnameh.

سخن هر چه زین گوهران بگذردنیابد بدو راه جان و خرد

sokhan har che zin gowharān bogzarad / nayābad bedu rāh jān o kherad

Whatever words reach beyond these two jewels, / soul and wisdom can find no way to follow.

The 'jewels' are the soul and the wisdom of the first couplet: God is beyond anything they can grasp.

Word by word

خرد گر سخن برگزیند همیهمان را گزیند که بیند همی

kherad gar sokhan bar-gozinad hami / hamān rā gozinad ke binad hami

When wisdom chooses its words, / it chooses only what it sees.

Ferdowsi often puts همی after the verb at the end of the half-line: برگزیند همی 'keeps choosing', بیند همی 'keeps seeing'.

tavānā bovad har ke dānā bovad / ze dānesh del-e pir bornā bovad

Whoever knows is strong; / knowledge keeps an old man's heart young.

The most quoted line of the Shahnameh. Remember that بود here is bovad 'is', not bud 'was'.

Reading the poem

The ideas. The Shahnameh is a book of kings and heroes, but it opens with wisdom. God is the lord of جان jān 'soul' and خرد kherad 'wisdom, reason', yet he is beyond both: the eyes cannot see him, thought cannot reach him, and words cannot praise him as he is. What is left for a human being? To admit that he exists, to keep away from idle talk, to serve and to seek – and to learn. The hymn ends not with faith but with knowledge, توانا بود هر که دانا بود tavānā bovad har ke dānā bovad, and wisdom is the thread that runs through the whole Shahnameh: its very next section is a chapter in praise of خرد kherad.

The rhythm. Ferdowsi wrote all his 50,000 couplets in motaqāreb, a quick, marching metre of four beats to the half-line: da-DUM-DUM da-DUM-DUM da-DUM-DUM da-DUM. Hear it in be nā-me kho-dā-van-de jā-no kho-rad. Each couplet rhymes on its own – خرد kherad / برنگذرد bar-nagzarad, جای jāy / رهنمای rahnamāy – and the beat is so strong that words are squeezed to fit it: بگذرد bogzarad, not begozarad, and هستیش hastish, not hasti-yash.

The grammar. The key word of the last couplet is بود bovad: the old present 'is' (today است ast or باشد bāshad), not بود bud 'was'. Other old forms are easy to recognise: اوی uy for او u, بدو bedu for به او be u, زین zin for از این az in, همی hami after the verb instead of می mi- before it, and verbs without می mi- for timeless truths, like نیابد nayābad 'does not find'. Couplet 6 in modern prose: اندیشه هم به او راه پیدا نمی‌کند، چون او از نام و از جایگاه برتر است andishe ham be u rāh peydā nemi-konad, chon u az nām va az jāygāh bartar ast.

Using it. به نام خداوند جان و خرد be nām-e khodāvand-e jān o kherad stands at the head of countless books, and توانا بود هر که دانا بود tavānā bovad har ke dānā bovad is painted on the walls of schools and libraries all over Iran; it is often the first line of classical poetry a child learns. Notice the old Persian names of the sky: کیوان keyvān is Saturn, ناهید nāhid Venus and مهر mehr the sun. Nahid is still a common girl's name, and Mehr is the month in which the Iranian school year begins.
