Poetry / Jami

الهی غنچه‌ی امید بگشایOpen the bud of hope

elāhi ghonche-ye omid begoshāy

Abd al-Rahman Jami of Herat (1414–1492) is often called the last great classical poet of Persian. A Sufi of the Naqshbandi order and a friend of the Timurid court, he wrote seven long poems, the Haft Owrang ('Seven Thrones'), and Yusuf and Zuleykha (1483) is the best loved of them. It retells the Quranic story of Joseph: Zuleykha, wife of the Egyptian official who has bought Joseph as a slave, falls in love with him – in Jami's version she has already loved him in her dreams – and tries to seduce him. He refuses, and she spends years in shame and longing until, old and blind, she is made young again and they marry. The poem opens with this prayer, and soon after with a famous chapter in praise of love.

Jami, Yusuf and Zuleykha, 15th century, masnavi (rhyming couplets) جامی

O God, open the bud of hope; / show me a rose from the everlasting garden.

بخندان از لب آن غنچه باغموزان گل عطرپرور کن دماغم

bekhandān az lab-e ān ghonche bāgh-am / vazān gol 'etr-parvar kon damāgh-am

Make my garden laugh with the lips of that bud, / and fill my senses with the scent of that rose.

In poetry a bud 'laughs' when it opens: the خنده of a flower is its blossoming.

Make my heart think of thanks, / and turn my tongue to your praise.

Reading the poem

The images. The prayer is a garden. Hope is a closed bud that only God can open; paradise is a rose garden from which the poet asks for a single flower; the open bud 'laughs' and the rose fills his senses with perfume. Against this garden stands the world, a محنت‌سرا mehnat-sarā, a house of trouble where no one shares your grief. The prayer asks for two simple things: to notice God's gifts and to thank him for them. Then the prologue turns to love, which for Jami is what makes a heart a heart: without it a body is only water and clay; the spinning sky and the turmoil of the world are its work; to be its prisoner is to be free. This is the key to the whole poem, in which Zuleykha's earthly passion for Joseph becomes, through suffering, a love of God.

The rhythm. Jami uses hazaj, the metre of Nezami's Khosrow and Shirin and of Baba Taher's dobeytis: da-DUM-DUM-DUM da-DUM-DUM-DUM da-DUM-DUM. Hear it in fa-lak sar-gash-te az sow-dā-ye 'esh-ghast. Each couplet rhymes on its own – بگشای begoshāy / بنمای benamāy, باغم bāgh-am / دماغم damāgh-am – and several end in a repeated word, as گردان gardān, نیست nist and مبادا mabādā do.

The grammar. The prayer is a string of commands addressed to God: بگشای begoshāy 'open', بنمای benamāy 'show', بخندان bekhandān 'make laugh', کن kon 'make', گردان gardān 'turn into'. Watch the little ـم -am 'me': in به نعمت‌های خویشم کن شناسا be ne'mat-hā-ye khish-am kon shenāsā it is attached to خویش khish but is the object of the verb – 'make me aware of your blessings'. خویش khish 'one's own' means 'your own' here, because it refers back to God. Couplet 4 in modern prose: دلم را شکرگزار کن و زبانم را به ستایش خودت بگردان del-am rā shokr-gozār kon va zabān-am rā be setāyesh-e khod-at begardān.

Using it. The opening couplet is often quoted on its own as a short prayer, and دلی فارغ ز درد عشق دل نیست deli fāregh ze dard-e 'eshgh del nist comes up whenever someone defends love against good sense. Today مبادا mabādā usually means 'lest, in case' – مبادا دیر کنی! mabādā dir koni! 'don't you dare be late!' – but in Jami it is still the old wish 'may it not be'.

«توانا بود هر که دانا بود»

“Whoever has knowledge has power.” – Ferdowsi
