Poetry / Sanai

ملکا ذکر تو گویمMy King, I speak your praise

malekā zekr-e to guyam

Hakim Sanai of Ghazni (died about 1131), in today's Afghanistan, began as a court poet writing praise of the Ghaznavid sultans and ended as one of the first great Sufi poets. His Hadiqat al-Haqiqa ('The Garden of Truth') was the first long mystical poem in Persian, and Rumi wrote that he came 'following Sanai and Attar'. This short ghazal is a monājāt, a private prayer: it praises God as pure, beyond need, beyond description and beyond change, and ends with the poet's hope that a tongue that has spoken only of God's oneness will be spared the fire. Its simple words and dancing rhythm have made it a favourite of Iranian schoolbooks and singers.

Sanai, Divan, 12th century, ghazal سنایی

ملکا ذکر تو گویم که تو پاکی و خدایینروم جز به همان ره که توام راه نمایی

malekā zekr-e to guyam ke to pāki vo khodāyi / naravam joz be hamān rah ke to-am rāh namāyi

My King, I speak your praise, for you are pure, you are God; / I will walk no road but the one you show me.

همه درگاه تو جویم همه از فضل تو پویمهمه توحید تو گویم که به توحید سزایی

I seek only your door, I run only by your grace, / I speak only of your oneness, for oneness is your due.

You are wise, you are great, you are generous, you are merciful; / you are the one who shows grace, you are worthy of praise.

بری از رنج و گدازی بری از درد و نیازیبری از بیم و امیدی بری از چون و چرایی

bari az ranj o godāzi bari az dard o niyāzi / bari az bim o omidi bari az chun o cherāyi

You are free of suffering and wasting, free of pain and need, / free of fear and hope, free of how and why.

«بی‌چون و چرا» 'without how or why' is still everyday Persian for 'without question'.

This creation was not, and you were; creation will not be, and you will be; / you do not move, you do not change, you neither shrink nor grow.

Reading the poem

The ideas. The poem is a list – and that is its power. Sanai piles up the qualities of God in short, balanced phrases: wise, great, generous, merciful; free of pain and need, of fear and hope, of eating and sleeping, of form and colour. It is a lesson in what theologians call the negative way: God is best described by what he is not, because nothing in our world is like him. He cannot be described or imagined, does not move or change; creation came after him and will pass before him. Only at the end does the poet appear, hoping that a mouth that has spoken of nothing but God's oneness will be saved.

The rhythm. The metre (ramal) breaks every half-line into four equal steps, da-da-DUM-DUM four times, and Sanai fills each step with a matching phrase: ma-la-kā zek-re to gu-yam ke to pā-ki yo kho-dā-yi. Most couplets also rhyme inside: جویم juyam / پویم puyam / گویم guyam, حکیمی hakimi / عظیمی 'azimi / کریمی karimi / رحیمی rahimi. The end rhyme is the long ـایی -āyi: خدایی khodāyi, نمایی namāyi, سزایی sezāyi, چرایی cherāyi. It is a rhythm children can clap to, which is one reason they learn it.

The grammar. Almost every phrase ends in ی -i 'you are': تو حکیمی to hakimi 'you are wise', بری از رنجی bari az ranji 'you are free of pain'. This is the short form of هستی hasti that modern Persian still uses after a noun or adjective: تو خسته‌ای to khaste-i 'you are tired'. The old verbs نبد nabod 'was not', نتوان natavān 'one cannot' and فزایی fazāyi 'you increase' are easy to decode. The sixth couplet in modern prose: نمی‌شود تو را وصف کرد، چون در فهم نمی‌گنجی؛ نمی‌شود برایت شبیهی گفت، چون به خیال نمی‌آیی nemi-shavad to rā vasf kard, chon dar fahm nemi-gonji; nemi-shavad barā-yat shabihi goft, chon be khiyāl nemi-āyi.

Using it. ملکا ذکر تو گویم malekā zekr-e to guyam is one of the classical poems Iranian children meet in their school Persian books, and it is often sung and recited at gatherings. Its vocabulary of praise is still alive, too: بی‌چون و چرا bi chun o cherā 'without how or why' means 'without question', and ذکر zekr is the word for the repeated prayers of Sufis.
