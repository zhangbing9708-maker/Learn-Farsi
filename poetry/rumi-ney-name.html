Poetry / Rumi

نی‌نامهThe Song of the Reed

ney-nāme

Jalal al-Din Rumi (1207–1273), born in Balkh and settled in Konya in Anatolia, spent the last fifteen years of his life dictating the Masnavi-ye Ma'navi ('Spiritual Couplets') to his disciple Hosam al-Din Chalabi: six books and some 26,000 couplets of stories, teaching and jokes. Iranians call it simply the Masnavi, and its first eighteen couplets, the Ney-nāme or 'Song of the Reed', are the most famous lines in it. An old story says Rumi already had them written on a slip of paper in his turban when Hosam al-Din asked him for a book. The reed flute, the ney, was cut from its reed bed, and every note it plays is a lament for home; readers have always heard in it the soul, cut off from its source and longing to go back.

Rumi, Masnavi, 13th century, masnavi (rhyming couplets) مولوی

بشنو این نی چون شکایت می‌کنداز جدایی‌ها حکایت می‌کند

beshnow in ney chon shekāyat mi-konad / az jodāyi-hā hekāyat mi-konad

Listen to this reed, how it complains, / how it tells the story of separations.

The best-known form of this line, quoted everywhere and printed in many popular editions, is «بشنو از نی چون حکایت می‌کند / از جدایی‌ها شکایت می‌کند» beshnow az ney chon hekāyat mi-konad / az jodāyi-hā shekāyat mi-konad – 'listen to the reed, how it tells its tale, / how it complains of separations'. Nicholson's edition, following the oldest manuscripts, has «بشنو این نی چون شکایت می‌کند», with 'complaint' and 'tale' the other way round.

Word by word
بشنوbeshnowlisten! – imperative of شنیدن (today usually said beshno)
نیneyreed; the reed flute
چونchonhow (here); also 'when, like, because'
شکایت کردنshekāyat kardanto complain, to lament
جدایی‌هاjodāyi-hāseparations, partings
حکایت کردنhekāyat kardanto tell (a story)

کز نیستان تا مرا ببریده‌انددر نفیرم مرد و زن نالیده‌اند

kaz neyestān tā marā bobride-and / dar nafir-am mard o zan nālide-and

'Ever since they cut me from the reed bed, / men and women have wept at my cry.'

Some editions read «از نفیرم» az nafir-am 'at my cry'. From this couplet on, the reed itself is speaking.

Word by word
کزkazthat from – که + از; here 'since'
نیستانneyestānreed bed (نی + ـستان 'place of')
تاtāever since (here)
ببریده‌اندbobride-andthey have cut (old ب- prefix + بریدن)
نفیرnafircry, wail; the blast of a horn
نالیدنnālidanto moan, to lament

سینه خواهم شرحه شرحه از فراقتا بگویم شرح درد اشتیاق

sine khāham sharhe sharhe az ferāgh / tā beguyam sharh-e dard-e eshtiyāgh

'I want a breast torn to shreds by parting, / so that I can tell the pain of longing in full.'

A play on two words that look alike: شرحه sharhe 'a strip of flesh' and شرح sharh 'a full account'.

Word by word
سینهsinebreast, chest
خواهمkhāhamI want – no می‌ in the classical present
شرحه شرحهsharhe sharhetorn in strips, in shreds
فراقferāghseparation (from the beloved)
شرحsharhexplanation, a full account
اشتیاقeshtiyāghlonging, yearning

هر کسی کو دور ماند از اصل خویشباز جوید روزگار وصل خویش

har kasi ku dur mānd az asl-e khish / bāz juyad ruzgār-e vasl-e khish

'Whoever is left far from their origin / longs to find again the days of union.'

Word by word
کوkuwho – که + او
ماندmāndremained, was left (past of ماندن)
اصلaslroot, origin
خویشkhishone's own – the same as خود
باز جستنbāz jostanto seek again
روزگار وصلruzgār-e vaslthe time of union

من به هر جمعیتی نالان شدمجفت بدحالان و خوش‌حالان شدم

man be har jam'iyyati nālān shodam / joft-e bad-hālān o khosh-hālān shodam

'I have wept in every company; / I have kept company with the unhappy and the happy.'

Word by word
جمعیتjam'iyyatgathering, a company of people (today 'population')
نالانnālānmoaning, lamenting
جفتjoftpair, companion
بدحالbad-hālin a bad way, unhappy
خوش‌حالkhosh-hālhappy

هر کسی از ظن خود شد یار مناز درون من نجست اسرار من

har kasi az zann-e khod shod yār-e man / az darun-e man najost asrār-e man

'Everyone became my friend through their own guesswork; / no one looked for the secrets within me.'

Word by word
ظنzannguess, opinion (Arabic)
یارyārfriend, companion
درونdaruninside, the inner self
نجستnajostdid not seek (past of جستن)
اسرارasrārsecrets (Arabic plural of سر)

سر من از ناله‌ی من دور نیستلیک چشم و گوش را آن نور نیست

serr-e man az nāle-ye man dur nist / lik cheshm o gush rā ān nur nist

'My secret is not far from my lament, / but eye and ear do not have the light to find it.'

Word by word
سرserrsecret – not سر sar 'head'
نالهnālemoan, lament
لیکlikbut – short for لیکن
چشم و گوشcheshm o gusheye and ear
نورnurlight

تن ز جان و جان ز تن مستور نیستلیک کس را دید جان دستور نیست

tan ze jān o jān ze tan mastur nist / lik kas rā did-e jān dastur nist

'Body is not hidden from soul, nor soul from body, / yet no one is allowed to see the soul.'

Word by word
تنtanbody
جانjānsoul, life
زzefrom – the poetic short form of از
مستورmasturhidden, veiled
دیدdidseeing, sight – the past stem used as a noun
دستورdasturpermission (the old meaning; today 'instruction, grammar')

آتش است این بانگ نای و نیست بادهر که این آتش ندارد نیست باد

ātash ast in bāng-e nāy o nist bād / har ke in ātash nadārad nist bād

'This cry of the reed is fire, not wind; / whoever does not have this fire – may he be nothing!'

Both halves end in the same two words, نیست باد, with two meanings: 'is not wind' and 'may he not be'. A rhyme on one word with two meanings is called جناس jenās.

Word by word
بانگbāngcry, call, sound
نایnāyreed flute – another form of نی
بادbādwind, breath; also 'may it be'
نیست بادnist bādmay he be nothing, may he perish

آتش عشق است کاندر نی فتادجوشش عشق است کاندر می فتاد

ātash-e 'eshgh ast kandar ney fetād / jushesh-e 'eshgh ast kandar mey fetād

'It is the fire of love that has fallen into the reed; / it is the ferment of love that has fallen into the wine.'

Word by word
کاندرkandarthat into – که + اندر
اندرandarin, into – the old form of در
فتادfetādfell – short for افتاد
جوششjusheshboiling, fermenting (from جوشیدن)
میmeywine

نی حریف هر که از یاری بریدپرده‌هایش پرده‌های ما درید

ney harif-e har ke az yāri borid / parde-hā-yash parde-hā-ye mā darid

'The reed is the companion of all who are cut off from a friend; / its melodies have torn away our veils.'

پرده is both a curtain or veil and a note or mode in music: the reed's tunes tear away the veils we hide behind.

Word by word
حریفharifcompanion, fellow (today also 'rival, opponent')
یاریyāria friend (یار + ـی)
بریدن ازboridan azto be cut off from, to break with
پردهpardecurtain, veil; a mode or note in music
دریدنdaridanto tear

همچو نی زهری و تریاقی که دیدهمچو نی دمساز و مشتاقی که دید

hamcho ney zahri o taryāghi ke did / hamcho ney damsāz o moshtāghi ke did

'Who ever saw a poison and an antidote like the reed? / Who ever saw a friend and a lover like the reed?'

Word by word
همچوhamcholike, just like
زهرzahrpoison
تریاقtaryāghantidote (from Greek *thēriakē*)
که دیدke didwho has seen? – a question expecting 'no one'
دمسازdamsāzclose friend, confidant – one who breathes in tune
مشتاقmoshtāghlonging, a lover

نی حدیث راه پر خون می‌کندقصه‌های عشق مجنون می‌کند

ney hadis-e rāh-e por khun mi-konad / ghesse-hā-ye 'eshgh-e majnun mi-konad

'The reed tells of the road soaked in blood; / it tells the tales of Majnun's love.'

Majnun, 'the possessed', is the madman of love whose story Nezami told in Leyli and Majnun.

Word by word
حدیثhadistalk, account, story
حدیث کردنhadis kardanto tell, to speak of
پر خونpor khunfull of blood
قصهghessestory, tale
مجنونmajnunmad, possessed – the lover of Leyli

محرم این هوش جز بی‌هوش نیستمر زبان را مشتری جز گوش نیست

mahram-e in hush joz bi-hush nist / mar zabān rā moshtari joz gush nist

'Only the senseless are let into this sense, / just as the tongue has no buyer but the ear.'

Word by word
محرمmahramone allowed inside, a confidant
هوشhushmind, understanding, awareness
بی‌هوشbi-hushunconscious, senseless – here 'out of oneself'
جزjozexcept, but
مر … راmar … rāan old way of marking 'for, to' or the object; مر adds no meaning
مشتریmoshtaribuyer, customer

در غم ما روزها بیگاه شدروزها با سوزها همراه شد

dar gham-e mā ruz-hā bigāh shod / ruz-hā bā suz-hā hamrāh shod

'In our grief the days have grown late; / the days have gone hand in hand with burning.'

The verbs are singular (شد) after the plural روزها: in classical Persian, things that are not people often take a singular verb.

Word by word
غمghamgrief, sorrow
روزهاruz-hādays
بیگاهbigāhlate, untimely (بی + گاه 'time')
سوزsuzburning, heartache
همراهhamrāhtogether with, companion

روزها گر رفت گو رو باک نیستتو بمان ای آن که چون تو پاک نیست

ruz-hā gar raft gu row bāk nist / to bemān ey ān ke chon to pāk nist

'If the days have gone, say: let them go, it does not matter – / only you remain, O you, the purest of all.'

Word by word
گرgarif – short for اگر
گوgusay! – imperative of گفتن (today بگو)
روrowgo! – imperative of رفتن (today برو)
باکbākfear, worry; باک نیست 'no matter'
بمانbemānstay! remain!
پاکpākpure, clean

هر که جز ماهی ز آبش سیر شدهر که بی‌روزیست روزش دیر شد

har ke joz māhi ze āb-ash sir shod / har ke bi-ruzist ruz-ash dir shod

'All but the fish have had their fill of water; / whoever lacks his daily bread finds the day long.'

روز 'day' and روزی 'daily bread' are another play on words. The fish is the true lover: it never has enough of the water it lives in.

Word by word
ماهیmāhifish
ز آبشze āb-ashof its water, of the water
سیر شدنsir shodanto be full, to have had enough
روزیruzidaily bread, one's portion (from روز 'day')
بی‌روزیستbi-ruzistis without his daily bread – بی‌روزی است
دیرdirlate, long

در نیابد حال پخته هیچ خامپس سخن کوتاه باید والسلام

dar nayābad hāl-e pokhte hich khām / pas sokhan kutāh bāyad vas-salām

'No one raw can grasp the state of the ripe, / so the talk must be short – and that is all.'

Word by word
در یافتنdar yāftanto grasp, to understand – here split: در نیابد 'does not grasp'
پختهpokhtecooked, ripe – mature in experience
خامkhāmraw, unripe – inexperienced
هیچhichany; with a negative verb, 'no one'
کوتاهkutāhshort
والسلامvas-salām'and peace' (Arabic) – and that's that, end of story

Reading the poem

The image. The ney, the end-blown reed flute of Persian music, is cut from a reed bed, hollowed out and pierced, and it only sings when someone breathes into it. Rumi makes it the voice of every soul cut off from where it came from: its music is homesickness, and only those who have lost something hear what it is really saying. Notice how the speaker changes – Rumi asks us to listen in the first couplet, and from the second on the reed talks itself. Notice too the paradoxes: the reed is poison and cure; its song is fire, not wind; its 'mind' is open only to those who are out of their minds. In the last couplet Rumi breaks off: the raw cannot understand the cooked, so the talk must be short.

The rhythm. The whole Masnavi is in ramal, the metre of Attar's Conference of the Birds: three beats to the half-line, DUM-da-DUM-DUM DUM-da-DUM-DUM DUM-da-DUM. Read the first half-line slowly: besh-na-vin ney chon she-kā-yat mi-ko-nad. In a masnavi each couplet has its own rhyme – شکایت shekāyat / حکایت hekāyat, فراق ferāgh / اشتیاق eshtiyāgh – which is why the form can run on for 26,000 couplets. Many couplets also repeat a word after the rhyme, as می‌کند mi-konad, شدم shodam and نیست nist do here.

The grammar. The language is easier than it looks. Watch for the run-together forms کز kaz (که + از), کو ku (که + او) and کاندر kandar (که + اندر), for فتاد fetād instead of افتاد oftād and لیک lik instead of لیکن liken, and for imperatives without ب be-: گو gu 'say', رو row 'go'. The old word مر mar in مر زبان را mar zabān rā only strengthens را rā. Couplet 4 in modern prose: هر کسی که از اصل خودش دور بماند، دوباره دنبال روزگار وصل خودش می‌گردد har kasi ke az asl-e khod-ash dur bemānad, dobāre donbāl-e ruzgār-e vasl-e khod-ash mi-gardad.

Using it. Recitals of the Masnavi begin with these lines, and singers and ney players have set them again and again. بشنو از نی beshnow az ney, in the popular reading, is a phrase in its own right. People quote هر کسی از ظن خود شد یار من har kasi az zann-e khod shod yār-e man about friends who only see in us what they want to see, and پس سخن کوتاه باید والسلام pas sokhan kutāh bāyad vas-salām to cut a long argument short.
