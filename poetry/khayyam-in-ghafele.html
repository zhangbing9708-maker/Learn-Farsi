Poetry / Khayyam

این قافله‌ی عمرThis caravan of life

in ghāfele-ye 'omr

Omar Khayyam (1048–1131) of Nishapur was famous in his lifetime as a mathematician and astronomer: he wrote a classic book on algebra and helped to reform the Persian calendar. The short four-line poems (robā'i) that made him world-famous were gathered only long after his death, and nobody knows how many of them are really his. Whoever wrote them, they speak with one voice: life is short, nobody knows what comes after it, so drink, love and do not waste today worrying about tomorrow. Here are six quatrains on time and the moment, beginning with life as a caravan passing in the night.

Khayyam, Rubaiyat, 11th century, quatrains (robā'i) خیام

این قافله‌ی عمر عجب می‌گذرددریاب دمی که با طرب می‌گذرد

How strangely fast this caravan of life goes by! / Catch the moment that passes in joy.

ساقی غم فردای حریفان چه خوریپیش آر پیاله را که شب می‌گذرد

sāghi gham-e fardā-ye harifān che khori / pish ār piyāle rā ke shab mi-gozarad

Cupbearer, why worry about your friends' tomorrow? / Bring the cup, for the night is passing.

از دی که گذشت هیچ از او یاد مکنفردا که نیامده‌ست فریاد مکن

az di ke gozasht hich az u yād makon / fardā ke nayāmade-st faryād makon

Do not think at all of yesterday, which is gone; / do not cry out over tomorrow, which has not come.

Our turn at life, these two or three days, has gone by / like water down a stream, like wind across the plain.

hargez gham-e do ruz marā yād nagasht / ruzi ke nayāmade-st o ruzi ke gozasht

Two days have never troubled me: / the day that has not come, and the day that has gone.

چون عهده نمی‌شود کسی فردا راحالی خوش دار این دل پرسودا را

chon 'ohde nemi-shavad kasi fardā rā / hāli khosh dār in del-e por-sowdā rā

Since no one can vouch for tomorrow, / keep this anxious heart happy now.

Another common reading of the second half is «حالی خوش کن تو این دل شیدا را» 'make this love-crazed heart happy now'.

mey nush be māhtāb ey māh ke māh / besyār betābad o nayābad mā rā

Drink wine by moonlight, my moon, for the moon / will shine again and again and not find us here.

ای دوست بیا تا غم فردا نخوریموین یک دم عمر را غنیمت شمریم

ey dust biyā tā gham-e fardā nakhorim / vin yek dam-e 'omr rā ghanimat shomarim

Come, friend, let us not worry about tomorrow, / and count this one breath of life as a gift.

فردا که از این دیر فنا درگذریمبا هفت‌هزارسالگان سربه‌سریم

fardā ke az in deyr-e fanā dar-gozarim / bā haft-hezār-sālegān sar-be-sar-im

Tomorrow, when we leave this house of passing things, / we shall be equal with those dead these seven thousand years.

Many people say «دیر کهن» deyr-e kohan 'this old house'. The 'seven-thousand-year-olds' are all who have died since the creation, which was thought to have been about seven thousand years ago.

برخیز و مخور غم جهان گذرانبنشین و دمی به شادمانی گذران

barkhiz o makhor gham-e jahān-e gozarān / benshin o dami be shādmāni gozarān

Get up, and do not grieve for this passing world; / sit down, and spend a moment in happiness.

The rhyme word is the same, گذران gozarān, but it means 'passing' in the first half and 'spend!' in the second – a play on one word with two meanings.

در طبع جهان اگر وفایی بودینوبت به تو خود نیامدی از دگران

dar tab'-e jahān agar vafāyi budi / nowbat be to khod nayāmadi az degarān

If there were any faithfulness in the nature of the world, / your turn would never have come after the others'.

بودی and نیامدی end in the old ـی of the unreal condition: 'if there were…, it would not have come'.

Reading the poem

The ideas. Each quatrain is a tiny argument with the same conclusion. Yesterday is gone and tomorrow has not come; the world is faithful to no one – if it were, it would still belong to the people before us; so the only time we own is now, this دم dam, this breath. The images are plain and physical: a caravan moving through the night, water running down a stream, wind over the plain, a moon that will rise again over our empty place. The wine, the cupbearer and the friends are the furniture of a good evening – and a challenge to the preachers who promise their rewards for tomorrow.

The rhythm. A robā'i has four half-lines. Lines 1, 2 and 4 rhyme and the third usually does not, so the last line lands like a punch line. Often the same word follows the rhyme each time – عجب 'ajab / طرب tarab / شب shab + می‌گذرد mi-gozarad – which Persian calls a ردیف radif. The metre has a heavy, swinging beat that poets compare to the Arabic phrase lā howla va lā ghovvata ellā be-llāh; listen to in ghā-fe-le-ye 'om-r 'a-jab mi-go-za-rad.

The grammar. The verbs show the poems' age. Negative commands begin with م ma-, not ن na-: مکن makon 'don't do', مخور makhor 'don't eat'. دی di is 'yesterday' and حالی hāli 'now'. In the last quatrain بودی budi and نیامدی nayāmadi are not 'you were' and 'you did not come' but the old forms of the unreal condition. Its last two lines in modern prose: اگر در طبیعت جهان وفایی بود، نوبت از دیگران به تو نمی‌رسید agar dar tabi'at-e jahān vafāyi bud, nowbat az digarān be to nemi-resid. And غم خوردن gham khordan, 'to eat sorrow', is still the everyday Persian for 'to worry': غم نخور! gham nakhor! 'don't worry!'

Using it. دریاب دمی که با طرب می‌گذرد daryāb dami ke bā tarab mi-gozarad and حالی خوش باش hāli khosh bāsh are Iran's carpe diem. People quote از دی که گذشت هیچ از او یاد مکن az di ke gozasht hich az u yād makon to someone stuck on a past mistake, and غنیمت شمردن ghanimat shomordan 'to count as a windfall' is the everyday way to say 'make the most of it': این فرصت را غنیمت بشمار in forsat rā ghanimat beshomār.
