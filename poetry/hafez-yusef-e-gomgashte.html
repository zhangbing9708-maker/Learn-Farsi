Poetry / Hafez

یوسف گم‌گشتهLost Joseph will come back

yusof-e gom-gashte

This is one of the best-loved ghazals of Hafez (about 1315–1390), and perhaps the most consoling poem in Persian: every couplet ends with the same two words, gham makhor, 'do not grieve'. It begins with the story of Joseph (Yusof), which Iranians know from the Quran and from poetry. His jealous brothers threw him into a well and told their father Jacob that a wolf had eaten him; Jacob wept in his 'house of sorrows' in Canaan until he went blind, and years later Joseph's shirt, sent from Egypt, gave him back his sight. Hafez turns that story into a promise: what is lost comes back, the wheel of time turns, and no road is without an end.

Hafez, Divan, 14th century, ghazal حافظ

yusof-e gom-gashte bāz āyad be kan'ān gham makhor / kolbe-ye ahzān shavad ruzi golestān gham makhor

Lost Joseph will come back to Canaan – do not grieve; / the house of sorrows will one day be a rose garden – do not grieve.

O grieving heart, you will feel better – do not lose heart; / this troubled head will come back to its senses – do not grieve.

هان مشو نومید چون واقف نه‌ای از سر غیبباشد اندر پرده بازی‌های پنهان غم مخور

hān masho nowmid chon vāghef na-'i az serr-e gheyb / bāshad andar parde bāzi-hā-ye penhān gham makhor

Take care, do not despair: you do not know the secret of the unseen; / there may be hidden games behind the curtain – do not grieve.

«پشت پرده» 'behind the curtain' is still the everyday Persian for what goes on out of sight.

ey del ar seyl-e fanā bonyād-e hasti bar-kanad / chon to rā nuh ast kashtibān ze tufān gham makhor

O heart, if the flood of death tears up the foundations of being, / with Noah as your boatman, do not grieve over the Flood.

طوفان here is the Flood of Noah; today it means any storm.

در بیابان گر به شوق کعبه خواهی زد قدمسرزنش‌ها گر کند خار مغیلان غم مخور

dar biyābān gar be showgh-e ka'be khāhi zad ghadam / sarzanesh-hā gar konad khār-e moghilān gham makhor

If you set out across the desert, longing for the Kaaba, / and the acacia thorns reproach you – do not grieve.

Pilgrims who crossed the Arabian desert were torn by acacia thorns; in poetry the thorns are the hardships, and the critics, on the way to any goal.

حال ما در فرقت جانان و ابرام رقیبجمله می‌داند خدای حال‌گردان غم مخور

hāl-e mā dar forghat-e jānān o ebrām-e raghib / jomle mi-dānad khodā-ye hāl-gardān gham makhor

How we fare, far from the beloved and pestered by the rival – / God, who changes every state, knows it all; do not grieve.

The prayer Iranians say at the moment the New Year begins calls God «محول الحول و الاحوال», 'the one who changes the years and the states' – the same God as Hafez's خدای حال‌گردان.

Hafez, in your corner of poverty and in the solitude of dark nights, / as long as prayer and the Quran are your daily practice, do not grieve.

Hafez's pen name means 'one who knows the Quran by heart', which he did.

Reading the poem

The images. Hafez gathers every picture of hope he can find. First the stories everyone knows: Joseph coming home to Canaan, Jacob's hut of sorrows turning into a rose garden, Noah steering his ark through the Flood. Then nature: a songbird that will sit enthroned in the meadow, under a parasol of roses, when spring comes back. Then the journey: a pilgrim crossing the desert to Mecca, torn by thorns, on a road that may be long and dangerous but must have an end. And above all the turning sky, دور گردون dowr-e gardun, which never stays still – what has gone against us will come round again.

The rhythm. The ghazal is in ramal, a long, rolling metre: DUM-da-DUM-DUM DUM-da-DUM-DUM DUM-da-DUM-DUM DUM-da-DUM. Listen to yu-so-fe gom-gash-te bā-zā-yad be kan-'ān gham ma-khor. Every couplet ends in the same rhyme – کنعان kan'ān, گلستان golestān, سامان sāmān, خوشخوان khoshkhān, دوران dowrān – followed by the same refrain, غم مخور gham makhor: a ردیف radif so strong that the whole poem feels like one long, patient reassurance.

The grammar. The language is close to today's. The old negative imperative uses م ma-: مخور makhor 'don't eat', مشو masho 'don't become', مکن makon 'don't do'. گر gar and ار ar are short for اگر agar; نه‌ای na-'i is نیستی nisti 'you are not'; نومید nowmid is ناامید nā-omid. And غم خوردن gham khordan, 'to eat sorrow', is still the everyday word for 'to worry'. Couplet 4 in modern prose: اگر چرخ روزگار دو روزی به میل ما نچرخید، غصه نخور؛ اوضاع دنیا همیشه یکسان نمی‌ماند agar charkh-e ruzgār do ruzi be meyl-e mā nacharkhid, ghosse nakhor; owzā'-e donyā hamishe yeksān nemi-mānad.

Using it. This is the ghazal Iranians send to a friend in trouble, and many people know its first line by heart. یوسف گم‌گشته باز آید به کنعان yusof-e gom-gashte bāz āyad be kan'ān is said when someone long absent comes home, and هیچ راهی نیست کان را نیست پایان hich rāhi nist kān rā nist pāyān to anyone who thinks a hard time will never end.

«توانا بود هر که دانا بود»

“Whoever has knowledge has power.” – Ferdowsi
