Poetry / Hafez

الا یا ایها الساقیCome, cupbearer

alā yā ayyoh as-sāghi

Hafez of Shiraz (about 1315–1390) is the best-loved poet of Iran: his Divan, about 500 ghazals gathered after his death, is in almost every Iranian home. The poems are arranged by the last letter of their rhyme, so a ghazal rhyming in alef comes first – and this is the one that opens the book. It begins and ends in Arabic. An old story, probably a legend, says the first half-line was borrowed from a verse by the Umayyad caliph Yazid, the most hated man in Shi'i history, and a later poet imagined Hafez answering in a dream that an unbeliever's goods are lawful for a believer to take. In seven couplets the poem moves from the tavern to a dark night at sea, and from a love that 'seemed easy at first' to the poet's advice to himself.

الا یا ایها الساقی ادر کأساً و ناولهاکه عشق آسان نمود اول ولی افتاد مشکل‌ها

alā yā ayyoh as-sāghi ader ka'san va nāvelhā / ke 'eshgh āsān nemud avval vali oftād moshkel-hā

Come, cupbearer, pass the cup round and hand it to me, / for love seemed easy at first, but then came the troubles.

The first half-line is Arabic, recited alā yā ayyohas-sāghi ader ka'san va nāvelhā – 'Ho, cupbearer! Pass a cup round and hand it over.' In the Persian half, نمود means 'seemed', not 'showed'.

به بوی نافه‌ای کاخر صبا زان طره بگشایدز تاب جعد مشکینش چه خون افتاد در دل‌ها

be bu-ye nāfe-i kākhar sabā zān torre begshāyad / ze tāb-e ja'd-e moshkin-ash che khun oftād dar del-hā

Waiting for the musk the east wind will at last shake loose from that lock of hair, / how many hearts have bled at the twist of those musky curls!

نافه is the musk pod of the musk deer; the beloved's dark, scented hair is compared to it. صبا sabā, the morning breeze from the east, carries scents and messages between lovers all through Hafez.

به می سجاده رنگین کن گرت پیر مغان گویدکه سالک بی‌خبر نبود ز راه و رسم منزل‌ها

be mey sajjāde rangin kon gar-at pir-e moghān guyad / ke sālek bi-khabar nabvad ze rāh o rasm-e manzel-hā

Stain your prayer rug with wine if the old Magian master tells you to, / for one who travels the path knows its ways and its stations.

In Hafez the pir-e moghān, the old Zoroastrian who keeps the tavern, stands for a spiritual guide who knows more than the official preachers. The couplet is a famous provocation: obey the true master, even if he tells you to stain the prayer rug.

hozuri gar hami-khāhi az u ghāyeb masho hāfez / matā mā talgha man tahvā da' ed-donyā va ahmelhā

If you want to be in his presence, Hafez, do not be absent from him: / when you meet the one you love, leave the world and let it go.

The last half-line is Arabic again, recited matā mā talgha man tahvā da'ed-donyā va ahmelhā – 'whenever you meet the one you desire, leave the world and neglect it'. Hafez names himself in the last couplet of nearly every ghazal; this signature is called the تخلص takhallos.

Reading the poem

The images. Hafez braids three worlds together. There is the tavern: the cupbearer, the wine, the old Magian master, a prayer rug stained red. There is the beloved, whose musky curls make lovers' hearts bleed. And there is the journey: the caravan bell that will not let anyone rest, the night sea with its whirlpool. All three say the same thing – love looked easy at the start, and it is not. Read one way, the beloved is God and the wine is the joy of being near him; read another, this is simply a lover's poem. Hafez never lets you settle on one reading for good, and that is half of his charm.

The rhythm. The ghazal is in hazaj, the commonest metre of Persian: four even steps to the half-line, da-DUM-DUM-DUM four times over. You can hear it in the first Persian words of the poem: ke 'esh-ghā-sān ne-mud av-val. Every couplet ends in the same rhyme – ناولها nāvelhā, مشکل‌ها moshkel-hā, دل‌ها del-hā, محمل‌ها mahmel-hā – so that the Persian plural ها -hā rhymes with the Arabic ها -hā 'it'.

The grammar. Two Arabic half-lines frame the poem, but the Persian between them is close to today's. Look for ز ze for از az, گر gar for اگر agar, کز kaz for که از ke az, همی‌خواهی hami-khāhi for می‌خواهی mi-khāhi, مشو masho for نشو nasho and نبود nabvad for نیست nist. In گرت پیر مغان گوید gar-at pir-e moghān guyad the little ـت -at 'you' hangs on the first word of the clause but belongs to the verb: 'if the old master tells you'. Couplet 6 in modern prose: سرانجام همه‌ی کارهایم از خودکامی به بدنامی کشید؛ رازی که درباره‌اش محفل‌ها می‌سازند چطور پنهان می‌ماند؟ saranjām hame-ye kār-hā-yam az khodkāmi be badnāmi keshid; rāzi ke darbāre-ash mahfel-hā mi-sāzand chetowr penhān mi-mānad?

Using it. Iranians open the Divan at random to take a fāl, an omen, especially on Yalda, the longest night of the year – and whoever opens it at the first page meets this ghazal. عشق آسان نمود اول ولی افتاد مشکل‌ها 'eshgh āsān nemud avval vali oftād moshkel-hā is said of any plan that turned out harder than it looked, and سبکباران ساحل‌ها sabokbārān-e sāhel-hā of people who judge a struggle from a safe distance.
