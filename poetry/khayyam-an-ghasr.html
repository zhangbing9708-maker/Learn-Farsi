Poetry / Khayyam

آن قصر که جمشیدThe palace of Jamshid

ān ghasr ke jamshid

These six quatrains are about ruins, dust and the people who went before us – the classic theme of the poems that go under the name of Omar Khayyam (1048–1131), the mathematician and astronomer of Nishapur. But did he write them? No writer of his own time mentions any Persian verse by him: the first quatrains quoted as his appear some seventy years or more after his death, the oldest anthologies have a few dozen, and later manuscripts several hundred, even over a thousand. Scholars call many of them 'wandering quatrains', because the same poem turns up under several names. Their fame in the West began with Edward FitzGerald's very free English versions of 1859, which made 'Omar' a household name in Britain and America. Whoever wrote them, Iranians read them as Khayyam's.

آن قصر که جمشید در او جام گرفتآهو بچه کرد و روبه آرام گرفت

ān ghasr ke jamshid dar u jām gereft / āhu bacche kard o rubah ārām gereft

In the palace where Jamshid once raised his cup, / the deer has borne its young and the fox has made its home.

بهرام که گور می‌گرفتی همه عمردیدی که چگونه گور بهرام گرفت

bahrām ke gur mi-gerefti hame 'omr / didi ke chegune gur bahrām gereft

Bahram, who spent his whole life catching wild asses – / you saw how the grave caught Bahram.

گور is both 'wild ass, onager' and 'grave'. Bahram Gur, a Sasanian king of the fifth century, was famous as a hunter of onagers; in the end the grave hunted him.

این کوزه‌گر دهر چنین جام لطیفمی‌سازد و باز بر زمین می‌زندش

in kuze-gar-e dahr chonin jām-e latif / mi-sāzad o bāz bar zamin mi-zanad-ash

yet the potter of time makes so fine a cup / and then smashes it on the ground.

The cup is the human body – or the head, with its brow – made by the potter of the world and broken again.

Reading the poem

The images. These quatrains look at the world the way an archaeologist does. The palace where the legendary king Jamshid held his feasts now shelters deer and foxes – and Iranians still call the ruins of Persepolis تخت جمشید takht-e jamshid, 'the throne of Jamshid'. A dove on the battlements of another palace calls ku? ku?, 'where? where?'. The potter's jug was once a lover, and its handle an arm round someone's neck; the grass by the stream grew from a lovely face. Everything around us is made of the dust of the people before us – and the world did not miss us before we were born and will not miss us when we are gone. The conclusion, in the other Khayyam quatrains, is always the same: enjoy today.

The rhythm. Each quatrain rhymes in lines 1, 2 and 4, usually with a repeated word or phrase after the rhyme, a ردیف radif: گرفت gereft, می‌زندش mi-zanad-ash, بوده است bude ast, رسته است roste ast, خواهد بود khāhad bud. The robā'i metre has a few variations but always the same heavy swing, close to the Arabic lā howla va lā ghovvata ellā be-llāh. Try ān ghasr ke jam-shid da-ru jām ge-reft, reading در او dar u as daru.

The grammar. Old habitual verbs end in ی -i: می‌گرفتی mi-gerefti 'he used to catch' and نهادندی nahādandi 'they used to lay', where modern Persian says می‌گرفت mi-gereft and می‌نهادند mi-nahādand. همی hami is the old می mi-, نبد nabod is نبود nabud, زین zin is از این az in, and شهان shahān is شاهان shāhān. The second quatrain in modern prose: آن قصری که به آسمان می‌رسید و شاهان بر درگاهش سجده می‌کردند، دیدیم که فاخته‌ای بر کنگره‌اش نشسته بود و می‌گفت: کو؟ کو؟ ān ghasri ke be āsemān mi-resid va shāhān bar dargāh-ash sajde mi-kardand, didim ke fākhte-i bar kongere-ash neshaste bud va mi-goft: ku? ku?

Using it. Iranians quote دیدی که چگونه گور بهرام گرفت didi ke chegune gur bahrām gereft when the mighty fall, and ای بس که نباشیم و جهان خواهد بود ey bas ke nabāshim o jahān khāhad bud to anyone who thinks they cannot be done without. The talking jugs are among the best-known images of Persian poetry, and Khayyam's garden tomb in Nishapur, with quatrains set into its tilework, is a favourite visit for Iranians and foreigners alike.
