Poetry / Baba Taher

ز دست دیده و دلThe eye and the heart

ze dast-e dide vo del

Baba Taher, called 'Oryān 'the Naked', was a wandering dervish of Hamadan in western Iran. The historian Ravandi tells that the Seljuk sultan Toghrul met him on a hill outside the city around 1055, got down from his horse, kissed his hand and asked for his blessing. His dobeytis – 'two-couplet' quatrains in a lighter, more singable metre than Khayyam's – were composed in the old dialect of the region, and shepherds, villagers and great singers have sung them for nine hundred years. They are printed here as they usually are today, in a Persian close to the standard language with a few dialect words. These six are among the best known: the eye and the heart, the beloved seen everywhere, and the pains and cures of love.

Baba Taher, Dobeytis, 11th century, dobeyti باباطاهر

ز دست دیده و دل هر دو فریادکه هر چه دیده بیند دل کند یاد

ze dast-e dide vo del har do faryād / ke har che dide binad del konad yād

Help! I cry out against my eyes and my heart, / for whatever the eye sees, the heart remembers.

«از دست کسی فریاد» 'help against someone!' is still how Persian complains: «از دست تو!» 'oh, you!'.

Reading the poem

The images. Baba Taher's world is small and concrete: the eye and the heart, the plain, the sea and the mountain, a dagger, a garment, a market. Out of these he makes the great themes of mystical love. The eye sees and the heart cannot forget, so the poet threatens to blind himself; the beloved is so present that he is seen in every landscape, and so close that lover and beloved can no longer be told apart. The heart is a buyer in love's market and wears a garment woven of love and suffering. The last two dobeytis are about surrender: the lover wants whatever the beloved wants, even pain – and if the beautiful like a bleeding heart, then bleed.

The rhythm. A dobeyti looks like a robā'i – four half-lines, rhyming in lines 1, 2 and 4 – but it has its own lighter metre, a short hazaj: da-DUM-DUM-DUM da-DUM-DUM-DUM da-DUM-DUM. Hear it in be sah-rā ben-ga-ram sah-rā ta vi-nam. It is the metre of folk song all over Iran, and Baba Taher's poems are more often sung than recited. Several repeat a word or two after the rhyme, a ردیف radif: ته وینم ta vinam, محبت mohabbat, پسندد pasandad, پسندند pasandand.

The grammar. The dialect words are few and easy: دیرم diram for دارم dāram, وینم vinam for بینم binam, ته ta for تو را to rā, آمیته āmite for آمیخته āmikhte, ندونم nadunam for ندانم nadānam. The ending ـه -e for است ast – دلبره delbar-e 'is the beloved', کدامه kodām-e 'which is it?' – is exactly what you hear in spoken Tehrani Persian today. The first dobeyti in modern prose: از دست چشم و دلم فریاد، چون دل هر چه را چشم می‌بیند به یاد می‌آورد. خنجری با نوک فولادی می‌سازم و به چشمم می‌زنم تا دلم آزاد شود az dast-e cheshm o del-am faryād, chon del har che rā cheshm mi-binad be yād mi-āvarad. khanjari bā nok-e fulādi mi-sāzam va be cheshm-am mi-zanam tā del-am āzād shavad.

Using it. Baba Taher is the poet of ordinary people: his dobeytis are sung at weddings and in the mountains, and Iran's great classical singers have recorded them again and again. His tomb, a modern tower on a hill in Hamadan, is one of the city's landmarks. به صحرا بنگرم صحرا ته وینم be sahrā bengaram sahrā ta vinam is the line people quote about a love they see everywhere, and گروهی آن گروهی این پسندند goruhi ān goruhi in pasandand settles any argument about taste.
